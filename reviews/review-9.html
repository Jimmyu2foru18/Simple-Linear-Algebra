1. Determinant Recurrences & Periodicity

Quiz 2 emphasizes the connection between cofactor expansion, recurrence relations, and complex eigenvalues. The $n \times n$ tri-diagonal matrix of ones $A_n$ produces a determinant recurrence when expanded along the first row.

1.1 The Determinant Recurrence

$$D_n = D_{n-1} - D_{n-2}, \quad D_1 = 1,\; D_2 = 0$$
Cofactor expansion along first row of tri-diagonal ones

The first entry of row 1 gives $D_{n-1}$ (a $+$ cofactor), and the second entry gives $-D_{n-2}$ (a $-$ cofactor). All other entries are zero, so only two terms survive.

1.2 Period-6 Determinant Sequence

4$-1$$-1 - 0$
5$0$$-1 - (-1)$

8$0$$1 - 1$
Periodicity Explained: The recurrence $D_n = D_{n-1} - D_{n-2}$ corresponds to the matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Its characteristic equation $\lambda^2 - \lambda + 1 = 0$ has roots $\lambda = e^{\pm i\pi/3}$ (on the unit circle at $\pm 60°$). Since $|\lambda| = 1$ and $\lambda^6 = 1$, we get $A^6 = I$, explaining the period-6 cycle.

2. Singular vs. Invertible Matrix Families

A family of symmetric matrices $A_n$ with zeros on the diagonal and increasing integers off-diagonal reveals a striking pattern:

2.1 The $A_3$ Example (Singular)

$A_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Column 3 is $2 \times$ column 1, so the columns are linearly dependent and $\det(A_3) = 0$.

$$\det(A_3 - \lambda I) = -\lambda(\lambda^2 - 5) = 0 \implies \lambda = 0,\; \pm\sqrt{5}$$
Eigenvalues of singular $A_3$

2.2 The $A_4$ Example (Invertible)

The $4 \times 4$ matrix in the same family has $\det(A_4) = 9 \neq 0$, so it is invertible. Since $C(A_4) = \mathbb{R}^4$, the projection matrix onto the column space is $P = I_4$.

2.3 Pattern: Odd vs. Even Index

Key Exam Insight: To check if a matrix is singular, look for proportional columns (or rows) before computing the determinant. A zero eigenvalue immediately implies singularity.

3. Projection Matrix onto the Column Space

For any matrix $A$ with independent columns, the projection matrix onto $C(A)$ is:

$$P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$$
Projection onto column space

If $A$ is square and invertible, then $C(A) = \mathbb{R}^n$ and $P = I_n$. If $A$ is singular, $C(A)$ is a proper subspace and $P \neq I$.

1. Symmetric Matrices & the Spectral Theorem

A matrix is symmetric if $A = A^T$. Symmetric matrices are the "well-behaved" matrices of linear algebra.

1.1 Real Eigenvalues, Orthogonal Eigenvectors

Spectral Theorem: Every real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and a complete set of $n$ orthonormal eigenvectors. It can be factored as $A = Q\Lambda Q^T$ where $Q$ is orthogonal ($Q^T Q = I$).

1.2 Proof Sketch: Orthogonality of Eigenvectors

If $A\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$ and $A\mathbf{y} = \alpha \mathbf{y}$ with $\lambda \neq \alpha$:

$$\mathbf{y}^T A \mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{x} \quad \text{and} \quad \mathbf{y}^T A \mathbf{x} = (A^T\mathbf{y})^T \mathbf{x} = \alpha \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{x}$$
Two expressions for $\mathbf{y}^T A \mathbf{x}$

Since $A = A^T$, both equal $\mathbf{y}^T A \mathbf{x}$. Thus $(\lambda - \alpha)\mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{x} = 0$, and since $\lambda \neq \alpha$, we get $\mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{x} = 0$ (orthogonality).

1.3 Decomposition into Rank-1 Projections

$$A = \lambda_1 \mathbf{q}_1 \mathbf{q}_1^T + \lambda_2 \mathbf{q}_2 \mathbf{q}_2^T + \cdots + \lambda_n \mathbf{q}_n \mathbf{q}_n^T$$
Spectral decomposition: weighted sum of projection matrices

Each $\mathbf{q}_i \mathbf{q}_i^T$ is a symmetric rank-1 projection matrix ($P^2 = P$, $P^T = P$). The eigenvalues $\lambda_i$ scale these projections along mutually perpendicular directions.

1.4 Hermitian Matrices (Complex Generalization)

For complex matrices, symmetry is replaced by the Hermitian property $A^H = A$, where $A^H = \bar{A}^T$ is the conjugate transpose. Hermitian matrices also have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors (using $\bar{\mathbf{x}}^T \mathbf{x}$ for length).

2. Positive Definite Matrices

A symmetric matrix $A$ is positive definite if $\mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x} > 0$ for all nonzero $\mathbf{x}$. Five equivalent conditions determine positive definiteness:

2.1 Four Equivalent Tests

TestCondition for Positive Definite
Quadratic form$\mathbf{x}^T A \mathbf{x} > 0$ for all $\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$
EigenvaluesAll $\lambda_i > 0$
PivotsAll pivots $d_i > 0$ in $A = LU$
Leading minorsAll $\det(A_k) > 0$ for $k = 1, \ldots, n$
Factorization$A = B^T B$ for some invertible $B$

2.2 The 2×2 Test

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{bmatrix}$, positive definiteness requires:

$$a > 0 \quad \text{and} \quad ac - b^2 > 0$$
2×2 positive definiteness conditions

Both conditions: the first pivot ($a$) is positive, and the determinant ($ac - b^2$) is positive.

2.3 Example: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$

  • Pivot 1 = 5 > 0 ✓
  • $\det(A) = 10 - 9 = 1 > 0$ ✓
  • Eigenvalues: $\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 1 = 0$, both roots positive ✓
Common Pitfall: A positive determinant does NOT imply positive definiteness. The matrix $\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ has $\det = 1 > 0$ but both eigenvalues are negative. Check all leading principal minors, not just the full determinant.

3. Pivots Match Eigenvalue Signs

For symmetric matrices, the number of positive pivots equals the number of positive eigenvalues, and similarly for negative and zero. This is a consequence of the Inertia Theorem:

$$\#\{\text{positive pivots}\} = \#\{\text{positive eigenvalues}\}$$
Sylvester's law of inertia (symmetric case)

This means you can determine the eigenvalue sign distribution by computing only the pivots from Gaussian elimination, no need to find the eigenvalues explicitly.

1. Roots of Unity & the Fourier Matrix

The $n$-th roots of unity are $1, W, W^2, \ldots, W^{n-1}$ where $W = e^{2\pi i/n}$. They lie on the unit circle at evenly spaced angles.

1.1 The Primitive Root

$$W = e^{2\pi i/n} = \cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{n}\right) + i\sin\!\left(\frac{2\pi}{n}\right), \quad W^n = 1$$
Primitive $n$-th root of unity

1.2 The Fourier Matrix $F_n$

The $n \times n$ Fourier matrix has entries $(F_n)_{jk} = W^{jk}$ for $j, k = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1$. For $n = 4$ with $W = i$:

$$F_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & i & -1 & -i \\ 1 & -1 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & -i & -1 & i \end{bmatrix}$$
4×4 Fourier matrix

2. Orthogonality & the Inverse

The columns of $F_n$ are orthogonal. The inner product of columns $j$ and $k$ is:

2.1 Column Inner Product

$$\langle \mathbf{c}_j, \mathbf{c}_k \rangle = \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} W^{mj}\,\overline{W^{mk}} = \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} W^{m(j-k)} = \begin{cases} n & j = k \\ 0 & j \neq k \end{cases}$$
Orthogonality of Fourier matrix columns

For $j \neq k$, this is a geometric series: $\frac{1 - (W^{j-k})^n}{1 - W^{j-k}} = \frac{1 - W^{n(j-k)}}{1 - W^{j-k}} = \frac{1 - 1}{1 - W^{j-k}} = 0$.

2.2 The Inverse Matrix

$$F_n^{-1} = \frac{1}{n}\,\overline{F_n}^{\,T}$$
Inverse of the Fourier matrix

Each column has length $\sqrt{n}$. Dividing $F_n$ by $\sqrt{n}$ gives an orthonormal matrix (complex unitary). This mirrors $Q^{-1} = Q^T$ for real orthogonal matrices.

3. Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)

The FFT is an algorithm that multiplies by $F_n$ in $O(n \log n)$ operations instead of $O(n^2)$.

3.1 The Recursion

The FFT recursively splits $F_{2n}$ into two copies of $F_n$ plus a permutation and diagonal fix-up:

$$F_{2n} = \text{fix-up} \times \begin{bmatrix} F_n & 0 \\ 0 & F_n \end{bmatrix} \times \text{permute}$$
FFT recursive decomposition

3.2 Operation Count

$$M(n) = \frac{1}{2}\,n\log_2 n$$
FFT operation count (vs. $n^2$ for direct)

The recurrence $M(2n) = 2M(n) + O(n)$ solves to $M(n) = O(n \log n)$. Dividing by $2^k$: $M(2^k)/2^k = M(2^{k-1})/2^{k-1} + c/2$, telescoping to $kc/2$, so $M(2^k) = \frac{c}{2} \cdot k \cdot 2^k$.
