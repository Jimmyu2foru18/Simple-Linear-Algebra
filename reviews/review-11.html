1. Linear Transformations

A linear transformation $T: V \to W$ is completely determined by its action on a basis. The matrix representation depends on the choice of input and output bases.

1.1 Matrix of a Linear Transformation

$$A_{ij} = \text{coefficient of } w_i \text{ in } T(v_j)$$
Column $j$ of $A$ is $T(v_j)$ in output coordinates
  • The $j$-th column of $A$ is $T(v_j)$ expressed in the output basis
  • If input basis = output basis = eigenvector basis, $A$ is diagonal
  • Coordinates depend on basis: the vector is coordinate-free, the matrix is not

1.2 Projection Example

Projection onto line $y=x$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$:

  • Eigenvector basis: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ (diagonal)
  • Standard basis: $P = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Same transformation, different bases, different matrices

1.3 Derivative as a Matrix

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$
Derivative $T: P_3 \to P_2$ with bases $\{1, x, x^2\}$ and $\{1, x\}$

2. Change of Basis & Image Compression

Changing the basis can dramatically simplify computation and enable compression. A good basis is fast to multiply, fast to invert, and concentrates signal energy into few coefficients.

2.1 Change of Basis Formula

$$x = Wc, \quad c = W^{-1}x$$
Change of basis: old coordinates = $W \times$ new coordinates
  • For orthogonal basis: $W^{-1} = W^T$
  • Fourier basis: $F_n^{-1} = \frac{1}{n}\bar{F}_n^T$
  • Wavelet basis: $W^{-1} = W^T$ (after normalization)

2.2 Similar Matrices from Change of Basis

$$B = M^{-1}AM$$
Similarity transformation

Same transformation $T$ in different bases. Preserves eigenvalues, trace, and determinant. The eigenvector basis gives $B = \Lambda$ diagonal.

2.3 Good Basis Criteria

CriterionFourier BasisWavelet Basis
Fast multiplicationFFT: $O(n \log n)$Fast wavelet transform
Fast inversion$F_n^{-1} = \frac{1}{n}\bar{F}_n^T$$W^{-1} = W^T$
Good compressionLow frequencies capture energyBetter for edges (JPEG 2000)

2.4 Image Compression

Key Insight: In the standard basis, throwing away 90% of pixels destroys the image. In the wavelet basis, throwing away high-frequency coefficients preserves structure because most energy is in a few coefficients.

3. Quiz 3 Review

Review of core matrix classifications and the SVD, connecting symmetry, positive definiteness, Markov and projection matrices through eigenvalue tests.

3.1 Matrix Classification by Eigenvalues

Matrix TypeEigenvalue Condition
SymmetricAll eigenvalues real; eigenvectors orthogonal
Positive definiteAll eigenvalues $\lambda_i > 0$
Positive semi-definiteAll eigenvalues $\lambda_i \geq 0$
Markov$\lambda = 1$ is largest eigenvalue; others $|\lambda| < 1$
Projection ($P^2 = P$)Eigenvalues are only $0$ and $1$

3.2 The SVD

$$A = U\Sigma V^T, \quad \sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i(A^T A)}$$
SVD: singular values are square roots of eigenvalues of $A^T A$
  • Every matrix has an SVD, even if rectangular or singular
  • Resolve sign ambiguity via $A v_i = \sigma_i u_i$
  • Rank equals number of nonzero singular values
  • Null space comes from $V$ columns for $\sigma_i = 0$

3.3 Key Theorems

Important: Symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors. If $A$ is not symmetric, eigenvalues may be complex and eigenvectors may not be orthogonal.
