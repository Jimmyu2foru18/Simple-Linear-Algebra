Diagonalization & Applications

Comprehensive review of diagonalization, matrix powers, differential equations via the matrix exponential, Markov chains, and Fourier series. Covers chapters 22–24.

1. Diagonalization & Matrix Powers

A matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if $A = S\Lambda S^{-1}$, where $S$ has eigenvectors as columns and $\Lambda$ is diagonal with eigenvalues. This yields the efficient power formula $A^k = S\Lambda^k S^{-1}$.

1.1 Diagonalization Theorem

Diagonalization Criterion: $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if it has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors. Then $S = [\mathbf{x}_1 \; \mathbf{x}_2 \; \cdots \; \mathbf{x}_n]$ and $\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_n)$, giving $A = S\Lambda S^{-1}$.

1.2 Matrix Powers via Diagonalization

$$A^k = S\Lambda^k S^{-1} = S\begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1^k & & \\ & \ddots & \\ & & \lambda_n^k \end{bmatrix}S^{-1}$$
Efficient computation of $A^k$

Raising the diagonal matrix $\Lambda$ to the $k$-th power is trivial (entrywise). The interior $S^{-1}S = I$ terms cancel in the product.

1.3 Fibonacci via Matrix System

The recurrence $F_{k+2} = F_{k+1} + F_k$ (with $F_0 = 0, F_1 = 1$) converts to first-order form by defining the state vector $\mathbf{u}_k = \begin{bmatrix} F_{k+1} \\ F_k \end{bmatrix}$:

$$\mathbf{u}_{k+1} = A\mathbf{u}_k, \quad A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{u}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
Fibonacci as a first-order matrix system

1.4 Fibonacci Eigenvalues

$$\lambda^2 - \lambda - 1 = 0 \implies \lambda_{1,2} = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2}$$
Golden ratio and its conjugate

The dominant eigenvalue $\lambda_1 = \frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 1.618$ (the golden ratio) controls the growth rate. The second eigenvalue $\lambda_2 \approx -0.618$ has $|\lambda_2| < 1$, so its contribution vanishes for large $k$.

1.5 Long-Term Dynamics Summary

Condition on EigenvaluesLong-Term Behavior of $A^k$

All $|\lambda_i| > 1$$A^k$ grows unbounded

Precondition Check: Before applying $A^k = S\Lambda^k S^{-1}$, verify that $A$ has $n$ independent eigenvectors. A repeated eigenvalue with only one eigenvector (defective matrix) cannot be diagonalized this way.

2. Differential Equations & the Matrix Exponential

The system $\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt} = A\mathbf{u}$ is the continuous-time analogue of the discrete system $\mathbf{u}_{k+1} = A\mathbf{u}_k$. Where powers $\lambda^k$ appear in the discrete case, exponentials $e^{\lambda t}$ appear in the continuous case.

2.1 The Matrix Exponential

$$e^{At} = I + At + \frac{(At)^2}{2!} + \frac{(At)^3}{3!} + \cdots$$
Matrix exponential series

2.2 General Solution

$$\mathbf{u}(t) = c_1 e^{\lambda_1 t}\mathbf{x}_1 + c_2 e^{\lambda_2 t}\mathbf{x}_2 + \cdots + c_n e^{\lambda_n t}\mathbf{x}_n$$
Solution as eigenmode expansion

Equivalently: $\mathbf{u}(t) = S\, e^{\Lambda t}\, S^{-1}\mathbf{u}(0)$, where $e^{\Lambda t} = \text{diag}(e^{\lambda_1 t}, \ldots, e^{\lambda_n t})$.

2.3 Stability Classification

Eigenvalue Real PartsBehavior of $e^{\lambda t}$System Stability
$\text{Re}(\lambda) < 0$$e^{\lambda t} \to 0$Decay to zero (stable)
$\text{Re}(\lambda) = 0$$|e^{\lambda t}| = 1$Oscillation (marginally stable)
$\text{Re}(\lambda) > 0$$e^{\lambda t} \to \infty$Growth (unstable)

2.4 Steady State from $\lambda = 0$

$$\text{If } A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} \text{ (singular) with } \lambda = 0: \quad e^{0t} = 1 \text{ persists as steady state}$$
Zero eigenvalue produces persistent steady state
Worked Example: For $A = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$ (singular, $\lambda_1 = 0, \lambda_2 = -3$) with $\mathbf{u}(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the solution is $\mathbf{u}(t) = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{1}{3}e^{-3t}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. As $t \to \infty$, the exponential term vanishes and the steady state is $\mathbf{u}_\infty = \begin{bmatrix} 2/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix}$.

3. Markov Chains & Fourier Series

Markov matrices model population transitions where total population is conserved. Fourier series extend the idea of orthonormal basis expansion from finite-dimensional vectors to infinite-dimensional function spaces.

3.1 Markov Matrix Properties

  • All entries nonneg: $a_{ij} \geq 0$
  • Columns sum to 1: $\sum_i a_{ij} = 1$ (column-stochastic) or rows sum to 1 (row-stochastic)
  • $\lambda = 1$ is always an eigenvalue (steady-state mode)
  • All other eigenvalues satisfy $|\lambda| \leq 1$

3.2 Markov Chain Solution

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix}$ with $\mathbf{u}_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1000 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$\mathbf{u}_k = c_1 (1)^k \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2 (0.7)^k \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
Decay of transient mode, persistence of steady state

With $c_1 = 1000/3$ and $c_2 = 2000/3$, the $(0.7)^k$ term vanishes and the steady state is $\mathbf{u}_\infty = \begin{bmatrix} 2000/3 \\ 1000/3 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 667 \\ 333 \end{bmatrix}$.

3.3 Fourier Series: Functions as Infinite-Dimensional Vectors

The orthonormal basis expansion $v = \sum (q_i^T v)\, q_i$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ generalizes to function spaces where the dot product becomes an integral:

$$f(x) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[ a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx) \right]$$
Fourier series on $[0, 2\pi]$

3.4 Fourier Coefficient Formulas

CoefficientFormulaNormalization
$a_0$$\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\,dx$Average value (inner product with $1$, length $\sqrt{2\pi}$)
$a_n$$\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx$Inner product with $\cos(nx)$, length $\sqrt{\pi}$
$b_n$$\frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx$Inner product with $\sin(nx)$, length $\sqrt{\pi}$
Orthogonality: $\int_0^{2\pi} \cos(mx)\cos(nx)\,dx = 0$ for $m \neq n$, and similarly for all cross-terms. This is why each coefficient is isolated by taking an inner product with the corresponding basis function.

3.5 Worked Example: $f(x) = x$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$

$f(x) = x$ is odd, so $a_0 = 0$ and all $a_n = 0$. The sine coefficients are $b_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} x\sin(nx)\,dx = \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$. Thus:

$$x = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\sin(nx)$$
Fourier series of $f(x) = x$

At $x = \pi/2$: $\frac{\pi}{2} = 2\left(1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{7} + \cdots\right)$, the Leibniz formula for $\pi$.
