Least Squares & Determinants

Comprehensive review of projection matrices and least squares, orthogonal matrices and Gram-Schmidt, and properties of determinants. Covers chapters 16–18 from Gilbert Strang's MIT 18.06 course.

1. Projection Matrices & Least Squares

When $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has no exact solution (overdetermined system), the least squares method finds the $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ minimizing $\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|^2$. This is equivalent to projecting $\mathbf{b}$ onto $C(A)$.

1.1 The Normal Equations

The minimum error occurs when the gradient of $\|A\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{b}\|^2$ is zero:

$$A^TA\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T\mathbf{b}$$
Normal equations for least squares

1.2 Projection and Error Vectors

The least squares solution decomposes $\mathbf{b}$ into orthogonal components:

$$\mathbf{b} = \underbrace{A\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_{\mathbf{p} \in C(A)} + \underbrace{(\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}})}_{\mathbf{e} \in N(A^T)}$$
Orthogonal decomposition of $\mathbf{b}$

Verification: $\mathbf{p}^T\mathbf{e} = 0$, $A^T\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}$, and $\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b}$.

1.3 Properties of $A^TA$

PropertyStatementCondition
Symmetric$(A^TA)^T = A^TA$Always
Invertible$A^TA$ has inverse$A$ has independent columns
Positive definite$\mathbf{x}^TA^TA\mathbf{x} > 0$$\mathbf{x} \neq \mathbf{0}$

1.4 Orthonormal Columns

Ideal Case: When columns of $A$ are orthonormal, $A^TA = I$ and the normal equations become $I\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T\mathbf{b}$, so $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T\mathbf{b}$. No matrix inversion is needed.

2. Orthogonal Matrices & Gram-Schmidt

The Gram-Schmidt process transforms any basis into an orthonormal basis. Orthogonal matrices, whose columns are orthonormal, represent rotations and reflections that preserve lengths and angles.

2.1 Orthogonal and Orthonormal Vectors

$$\mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{v} = 0 \quad \text{(orthogonal)}$$ $$Q^TQ = I \quad \text{(orthonormal columns)}$$
Orthogonality conditions

2.2 Gram-Schmidt Orthogonalization

Given independent vectors $\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \mathbf{a}_3$, Gram-Schmidt produces orthogonal vectors $\mathbf{q}_1, \mathbf{q}_2, \mathbf{q}_3$:

$$\mathbf{q}_1 = \mathbf{a}_1$$ $$\mathbf{q}_2 = \mathbf{a}_2 - \frac{\mathbf{q}_1^T\mathbf{a}_2}{\mathbf{q}_1^T\mathbf{q}_1}\mathbf{q}_1$$ $$\mathbf{q}_3 = \mathbf{a}_3 - \frac{\mathbf{q}_1^T\mathbf{a}_3}{\mathbf{q}_1^T\mathbf{q}_1}\mathbf{q}_1 - \frac{\mathbf{q}_2^T\mathbf{a}_3}{\mathbf{q}_2^T\mathbf{q}_2}\mathbf{q}_2$$
Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization steps

2.3 QR Factorization

Any matrix $A$ with linearly independent columns can be factored as $A = QR$, where $Q$ has orthonormal columns and $R$ is upper triangular.

$$A = QR, \quad R = Q^TA$$
QR factorization
Key Property: The entries of $R$ are inner products: $r_{ij} = \mathbf{q}_i^T\mathbf{a}_j$. Because each $\mathbf{q}_j$ is perpendicular to all previous $\mathbf{q}_i$ ($i < j$), the lower triangle of $R$ is zero.

2.4 Properties of Orthogonal Matrices

  • $Q^TQ = QQ^T = I$
  • $|Q\mathbf{x}| = |\mathbf{x}|$ for all $\mathbf{x}$ (preserves lengths)
  • $\det(Q) = \pm 1$ ($+1$ for rotation, $-1$ for reflection)
  • $Q^{-1} = Q^T$

3. Properties of Determinants

The determinant is a scalar function that measures the volume scaling factor of a linear transformation. It provides a complete test for invertibility and satisfies elegant algebraic properties.

3.1 Computing via Elimination

Gaussian elimination reduces $A$ to upper triangular form $U$. The determinant is the product of the pivots, up to sign from row exchanges:

$$\det(A) = (-1)^r \cdot u_{11} u_{22} \cdots u_{nn}$$
Determinant via elimination ($r$ = row exchanges)

3.2 Key Properties

PropertyMathematical Statement
Identity$\det(I) = 1$
Row exchange$\det(PA) = -\det(A)$
Row scaling$\det(cR_i) = c \cdot \det(A)$
Row addition$R_i \leftarrow R_i + cR_j$ leaves $\det$ unchanged
Zero rowRow of zeroes $\implies \det = 0$
Product$\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B)$
Transpose$\det(A^T) = \det(A)$
Scalar multiple$\det(cA) = c^n\det(A)$ for $n \times n$ matrix

3.3 Singularity Test

Invertibility Criterion: $A$ is invertible if and only if $\det(A) \neq 0$. If elimination produces a zero pivot, $\det(A) = 0$. If all pivots are nonzero, $\det(A)$ equals their product (up to sign).

3.4 Determinant of Special Matrices

  • Diagonal: $\det(D) = d_1 d_2 \cdots d_n$
  • Triangular: $\det(T) = \prod_{i=1}^n t_{ii}$
  • Inverse: $\det(A^{-1}) = 1/\det(A)$
  • Power: $\det(A^k) = (\det(A))^k$
