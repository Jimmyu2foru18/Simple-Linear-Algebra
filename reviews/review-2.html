Matrix Factorization & Subspaces

Comprehensive review of LU factorization, matrix transposes, vector spaces $\mathbb{R}^n$, and column space & nullspace. Covers chapters 4–6 from Gilbert Strang's MIT 18.06 course.

1. LU Factorization

When we perform Gaussian elimination without row exchanges, the process naturally produces a factorization $A = LU$, where $L$ is a lower triangular matrix containing the multipliers used during elimination, and $U$ is the resulting upper triangular matrix.

1.1 The L Matrix

$L$ is lower triangular with ones on the diagonal. Its subdiagonal entries $l_{ij}$ are exactly the multipliers used to eliminate the entries below the pivots during Gaussian elimination.

$$A = LU \quad \text{where} \quad L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ l_{21} & 1 & 0 \\ l_{31} & l_{32} & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad U = \begin{bmatrix} u_{11} & u_{12} & u_{13} \\ 0 & u_{22} & u_{23} \\ 0 & 0 & u_{33} \end{bmatrix}$$
LU decomposition structure

1.2 Solving Systems Using LU

To solve $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ using LU factorization:

  1. Factor $A = LU$ (done once)
  2. Solve $L\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}$ (forward substitution)
  3. Solve $U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}$ (back substitution)

1.3 Computational Cost

The cost of Gaussian elimination for an $n \times n$ matrix is approximately $n^3/3$ operations. This is far more efficient than computing $A^{-1}$ (which costs $n^3$ operations) and then multiplying by $\mathbf{b}$.

1.4 Permutation Matrices

When row exchanges are needed, we introduce permutation matrices $P$. The factorization becomes $PA = LU$, where $P$ reorders the rows of $A$ to enable safe elimination. $P$ is orthogonal: $P^{-1} = P^T$.

2. Transposes & Permutations

The transpose of a matrix $A$, denoted $A^T$, is formed by flipping $A$ across its main diagonal. Rows become columns and columns become rows.

2.1 Properties of Transposes

$$(A^T)_{ij} = a_{ji}$$
Transpose operation

Key algebraic rules:

  • $(A^T)^T = A$
  • $(A + B)^T = A^T + B^T$
  • $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$ (order reversal!)

2.2 Symmetric Matrices

A matrix is symmetric if $A^T = A$. Symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors. They appear frequently in applications because they represent quadratic forms and covariance matrices.

2.3 Permutation Matrices

Permutation matrices are obtained by permuting the rows (or columns) of the identity matrix. They have exactly one '1' in each row and column, with zeros elsewhere. $P^T = P^{-1}$, so permutation matrices are orthogonal.

Mnemonic: "Flip Across the Diagonal"
To transpose a matrix, flip it across its main diagonal (top-left to bottom-right). Visualize turning the page of a book.

3. Vector Spaces $\mathbb{R}^n$

A vector space is a collection of vectors closed under addition and scalar multiplication. $\mathbb{R}^n$ is the $n$-dimensional real vector space. A subspace is a subset that is itself a vector space, requiring closure under linear combinations.

3.1 The Four Requirements of a Vector Space

A set $V$ with addition and scalar multiplication is a vector space if it satisfies:

  1. Closure under addition: $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in V$
  2. Closure under scalar multiplication: $c\mathbf{u} \in V$
  3. Commutativity: $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$
  4. Existence of zero vector: $\mathbf{0} \in V$

3.2 Common Vector Spaces

SpaceDescriptionDimension
$\mathbb{R}^n$All $n$-tuples of real numbers$n$
$M_{m \times n}(\mathbb{R})$All $m \times n$ matrices$mn$
$P_n$Polynomials of degree $\leq n$$n+1$

3.3 Subspace Test

A nonempty subset $W \subseteq V$ is a subspace if and only if:
  1. $\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in W$ for all $\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in W$
  2. $c\mathbf{u} \in W$ for all $c \in \mathbb{R}, \mathbf{u} \in W$

3.4 Span

$$\text{span}\{\mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_k\} = \{c_1\mathbf{v}_1 + \cdots + c_k\mathbf{v}_k : c_i \in \mathbb{R}\}$$
Span definition

The span of a set of vectors is the set of all possible linear combinations. It is always a subspace.

4. Column Space & Nullspace

Understanding the fundamental subspaces of a matrix. This section introduces the column space and nullspace, two key subspaces that characterize the behavior of the matrix equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$.

4.1 The Column Space $C(A)$

The column space of $A$, denoted $C(A)$, is the set of all linear combinations of the columns of $A$. It is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^m$ (where $A$ is $m \times n$). The system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ is solvable if and only if $\mathbf{b}$ lies in $C(A)$.

4.2 The Nullspace $N(A)$

The nullspace of $A$, denoted $N(A)$, is the set of all solutions to $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. It is a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$. If $A$ is square and invertible, the nullspace contains only the zero vector. Otherwise, the nullspace has nontrivial solutions.

4.3 Rank and Pivot Columns

The rank of $A$ is the dimension of the column space, equal to the number of pivot columns in the row-echelon form of $A$. The pivot columns of $A$ form a basis for $C(A)$.

4.4 Rank-Nullity Theorem

$$\text{rank}(A) + \text{nullity}(A) = n$$
Rank-Nullity Theorem

For an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ with rank $r$:

  • $\dim(C(A)) = r$ (number of pivot columns)
  • $\dim(N(A)) = n - r$ (number of free variables)

4.5 Free Variables and the Nullspace

Variables corresponding to non-pivot columns are free variables. Setting one free variable to 1 (and others to 0) generates a special solution. All special solutions span the nullspace.
