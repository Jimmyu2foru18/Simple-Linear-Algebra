Chapter 28: Positive Definite Matrices and Minima

This chapter reveals the deep connection between positive definite matrices and optimization. Just as a positive second derivative guarantees a minimum in single-variable calculus, a positive definite matrix of second derivatives guarantees a minimum in multivariable calculus. The chapter shows how completing the square is exactly Gaussian elimination in disguise, how pivots appear outside the squares and multipliers inside, and how the geometry of the graph—a bowl, an ellipsoid, a paraboloid—reflects the eigenvalue structure of the matrix. The principal axis theorem states that every positive definite matrix can be diagonalized by an orthogonal transformation, aligning the axes of the bowl with the eigenvectors.

Detailed Topic Breakdowns

From Calculus to Linear Algebra: The Second Derivative Test

In single-variable calculus, a function $f(x)$ has a minimum at $x = a$ when $f'(a) = 0$ and $f''(a) > 0$. The first derivative being zero finds the critical point; the second derivative being positive ensures it is a minimum rather than a maximum. In multivariable calculus, the first derivative becomes the gradient $\nabla f = 0$, and the second derivative becomes the Hessian matrix $H$ of second partial derivatives. The condition $f''(a) > 0$ generalizes to $H$ being positive definite. For a function $f(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$, the Hessian is the symmetric matrix $H_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j}$. By Clairaut's theorem, mixed partials are equal: $\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_i \partial x_j} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_j \partial x_i}$, so $H$ is symmetric.

Completing the Square Is Elimination

For a quadratic form $Q(x) = x^T A x$ with $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 6 & 20 \end{bmatrix}$, completing the square gives $Q(x) = 2(x_1 + 3x_2)^2 + 2x_2^2$. The numbers outside the squares (2 and 2) are the pivots from Gaussian elimination. The numbers inside the squares (3) are the multipliers from elimination. This is not a coincidence: completing the square is exactly Gaussian elimination expressed in terms of squares. The pivot is the coefficient outside the first square; the multiplier is the coefficient inside. Positive pivots guarantee a sum of positive squares, which guarantees $Q(x) > 0$ for all nonzero $x$.

The Matrix of Second Derivatives

For a function $f(x, y)$, the matrix of second derivatives at a critical point is: $$H = \begin{bmatrix} f_{xx} & f_{xy} \\ f_{yx} & f_{yy} \end{bmatrix}$$ where $f_{xx} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x^2}$, $f_{yy} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial y^2}$, and $f_{xy} = f_{yx} = \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x \partial y}$. The diagonal entries $f_{xx}$ and $f_{yy}$ must be positive for a minimum. But that is not enough: the cross-derivative $f_{xy}$ must not be too large. The determinant condition $f_{xx} f_{yy} - f_{xy}^2 > 0$ ensures the cross-term is controlled. In matrix language, $H$ must be positive definite: all eigenvalues positive, all pivots positive, all leading principal minors positive.

Geometry: Bowls, Ellipsoids, and the Principal Axis Theorem

The graph of $f(x) = x^T A x$ with $A$ positive definite is a bowl-shaped surface called a paraboloid. For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, the function is $f(x) = 2x_1^2 + 2x_2^2 + 2x_3^2 - 2x_1x_2 - 2x_2x_3$. The level set $f(x) = 1$ is an ellipsoid—a "football" shape in 3D. The principal axis theorem states that $A = Q \Lambda Q^T$ where $Q$ has orthonormal eigenvectors and $\Lambda$ is diagonal with eigenvalues $\lambda_i$. The eigenvectors give the principal axes of the ellipsoid; the eigenvalues determine the lengths of those axes. Specifically, the axis in direction $q_i$ has length proportional to $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$.

The Test for Positive Definiteness

For a symmetric matrix $A$, these conditions are equivalent: (1) all eigenvalues $\lambda_i > 0$; (2) all pivots $d_i > 0$; (3) all leading principal minors $\det(A_k) > 0$ for $k = 1, 2, \ldots, n$; (4) $x^T A x > 0$ for all nonzero $x$; (5) $A$ can be written as $A = L D L^T$ with $D$ having positive diagonal entries. The pivot test is often fastest computationally. For the matrix $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, the pivots are $2, 3/2, 4/3$, all positive, so the matrix is positive definite. The eigenvalues are $2 - \sqrt{2} \approx 0.586$, $2$, and $2 + \sqrt{2} \approx 3.414$, all positive. The determinants of leading principal minors are $2, 3, 4$, all positive.

Computation Examples

Derivation: Completing the Square = Gaussian Elimination

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 6 & 20 \end{bmatrix}$, the quadratic form is $Q(x) = 2x_1^2 + 12x_1x_2 + 20x_2^2$. Completing the square in $x_1$: factor out 2, add and subtract $(6x_2)^2/2 = 18x_2^2$: $$Q(x) = 2\left(x_1^2 + 6x_1x_2 + 9x_2^2\right) + 20x_2^2 - 18x_2^2 = 2(x_1 + 3x_2)^2 + 2x_2^2$$ The pivot 2 is outside the first square; the multiplier 3 is inside. The second pivot is 2. Gaussian elimination gives $L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, so $A = LU$. The pivots are exactly the coefficients outside the squares.

Derivation: Positive Definiteness from Pivots and Eigenvalues

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, compute pivots by elimination: pivot 1 = 2. Eliminate: row 2 + (1/2)row 1 gives $[0, 1.5, -1]$. Pivot 2 = 1.5. Eliminate: row 3 + (2/3)row 2 gives $[0, 0, 4/3]$. Pivot 3 = 4/3. All pivots positive. Determinants of leading principal minors: $\det(A_1) = 2 > 0$, $\det(A_2) = 3 > 0$, $\det(A_3) = 4 > 0$. Eigenvalues of this tridiagonal matrix are $2(1 - \cos(k\pi/4))$ for $k=1,2,3$: approximately $0.586, 2.0, 3.414$, all positive. All five tests confirm $A$ is positive definite.

Derivation: The Principal Axis Theorem

For a positive definite symmetric matrix $A$, the spectral theorem gives $A = Q \Lambda Q^T$ with orthonormal eigenvectors $q_i$ and positive eigenvalues $\lambda_i$. The quadratic form becomes: $$x^T A x = x^T Q \Lambda Q^T x = y^T \Lambda y = \lambda_1 y_1^2 + \lambda_2 y_2^2 + \cdots + \lambda_n y_n^2$$ where $y = Q^T x$ is the coordinate vector in the eigenvector basis. The level set $x^T A x = 1$ becomes $\lambda_1 y_1^2 + \cdots + \lambda_n y_n^2 = 1$, which is an ellipsoid with semi-axes $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$ in the directions $q_i$. The eigenvectors determine the orientation of the ellipsoid; the eigenvalues determine its shape.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "Second Derivative Positive = Minimum; Matrix Positive Definite = Minimum"

In calculus: $f''(a) > 0$ means minimum. In linear algebra: $H$ positive definite means minimum. The scalar second derivative becomes the Hessian matrix of second derivatives.

Mnemonic: "Completing the Square = Elimination: Pivots Outside, Multipliers Inside"

When you complete the square for $x^T A x$, the numbers outside the squares are the pivots and the numbers inside are the multipliers from Gaussian elimination. Positive pivots mean a sum of positive squares.

Mnemonic: "Eigenvalues Tell Axis Lengths: 1/sqrt(lambda)"

For the ellipsoid $x^T A x = 1$, the principal axes are in the eigenvector directions. The semi-axis length in direction $q_i$ is $1/\sqrt{\lambda_i}$. Larger eigenvalue = shorter axis.

Study Tips

  • For a minimum of $f(x)$, first set $\nabla f = 0$, then check that the Hessian is positive definite
  • Completing the square is just Gaussian elimination in disguise: pivots go outside, multipliers go inside
  • For $2 \times 2$ matrices, the test $a > 0$ and $ac > b^2$ is equivalent to positive definiteness
  • The geometry of $x^T A x = 1$ is an ellipsoid when $A$ is positive definite, a hyperboloid when indefinite
  • Pivots are usually faster to compute than eigenvalues for testing positive definiteness
  • If any pivot is zero or negative, the matrix is NOT positive definite

The matrix "sees" along its eigenvectors.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Completing the Square and Elimination

Problem: For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 6 & 20 \end{bmatrix}$, complete the square for $Q(x) = x^T A x$ and relate the result to Gaussian elimination.

Solution:

  • $Q(x) = 2x_1^2 + 12x_1x_2 + 20x_2^2$
  • Complete the square: $Q(x) = 2(x_1 + 3x_2)^2 + 2x_2^2$
  • Pivot 1 = 2 (outside first square), multiplier = 3 (inside square)
  • Pivot 2 = 2 (outside second square)
  • Gaussian elimination: $L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$
  • $A = LU = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 3 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$
  • All pivots positive $\Rightarrow$ positive definite

Example 2: Positive Definiteness of a Tridiagonal Matrix

Problem: Determine whether $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ is positive definite using pivots, determinants, and eigenvalues.

Solution:

  • Pivot test: pivot 1 = 2, pivot 2 = 3/2, pivot 3 = 4/3. All positive.
  • Determinant test: $\det(A_1) = 2 > 0$, $\det(A_2) = 3 > 0$, $\det(A_3) = 4 > 0$. All positive.
  • Eigenvalue test: eigenvalues are $2(1 - \cos(k\pi/4))$ for $k=1,2,3$, giving approximately $0.586, 2.0, 3.414$. All positive.
  • Conclusion: $A$ is positive definite by all tests.
  • Quadratic form: $Q(x) = 2x_1^2 + 2x_2^2 + 2x_3^2 - 2x_1x_2 - 2x_2x_3 > 0$ for all nonzero $x$.

Example 3: Geometry of the Ellipsoid

Problem: For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, describe the geometry of the ellipsoid $x^T A x = 1$. Find the principal axes and their lengths.

Solution:

  • Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 2 - \sqrt{2} \approx 0.586$, $\lambda_2 = 2$, $\lambda_3 = 2 + \sqrt{2} \approx 3.414$
  • Eigenvectors give principal axes directions
  • Semi-axis lengths: $1/\sqrt{\lambda_1} \approx 1.306$, $1/\sqrt{\lambda_2} = 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707$, $1/\sqrt{\lambda_3} \approx 0.541$
  • The ellipsoid is a "lopsided football" with one long axis and two shorter axes
  • Since all eigenvalues are distinct, the ellipsoid has three distinct principal axes
  • The graph of $f(x) = x^T A x$ is a bowl (paraboloid) opening upward

Practice Problems

Problem Set 28

Problem 1: For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 6 \\ 6 & 20 \end{bmatrix}$, complete the square for $Q(x) = x^T A x$. Identify the pivots and multipliers, and explain why $A$ is positive definite.

Hint: Factor out the first pivot from the first two terms, then complete the square. The multiplier is the ratio of the off-diagonal entry to the first pivot.

Solution

$Q(x) = 2x_1^2 + 12x_1x_2 + 20x_2^2 = 2(x_1 + 3x_2)^2 + 2x_2^2$. Pivot 1 = 2, multiplier = 3, pivot 2 = 2. All pivots positive, so $A$ is positive definite. The quadratic form is a sum of positive squares.

Problem 2: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Determine if $A$ is positive definite using the pivot test, determinant test, and eigenvalue test.

Hint: Compute the pivots from elimination. What is the second pivot? What does a negative second pivot tell you?

Solution

Pivot 1 = 1. Eliminate: row 2 - 2*row 1 gives $[0, -3]$. Pivot 2 = -3. Since pivot 2 is negative, $A$ is not positive definite. Eigenvalues: $\lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 3 = 0$, giving $\lambda_1 = 3 > 0$, $\lambda_2 = -1 < 0$. One positive, one negative: indefinite. Determinant = -3 < 0.

Problem 3: For $f(x, y) = 2x^2 + 12xy + 20y^2$, find the critical point, verify it is a minimum using the Hessian, and describe the geometry of the level curves $f(x, y) = c$.

Hint: The critical point is at the origin. The Hessian is the matrix of second derivatives. Complete the square to identify the curve type.

Solution

Critical point: $\nabla f = [4x + 12y, 12x + 40y] = [0, 0] \Rightarrow x = 0, y = 0$. Hessian: $H = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 12 \\ 12 & 40 \end{bmatrix}$. Pivots: 4 and $(40 - 36)/4 = 1$. Both positive, so $H$ is positive definite. The origin is a strict local minimum. Level curves: $2(x+3y)^2 + 2y^2 = c$, which are ellipses centered at the origin.

Problem 4: Prove that if $A$ is positive definite, then all its diagonal entries $a_{ii} > 0$. Then give an example of a matrix with positive diagonal entries that is NOT positive definite.

Hint: Consider $x = e_i$ (the $i$-th standard basis vector). What is $e_i^T A e_i$? For the example, try a $2 \times 2$ matrix with large off-diagonal entries.

Solution

If $A$ is positive definite, then $x^T A x > 0$ for all nonzero $x$. Take $x = e_i$: $e_i^T A e_i = a_{ii} > 0$. So all diagonal entries must be positive. However, positive diagonals are not sufficient. For example, $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ has positive diagonals but $\det(A) = -3 < 0$, so it is indefinite (eigenvalues $3$ and $-1$).
