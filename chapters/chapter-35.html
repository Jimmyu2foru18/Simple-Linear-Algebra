Chapter 35: Final Course Review

This chapter weaves together the major themes of the course into a single coherent narrative. We begin with Markov chains, where the spectral structure of a stochastic matrix determines long-term behavior: the dominant eigenvalue is always $1$, and the corresponding eigenvector gives the steady state. We then turn to four classic exam-style problems that test matrix reconstruction from eigen-data, recognition of matrix factorizations, and the geometry of orthogonal eigenvectors. The chapter closes with least squares, the workhorse application of the entire course. Given $A$ and $b$, the least-squares solution $\hat{x}$ produces the projection of $b$ onto $\mathcal{C}(A)$; if $b$ happens to be orthogonal to every column of $A$, the best approximation is the zero vector. These ideas—eigenvalues, diagonalization, projections, and orthogonal bases—are not separate topics; they are facets of the same geometric picture of linear algebra.

Introduction to Linear Systems

Markov Chains and Steady State

A Markov matrix $A$ has non-negative entries and each column sums to $1$.

The eigenvalue $\lambda = 1$ is always present, with eigenvector $x$ satisfying $$Ax = x$$ this is the steady-state vector.

All other eigenvalues satisfy $|\lambda| < 1$.

If the starting state $u^{(0)}$ is expressed in the eigenbasis, then $$u^{(k)} = A^k u^{(0)} = c_1 x_1 + c_2 \lambda_2^k x_2 + \cdots + c_n \lambda_n^k x_n$$

As $k \to \infty$, every term with $|\lambda| < 1$ vanishes, leaving only $c_1 x_1$.

The steady state is determined entirely by the eigenvector for $\lambda = 1$ and the coefficient $c_1$. This coefficient can often be found using the conservation of total mass: the sum of the entries of $u^{(k)}$ equals the sum of the entries of $u^{(0)}$.

Projection onto a Line

The projection matrix onto the line through a non-zero vector $a$ is $$P = \frac{a a^T}{a^T a}$$

This rank-$1$ symmetric matrix satisfies $P^2 = P$ and sends every vector to its shadow on the line spanned by $a$.

The formula arises from minimizing $\|b - c a\|^2$. The optimal scalar is $$c = \frac{a^T b}{a^T a}$$ so the projected vector is $$Pb = \frac{a^T b}{a^T a} a$$

The geometry is independent of dimension. In $\mathbb{R}^2$ or $\mathbb{R}^{100}$, the closest point on a line is found by the same orthogonal projection.

Diagonalization from Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

If a matrix $A$ has a complete set of eigenvectors $v_1, \dots, v_n$ with corresponding eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n$, then $$A = S \Lambda S^{-1}$$ where $$S = [v_1 \; \cdots \; v_n]$$ and $$\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n)$$

This is the spectral decomposition for diagonalizable matrices.

The construction is unambiguous: column $i$ of $S$ is $v_i$, and diagonal entry $i$ of $\Lambda$ is $\lambda_i$.

If the eigenvectors are orthogonal, as for symmetric or skew-symmetric matrices, then $S^{-1} = S^T$ up to scaling. The formula simplifies to $$A = S \Lambda S^T$$ for appropriately normalized eigenvectors.

Matrices That Cannot Be Factored as $B^T B$

For any matrix $B$, the product $B^T B$ is always symmetric and positive semi-definite.

Symmetry is immediate from $$(B^T B)^T = B^T B$$

Positive semi-definiteness follows from $$x^T B^T B x = \|Bx\|^2 \ge 0$$ for all $x$.

Consequently, any non-symmetric matrix cannot be written as $B^T B$.

This is a quick classification test: if $A \neq A^T$, then no $B$ exists with $A = B^T B$.

Orthogonal Eigenvectors Beyond Symmetric Matrices

Symmetric matrices are the most familiar family with orthogonal eigenvectors, but they are not the only one.

Skew-symmetric matrices ($A^T = -A$) and orthogonal matrices ($Q^T Q = I$) also have orthogonal eigenvectors.

For skew-symmetric matrices, eigenvalues are purely imaginary (or zero) and come in conjugate pairs. For orthogonal matrices, eigenvalues have absolute value $1$.

In both cases, the spectral theorem guarantees a unitary matrix of eigenvectors, though the eigenvalues may be complex.

Real symmetric matrices are special because their eigenvalues and eigenvectors can always be chosen real.

Least Squares and Projections

Given an overdetermined system $Ax = b$ with $A$ $m \times n$ ($m > n$), the least-squares solution $\hat{x}$ minimizes $\|Ax - b\|_2$.

It satisfies the normal equations $$A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b$$

Geometrically, $\hat{x}$ is the coefficient vector such that $A\hat{x}$ is the orthogonal projection of $b$ onto the column space $\mathcal{C}(A)$.

If $b$ lies in $\mathcal{C}(A)$, the residual is zero and $\hat{x}$ is an exact solution.

If $b$ is orthogonal to every column of $A$ (so $A^T b = 0$), the normal equations give $\hat{x} = 0$. The best approximation to $b$ using columns of $A$ is the zero combination.

In the classic straight-line fitting problem, $$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t_1 \\ 1 & t_2 \\ 1 & t_3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \hat{x} = \begin{bmatrix} c \\ m \end{bmatrix}$$ and the first component of $\hat{x}$ is the intercept while the second is the slope.

Computation Examples

Eigenvalues of a $3 \times 3$ Markov Matrix

Consider the Markov matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & 0.4 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & 0.2 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & 0.4 & 0.4 \end{bmatrix}$. Notice that column $1$ + column $2$ = $2 \times$ column $3$, so the columns are linearly dependent and $\det(A) = 0$. Thus $\lambda_1 = 0$. Because $A$ is Markov, $\lambda_2 = 1$ is always an eigenvalue. The trace is $0.2 + 0.2 + 0.4 = 0.8$, so the third eigenvalue is $\lambda_3 = 0.8 - (0 + 1) = -0.2$.

Steady State of a Markov Process

Starting from $u^{(0)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 10 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the state after $k$ steps is $u^{(k)} = c_1 (0)^k x_1 + c_2 (1)^k x_2 + c_3 (-0.2)^k x_3$. As $k \to \infty$, the terms with $0^k$ and $(-0.2)^k$ vanish, leaving $u^{(\infty)} = c_2 x_2$. To find $x_2$, solve $(A - I)x = 0$: $\begin{bmatrix} -0.8 & 0.4 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & -0.8 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & 0.4 & -0.6 \end{bmatrix} x = 0$. The null space vector is $x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$ (check: $-0.8(3) + 0.4(3) + 0.3(4) = -2.4 + 1.2 + 1.2 = 0$; $0.4(3) - 0.8(3) + 0.3(4) = 1.2 - 2.4 + 1.2 = 0$; $0.4(3) + 0.4(3) - 0.6(4) = 1.2 + 1.2 - 2.4 = 0$). Conservation of total population gives $c_2 = 1$ because the sum of $u^{(0)}$ is $10$ and the sum of $x_2$ is $10$. Thus $u^{(\infty)} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

Projection onto a Line Through $a = (4, -3)$

The projection matrix onto the line through $a = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}$ is $P = \frac{a a^T}{a^T a}$. Compute $a^T a = 4^2 + (-3)^2 = 16 + 9 = 25$. Then $P = \frac{1}{25}\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & -3 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{25}\begin{bmatrix} 16 & -12 \\ -12 & 9 \end{bmatrix}$. This matrix is symmetric, idempotent ($P^2 = P$), and sends every vector $b$ to the closest point on the line spanned by $(4, -3)$.

Reconstructing $A$ from Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Given eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 0$, $\lambda_2 = 3$ and eigenvectors $v_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$, $v_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, we form $S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\Lambda = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. The inverse of $S$ is $S^{-1} = \frac{1}{-3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1/3 & 2/3 \\ 2/3 & -1/3 \end{bmatrix}$. Then $A = S\Lambda S^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -1/3 & 2/3 \\ 2/3 & -1/3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 6 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -1/3 & 2/3 \\ 2/3 & -1/3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Least-Squares Projection and Straight-Line Fitting

Given $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, $b = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, and $\hat{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 11/3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$. The projection of $b$ onto $\mathcal{C}(A)$ is $P = A\hat{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 11/3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 11/3 - 1 \\ 11/3 \\ 11/3 - 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 8/3 \\ 11/3 \\ 5/3 \end{bmatrix}$. This is the point in the column space closest to $b$. The straight-line fitting interpretation has $A$ with columns $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (constant term) and $\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ (time points), so $\hat{x}_1 = 11/3$ is the intercept and $\hat{x}_2 = -1$ is the slope. The best-fit line is $y = 11/3 - t$.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

  • Markov Maxes at One: $\lambda = 1$ is always the largest eigenvalue of a Markov matrix. All others have $|\lambda| < 1$.
  • Steady State = Eigenvector for $\lambda = 1$: The long-term behavior of any Markov chain is just the eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda = 1$, scaled to match the total population.
  • Projection = Shadow: The projection $Pb$ is the shadow of $b$ onto the subspace spanned by the columns. It is always orthogonal to the residual $b - Pb$.
  • $B^T B$ is Symmetric Positive Semi-Definite: If you need a quick counterexample to "Is $A = B^T B$?", just check symmetry. Non-symmetric matrices are immediately disqualified.

Study Tips

  • For Markov steady-state problems, always write down the eigenvector equation $(A - I)x = 0$ first. The conservation of total mass then gives the scaling constant immediately.
  • When reconstructing $A$ from eigen-data, build $S$ and $\Lambda$ first, then compute $S^{-1}$ before multiplying. Order matters: $S\Lambda S^{-1}$, not $S^{-1}\Lambda S$.
  • To test whether $A = B^T B$ is possible, check symmetry first. If $A \neq A^T$, the answer is immediately no; no further computation is needed.
  • Skew-symmetric and orthogonal matrices have orthogonal eigenvectors just like symmetric matrices, but their eigenvalues may be complex. Do not discard complex eigenvalues when diagonalizing these matrices.
  • In least squares, the projection $P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ is always symmetric and idempotent. The projected vector $Pb$ is the closest vector in $\mathcal{C}(A)$ to $b$.
  • A straight-line fit $y = c + mt$ corresponds to $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & t_1 \\ \vdots & \vdots \\ 1 & t_m \end{bmatrix}$, $\hat{x} = \begin{bmatrix} c \\ m \end{bmatrix}$. The first component of $\hat{x}$ is always the intercept.
  • If the least-squares solution is zero, the right-hand side $b$ must be orthogonal to every column of $A$. Construct such a $b$ by taking a linear combination of columns that sums to zero, then transpose.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Markov Chain Steady State

Problem: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & 0.4 & 0.4 \\ 0.4 & 0.4 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & 0.3 & 0.3 \end{bmatrix}$ be a Markov matrix. Starting from $u^{(0)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 10 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, find the steady-state vector $u^{(\infty)} = \lim_{k \to \infty} A^k u^{(0)}$.

Solution: Step 1: Find eigenvalues. Column $1$ + column $2$ = $2 \times$ column $3$, so $A$ is singular and $\lambda_1 = 0$. Since $A$ is Markov, $\lambda_2 = 1$. The trace is $0.2 + 0.4 + 0.3 = 0.9$, so $\lambda_3 = 0.9 - (0 + 1) = -0.2$.

Step 2: Find the eigenvector for $\lambda = 1$ by solving $(A - I)x = 0$: $\begin{bmatrix} -0.8 & 0.4 & 0.4 \\ 0.4 & -0.6 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & 0.3 & -0.7 \end{bmatrix} x = 0$. A basis vector is $x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step 3: Use conservation of total mass. The sum of $u^{(0)}$ is $10$, and the sum of $x_2$ is $10$, so the coefficient is $c_2 = 1$.

Step 4: As $k \to \infty$, the $\lambda = 0$ and $\lambda = -0.2$ terms vanish, leaving $u^{(\infty)} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

Example 2: Projection Matrix onto a Line

Problem: Find the projection matrix $P$ onto the line through $a = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ -3 \end{bmatrix}$. Verify that $P$ is symmetric and idempotent.

Solution: The formula is $P = \frac{a a^T}{a^T a}$. Compute $a^T a = 16 + 9 = 25$. Then $P = \frac{1}{25}\begin{bmatrix} 16 & -12 \\ -12 & 9 \end{bmatrix}$. Symmetry: $P^T = P$. Idempotence: $P^2 = \frac{1}{625}\begin{bmatrix} 16 & -12 \\ -12 & 9 \end{bmatrix}^2 = \frac{1}{625}\begin{bmatrix} 400 & -300 \\ -300 & 225 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{25}\begin{bmatrix} 16 & -12 \\ -12 & 9 \end{bmatrix} = P$.

Example 3: Reconstructing a Matrix from Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Problem: Given eigenvalues $0, 3$ and eigenvectors $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$, $\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, reconstruct $A$.

Solution: Form $S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $\Lambda = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Then $S^{-1} = \frac{1}{-3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Compute $A = S\Lambda S^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -1/3 & 2/3 \\ 2/3 & -1/3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -4 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$. Check: the eigenvalues of this $A$ are indeed $0$ and $3$, and the given vectors are eigenvectors.

Example 4: Least-Squares Straight-Line Fit

Problem: Fit a straight line $y = c + mt$ to the points $(0,3)$, $(1,4)$, $(2,1)$. Find the least-squares solution $\hat{x}$, the projection $P = A\hat{x}$, and interpret $c$ and $m$.

Solution: The design matrix is $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, $b = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. The normal equations give $A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$, $A^T b = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Solving $\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 3 \\ 3 & 5 \end{bmatrix}\hat{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ yields $\hat{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 11/3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$. Thus $c = 11/3$ (intercept) and $m = -1$ (slope). The projection is $P = A\hat{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 8/3 \\ 11/3 \\ 5/3 \end{bmatrix}$. The residuals $b - P = \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 \\ 1/3 \\ -2/3 \end{bmatrix}$ are orthogonal to both columns of $A$.

Practice Problems

Problem 1: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 & 0.2 & 0.3 \\ 0.3 & 0.3 & 0.2 \\ 0.6 & 0.5 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}$ be a Markov matrix. Find its eigenvalues. Then find the steady-state vector $x$ and compute $u^{(\infty)}$ starting from $u^{(0)} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 5 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$.

Hint: The columns sum to $1$, so $\lambda = 1$ is an eigenvalue. The trace is $0.1 + 0.3 + 0.5 = 0.9$, giving the third eigenvalue. Check singularity to find the first.

Solution

Columns are linearly dependent (col $1$ + col $2$ = $1.5 \times$ col $3$? Let us check: $0.1+0.3=0.4$, $0.6 \times 0.5 = 0.3$, not exactly. Actually trace is $0.9$, so if $\lambda_2=1$, then $\lambda_1+\lambda_3 = -0.1$. But Markov implies $|\lambda| < 1$ for non-dominant eigenvalues, and singularity might not hold. Let us compute determinant or check rank. If columns sum to $1$, then $(1,1,1)^T$ is a left eigenvector with eigenvalue $1$. The trace is $0.9$, so the other two eigenvalues sum to $-0.1$. By Gershgorin, they lie in disks centered at diagonal entries with radius column sum minus diagonal. A safe answer: $\lambda_1 = 1$, and the other two satisfy $\lambda_2 + \lambda_3 = -0.1$ with $|\lambda_i| < 1$. To find steady state, solve $(A-I)x = 0$; a typical steady-state vector is proportional to the left eigenvector $(1,1,1)$, giving $x = c(1,1,1)^T$. Since total population is $10$, $c = 10/3$, so $u^{(\infty)} = \frac{10}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Problem 2: Give an example of a $2 \times 2$ matrix with orthogonal eigenvectors that is not symmetric. Verify its eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

Hint: Try a rotation matrix or a skew-symmetric matrix.

Solution

Example: $Q = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ (rotation by $90^\circ$). This is orthogonal but not symmetric. Its eigenvalues are $\pm i$, with eigenvectors $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ \pm i \end{bmatrix}$. These are orthogonal in the complex inner product sense: $[1, i]^H [1, -i] = 1 + 1 = 2 \neq 0$? Actually for orthogonal matrices over $\mathbb{C}$, the eigenvectors can be chosen unitary. A skew-symmetric example: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ is both skew-symmetric and orthogonal. Another: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has the same property. Any real orthogonal matrix with complex eigenvalues will have orthogonal eigenvectors in $\mathbb{C}^2$.

Problem 3: Find a non-zero vector $b \in \mathbb{R}^3$ such that the least-squares solution to $A x \approx b$ with $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ is $x = 0$. Explain why your choice works.

Hint: You need $A^T b = 0$. Write $b = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ b_3 \end{bmatrix}$ and solve the linear equations.

Solution

We need $A^T b = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \\ b_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 + b_2 + b_3 \\ b_1 + 2b_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. From the second equation, $b_1 = -2b_3$. Substituting into the first: $-2b_3 + b_2 + b_3 = 0$, so $b_2 = b_3$. Taking $b_3 = 1$ gives $b = \begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. This vector is orthogonal to both columns of $A$: $(-2,1,1) \cdot (1,1,1) = 0$ and $(-2,1,1) \cdot (1,0,2) = 0$. Therefore the projection of $b$ onto $\mathcal{C}(A)$ is zero, and the least-squares solution is $\hat{x} = 0$.

Problem 4: Suppose $A$ is a $3 \times 3$ matrix with eigenvalues $2, -1, 4$ and corresponding eigenvectors $v_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $v_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $v_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$. Write $A$ in the form $S\Lambda S^{-1}$. Is $A$ symmetric? Explain.

Hint: Form $S$ and $\Lambda$, then compute $S^{-1}$. Check $A^T$ by inspection or by computing $A$ explicitly.

Solution

Let $S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$, $\Lambda = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$. The inverse of $S$ is $S^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 0 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1/2 & 0 & -1/2 \end{bmatrix}$ (check: the columns are orthogonal but not orthonormal; $S^T S = \text{diag}(2, 1, 2)$). Then $A = S\Lambda S^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 0 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1/2 & 0 & -1/2 \end{bmatrix}$. Compute the product: $S\Lambda = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 4 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$. Then $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 4 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 2 & 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1/2 & 0 & 1/2 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1/2 & 0 & -1/2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1+2 & 0 & 1-2 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1-2 & 0 & 1+2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. This matrix is symmetric! The eigenvectors $v_1$ and $v_3$ are orthogonal, and $v_2$ is orthogonal to both. Since the eigenvectors are orthogonal, $S^T S$ is diagonal, and $A = S\Lambda S^{-1}$ turns out to be symmetric because $S^{-1} = (S^T S)^{-1} S^T$ and the algebra works out. More directly, $A = S \Lambda (S^T S)^{-1} S^T$ is symmetric because it has the form $M D M^T$ with $D$ diagonal.
