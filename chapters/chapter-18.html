Detailed Topic Breakdowns

Determinant via Elimination

The determinant of any square matrix can be computed by reducing to upper triangular form via row elimination. The determinant of an upper triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries. Row exchanges flip the sign of the determinant. If elimination produces a row of zeroes, the determinant is zero. This connects the determinant directly to the pivots produced by Gaussian elimination.

Diagonal and Triangular Matrices

For a diagonal matrix $D = \text{diag}(d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_n)$, the determinant is the product of the diagonal entries: $\text{det}(D) = d_1 d_2 \cdots d_n$. This follows from Property 3A (factoring out row multipliers) and Property 1 (determinant of the identity is 1). For triangular matrices, the same formula applies. If any diagonal entry is zero, the determinant is zero, which corresponds to singularity.

Determinant of a Product

Property 9 states that the determinant of a product equals the product of the determinants: $\text{det}(AB) = \text{det}(A)\text{det}(B)$. This is remarkable because the determinant does not satisfy an addition property ($\text{det}(A+B) \neq \text{det}(A) + \text{det}(B)$), but it does satisfy a multiplication property. From this, $\text{det}(A^{-1}) = 1/\text{det}(A)$, $\text{det}(A^2) = (\text{det}(A))^2$, and more generally $\text{det}(A^k) = (\text{det}(A))^k$.

Determinant of Scalar Multiples

For a scalar $c$ and $n \times n$ matrix $A$, $\text{det}(cA) = c^n \text{det}(A)$. This is because factoring $c$ out of each of the $n$ rows contributes a factor of $c$ per row by Property 3A. Geometrically, scaling all sides of an $n$-dimensional box by $c$ multiplies its volume by $c^n$. For a $3 \times 3$ matrix, $\text{det}(2A) = 2^3 \text{det}(A) = 8\text{det}(A)$.

Determinant of Transpose

Property 10 states that $\text{det}(A^T) = \text{det}(A)$. Transposing a matrix does not change its determinant. This means all properties that hold for rows also hold for columns: if a column is zero, the determinant is zero; exchanging two columns reverses the sign; adding a multiple of one column to another leaves the determinant unchanged. The proof uses LU factorization: $A = LU$ implies $A^T = U^T L^T$, and $\text{det}(L) = \text{det}(L^T) = 1$ for lower triangular matrices with ones on the diagonal.

Singular vs Non-Singular

The determinant provides a complete test for invertibility: $\text{det}(A) \neq 0$ if and only if $A$ is invertible. If $A$ is singular, elimination produces a row of zeroes, and by Property 6 the determinant is zero. If $A$ is invertible, elimination produces a full set of pivots, and the determinant is the product of those non-zero pivots. The determinant is zero exactly when the column space is not full-dimensional, equivalently when the null space is non-trivial.

Computation Examples

Derivation: Determinant via LU Factorization

For any invertible matrix $A$, Gaussian elimination produces $A = LU$ where $L$ is lower triangular with ones on the diagonal and $U$ is upper triangular. The determinant of $L$ is 1 (product of diagonal entries). The determinant of $U$ is the product of its diagonal entries $u_{11} u_{22} \cdots u_{nn}$, which are the pivots. Therefore:

$$\text{det}(A) = \text{det}(L)\text{det}(U) = 1 \cdot (u_{11} u_{22} \cdots u_{nn}) = \text{product of pivots}$$

If $A$ is singular, elimination produces a row of zeroes in $U$, so at least one pivot is zero and $\text{det}(A) = 0$. Row exchanges introduce sign changes: $\text{det}(A) = (-1)^r \cdot (\text{product of pivots})$ where $r$ is the number of row exchanges.

Derivation: Proof that $\text{det}(AB) = \text{det}(A)\text{det}(B)$

For diagonal matrices, the property is immediate: if $A = \text{diag}(a_1, \ldots, a_n)$ and $B = \text{diag}(b_1, \ldots, b_n)$, then $AB = \text{diag}(a_1 b_1, \ldots, a_n b_n)$ and $\text{det}(AB) = \prod a_i b_i = (\prod a_i)(\prod b_i) = \text{det}(A)\text{det}(B)$. For general matrices, use elimination: any matrix $A$ can be factored as $A = LU$ with $L$ having determinant 1. Then $AB = LUB = L(UB)$, and by induction on the number of matrices, the property extends. The key is that elimination steps (which preserve determinant up to sign) reduce any matrix to diagonal form where the property is obvious.

Derivation: Proof that $\text{det}(A^T) = \text{det}(A)$

For an invertible matrix $A$, factor $A = LU$ where $L$ is lower triangular with ones on the diagonal and $U$ is upper triangular. Then $A^T = U^T L^T$. The determinant of a triangular matrix equals the product of its diagonal entries, so $\text{det}(L) = 1$ and $\text{det}(U) = u_{11} \cdots u_{nn}$. Also $\text{det}(L^T) = 1$ and $\text{det}(U^T) = u_{11} \cdots u_{nn}$. Using Property 9:

$$\text{det}(A^T) = \text{det}(U^T)\text{det}(L^T) = (u_{11} \cdots u_{nn}) \cdot 1 = \text{det}(U)\text{det}(L) = \text{det}(A)$$

For singular matrices, both determinants are zero. For matrices requiring row exchanges, the sign changes cancel because the same number of exchanges applies to $A^T$.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "Determinant = Product of Pivots (up to sign)"

After elimination, the determinant is the product of the pivots, multiplied by $(-1)^r$ where $r$ is the number of row exchanges. A zero pivot means zero determinant.

Mnemonic: "det(AB) = det(A) times det(B)"

Multiplication multiplies determinants. This gives $\text{det}(A^{-1}) = 1/\text{det}(A)$, $\text{det}(A^2) = (\text{det}(A))^2$, and $\text{det}(cA) = c^n \text{det}(A)$. The determinant turns matrix multiplication into number multiplication.

Mnemonic: "Rows and Columns are Equal"

Property 10: $\text{det}(A^T) = \text{det}(A)$. Everything true for rows is equally true for columns. Zero column means zero determinant. Column exchange reverses sign. Column operations work just like row operations.

Study Tips

  • Compute determinants via elimination whenever possible - it is far more efficient than cofactor expansion for large matrices
  • If any pivot is zero during elimination, the determinant is zero and the matrix is singular
  • Remember: $\text{det}(I) = 1$, $\text{det}(A^T) = \text{det}(A)$, $\text{det}(AB) = \text{det}(A)\text{det}(B)$
  • For diagonal or triangular matrices, the determinant is immediately the product of diagonal entries
  • The determinant is a volume: $\text{det}(cA) = c^n \text{det}(A)$ because scaling all dimensions by $c$ scales volume by $c^n$
  • Singular means $\text{det}(A) = 0$; non-singular means $\text{det}(A) \neq 0$

Visual Aids

Figure: Elimination Path to the Determinant

From matrix $A$ to determinant via LU factorization:

$$A \xrightarrow{\text{elimination}} U \xrightarrow{\text{diagonal}} D$$

Each elimination step preserves the determinant up to sign. Once $U$ is upper triangular, $\text{det}(A) = (-1)^r \cdot d_1 d_2 \cdots d_n$ where $r$ is the number of row exchanges and $d_i$ are the diagonal entries of $U$.

Figure: The Ten Properties of Determinants

Summary of the fundamental determinant properties:

$$\text{Property 1: } \text{det}(I) = 1$$ $$\text{Property 2: Row exchange reverses sign: } \text{det}(P A) = -\text{det}(A)$$ $$\text{Property 3A: } \text{det}(cR) = c \cdot \text{det}(A) \quad \text{(factor from row)}$$ $$\text{Property 3B: } \text{det}(R_1 + R_2) = \text{det}(R_1) + \text{det}(R_2) \quad \text{(linear in each row)}$$ $$\text{Property 4: Zero row } \Rightarrow \text{det} = 0$$ $$\text{Property 5: Two equal rows } \Rightarrow \text{det} = 0$$ $$\text{Property 6: Row operation } R_i \leftarrow R_i + cR_j \text{ leaves det unchanged}$$ $$\text{Property 9: } \text{det}(AB) = \text{det}(A)\text{det}(B)$$ $$\text{Property 10: } \text{det}(A^T) = \text{det}(A)$$

Properties 1-6 define the determinant; Properties 7-10 are consequences.

Figure: Determinant as Volume

In $\mathbb{R}^n$, the absolute value of the determinant measures the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by the column vectors of $A$:

$$\text{Volume} = |\text{det}(A)|$$

For a $2 \times 2$ matrix, $|\text{det}(A)|$ is the area of the parallelogram. For a $3 \times 3$ matrix, it is the volume of the parallelepiped. Scaling the matrix by $c$ multiplies the volume by $c^n$.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Determinant via Elimination

Problem: Compute the determinant of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ using elimination.

Solution:

  • Subtract $2 \times$ row 1 from row 2: $R_2 \leftarrow R_2 - 2R_1$, giving $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • A row of zeroes appears, so $\text{det}(A) = 0$
  • This matches singularity: columns 1 and 2 are dependent ($C_2 = 2C_1$)
  • No row exchanges were needed ($r = 0$), so $\text{det}(A) = 0$ directly

Example 2: Determinant of a Triangular Matrix

Problem: Compute $\text{det}(A)$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ and verify using the product of pivots.

Solution:

  • A is already upper triangular
  • Diagonal entries: 2, 3, 4
  • $\text{det}(A) = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot 4 = 24$
  • No row exchanges needed, so sign factor is $(-1)^0 = 1$
  • The pivots from elimination are exactly 2, 3, 4

Example 3: Using Product and Transpose Properties

Problem: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Compute $\text{det}(AB)$, $\text{det}(A^T)$, $\text{det}(B^{-1})$, and $\text{det}(2B)$.

Solution:

  • $\text{det}(A) = 1(4) - 2(3) = -2$
  • $\text{det}(B) = 0(0) - 1(1) = -1$
  • $\text{det}(AB) = \text{det}(A)\text{det}(B) = (-2)(-1) = 2$
  • $\text{det}(A^T) = \text{det}(A) = -2$
  • $\text{det}(B^{-1}) = 1/\text{det}(B) = 1/(-1) = -1$
  • $\text{det}(2B) = 2^2 \text{det}(B) = 4(-1) = -4$
  • Verify directly: $AB = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, $\text{det}(AB) = 2(3) - 1(4) = 2$

Example 4: Determinant and Singularity

Problem: For what value of $c$ is $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & c \end{bmatrix}$ singular? Verify using the determinant.

Solution:

  • A is singular when $\text{det}(A) = 0$
  • Compute by elimination or cofactor expansion
  • Row 2 $- 4 \times$ row 1: $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -3 & -6 \\ 7 & 8 & c \end{bmatrix}$
  • Row 3 $- 7 \times$ row 1: $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -3 & -6 \\ 0 & -6 & c-21 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Row 3 $- 2 \times$ row 2: $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -3 & -6 \\ 0 & 0 & c-21+12 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & -3 & -6 \\ 0 & 0 & c-9 \end{bmatrix}$
  • $\text{det}(A) = 1 \cdot (-3) \cdot (c-9) = -3(c-9)$
  • Set to zero: $c = 9$
  • When $c = 9$, row 3 of $U$ is zero, confirming singularity

Practice Problems

Problem Set 18

Problem 1: Compute the determinant of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ using elimination. Verify your answer by multiplying the diagonal entries of the upper triangular matrix.

Hint: Subtract half of row 1 from row 3 to create a zero in position (3,1).

Solution

$R_3 \leftarrow R_3 - \frac{1}{2}R_1$: $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 3/2 \end{bmatrix}$.

$\text{det}(A) = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot \frac{3}{2} = 9$.

No row exchanges were needed.

Problem 2: If $\text{det}(A) = 5$ for a $4 \times 4$ matrix $A$, find $\text{det}(A^{-1})$, $\text{det}(A^2)$, $\text{det}(A^T)$, and $\text{det}(3A)$.

Hint: Use Properties 9 and 10, and the scalar multiple rule $\text{det}(cA) = c^n \text{det}(A)$.

Solution

$\text{det}(A^{-1}) = 1/\text{det}(A) = 1/5$.

$\text{det}(A^2) = (\text{det}(A))^2 = 25$.

$\text{det}(A^T) = \text{det}(A) = 5$.

$\text{det}(3A) = 3^4 \text{det}(A) = 81 \cdot 5 = 405$.

Problem 3: True or False: If $A$ and $B$ are $n \times n$ matrices with $\text{det}(A) = 2$ and $\text{det}(B) = 3$, then $\text{det}(A+B) = 5$. Explain.

Hint: The determinant is NOT a linear function. Test with a simple $2 \times 2$ example.

Solution

False. The determinant is not additive. For example, $A = I$ and $B = I$ both have determinant 1, but $A+B = 2I$ has determinant $2^n$, not 2. For $2 \times 2$: $\text{det}(I) = 1$, but $\text{det}(2I) = 4 \neq 2$.

Problem 4: For what value of $t$ is $A = \begin{bmatrix} t & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & t & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & t \end{bmatrix}$ singular? Use the determinant to find $t$.

Hint: Compute the determinant by cofactor expansion along the first row or by elimination.

Solution

Expand along row 1: $\text{det}(A) = t \cdot \text{det}\begin{bmatrix} t & 1 \\ 1 & t \end{bmatrix} - 1 \cdot \text{det}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & t \end{bmatrix} = t(t^2 - 1) - 1(t) = t^3 - 2t$.

Set to zero: $t(t^2 - 2) = 0$, so $t = 0$ or $t = \pm\sqrt{2}$.

A is singular for $t = 0, \sqrt{2}, -\sqrt{2}$.
