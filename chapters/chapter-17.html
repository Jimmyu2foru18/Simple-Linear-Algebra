Detailed Topic Breakdowns

Orthogonal and Orthonormal Vectors

Two vectors $\mathbf{u}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are orthogonal if $$\mathbf{u}^T\mathbf{v} = 0.$$

They are orthonormal if they are orthogonal and each has unit length: $$\|\mathbf{u}\| = \|\mathbf{v}\| = 1.$$

A set of vectors $\{\mathbf{q}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{q}_n\}$ is orthonormal if $$\mathbf{q}_i^T\mathbf{q}_j = \delta_{ij}$$ (Kronecker delta: 1 if $i=j$, 0 otherwise).

Orthogonal Matrices

A square matrix $Q$ is orthogonal if its columns are orthonormal. This means $$Q^T Q = I.$$

Equivalently, $$Q^{-1} = Q^T.$$

Orthogonal matrices preserve lengths and angles: $$|Q\mathbf{x}| = |\mathbf{x}|$$ for all $\mathbf{x}$.

They represent rotations and reflections in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

The determinant of an orthogonal matrix is either $+1$ (rotation) or $-1$ (reflection).

The Gram-Schmidt Process

Given linearly independent vectors $\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_n$, Gram-Schmidt constructs orthonormal vectors $\mathbf{q}_1, \mathbf{q}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{q}_n$ spanning the same subspace.

The process proceeds sequentially: at step $j$, subtract from $\mathbf{a}_j$ its projection onto each previously constructed orthogonal vector.

The result is orthogonal to all previous vectors.

Then normalize to get $\mathbf{q}_j$.

The QR Factorization

Any matrix $A$ with linearly independent columns can be factored as $$A = QR,$$ where $Q$ has orthonormal columns and $R$ is upper triangular.

The entries of $R$ are inner products: $$r_{ij} = \mathbf{q}_i^T\mathbf{a}_j.$$

Because each $\mathbf{q}_i$ is orthogonal to all $\mathbf{q}_j$ with $j > i$, the lower triangle of $R$ is zero.

QR factorization is the key to many numerical algorithms, including solving least squares problems.

Computation Examples

Derivation: The Gram-Schmidt Formulas

Given independent vectors $\mathbf{a}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_n$:

$$\mathbf{A}_1 = \mathbf{a}_1, \quad \mathbf{q}_1 = \frac{\mathbf{A}_1}{\|\mathbf{A}_1\|}$$ $$\mathbf{A}_2 = \mathbf{a}_2 - (\mathbf{q}_1^T\mathbf{a}_2)\mathbf{q}_1, \quad \mathbf{q}_2 = \frac{\mathbf{A}_2}{\|\mathbf{A}_2\|}$$ $$\mathbf{A}_3 = \mathbf{a}_3 - (\mathbf{q}_1^T\mathbf{a}_3)\mathbf{q}_1 - (\mathbf{q}_2^T\mathbf{a}_3)\mathbf{q}_2, \quad \mathbf{q}_3 = \frac{\mathbf{A}_3}{\|\mathbf{A}_3\|}$$

In general:

$$\mathbf{A}_j = \mathbf{a}_j - \sum_{i=1}^{j-1} (\mathbf{q}_i^T\mathbf{a}_j)\mathbf{q}_i, \quad \mathbf{q}_j = \frac{\mathbf{A}_j}{\|\mathbf{A}_j\|}$$

Derivation: QR Factorization from Gram-Schmidt

Each original vector $\mathbf{a}_j$ can be written in terms of the orthonormal basis:

$$\mathbf{a}_j = \sum_{i=1}^{j} (\mathbf{q}_i^T\mathbf{a}_j)\mathbf{q}_i = \sum_{i=1}^{j} r_{ij}\mathbf{q}_i$$

where $r_{ij} = \mathbf{q}_i^T\mathbf{a}_j$. In matrix form:

$$A = [\mathbf{a}_1 \; \cdots \; \mathbf{a}_n] = [\mathbf{q}_1 \; \cdots \; \mathbf{q}_n] \begin{bmatrix} r_{11} & r_{12} & \cdots & r_{1n} \\ 0 & r_{22} & \cdots & r_{2n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & r_{nn} \end{bmatrix} = QR$$

Derivation: Properties of Orthogonal Matrices

If $Q$ is orthogonal ($Q^T Q = I$):

$$Q^T Q = I \implies Q^{-1} = Q^T$$ $$|Q\mathbf{x}|^2 = \mathbf{x}^T Q^T Q \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{x} = |\mathbf{x}|^2 \implies |Q\mathbf{x}| = |\mathbf{x}|$$ $$\det(Q^T Q) = \det(I) = 1 \implies (\det Q)^2 = 1 \implies \det Q = \pm 1$$

Orthogonal matrices preserve lengths and angles. They are the rotations and reflections of $n$-dimensional space.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "Orthogonal: Perpendicular, Orthonormal: Perpendicular Unit Length"

Orthogonal vectors have dot product zero. Orthonormal vectors have dot product zero AND length one. The extra "n" in orthonormal stands for "normalized" (unit length).

Mnemonic: "Gram-Schmidt: Subtract Projections One by One"

Start with the first vector unchanged. For each subsequent vector, subtract its projection onto all previous orthogonal vectors. The result is orthogonal to everything before it. Then normalize.

Mnemonic: "QR: Q is Orthonormal, R is Upper Triangular"

In $A = QR$, $Q$ has orthonormal columns ($Q^T Q = I$) and $R$ is upper triangular. The entries of $R$ are inner products: $r_{ij} = \mathbf{q}_i^T\mathbf{a}_j$. Because $\mathbf{q}_i$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf{q}_j$ for $j < i$, the lower triangle of $R$ is zero.

Study Tips

  • Practice the Gram-Schmidt process with 2 and 3 vectors until the pattern is automatic
  • Always verify orthogonality by computing dot products after each step
  • Check that $Q^T Q = I$ after constructing $Q$
  • Remember: $R = Q^T A$ when $Q$ has orthonormal columns
  • The key insight: Gram-Schmidt subtracts projections to make vectors orthogonal
  • Orthogonal matrices satisfy $Q^T = Q^{-1}$, use this to check your work

Visual Aids

Figure: Gram-Schmidt as Successive Projection Removal

Starting with independent vectors $\mathbf{a}, \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{c}$, Gram-Schmidt subtracts projections step by step:

$$\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{a}$$ $$\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{b} - \frac{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{a} \quad (\text{remove projection of } \mathbf{b} \text{ onto } \mathbf{A})$$ $$\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{c} - \frac{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{a} - \frac{\mathbf{b}^T\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{b}^T\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{b} \quad (\text{remove projections of } \mathbf{c} \text{ onto } \mathbf{A} \text{ and } \mathbf{B})$$

Each new vector is orthogonal to all previous ones.

Figure: The QR Factorization Geometry

The matrix $A$ has columns $\mathbf{a}_1, \mathbf{a}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{a}_n$. The matrix $Q$ has orthonormal columns $\mathbf{q}_1, \mathbf{q}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{q}_n$ spanning the same column space. The upper triangular matrix $R$ encodes the coefficients:

$$A = QR \implies \mathbf{a}_j = \sum_{i=1}^{j} r_{ij}\mathbf{q}_i$$

Because each $\mathbf{q}_j$ is orthogonal to all $\mathbf{q}_i$ with $i < j$, the coefficients $r_{ij}$ for $i > j$ are zero.

Figure: Orthogonal Matrix as Rotation/Reflection

An orthogonal matrix $Q$ with $\det(Q) = +1$ is a rotation. An orthogonal matrix with $\det(Q) = -1$ is a reflection. Both preserve lengths and angles: $|Q\mathbf{x}| = |\mathbf{x}|$ for all $\mathbf{x}$.

$$Q^T Q = I \implies |Q\mathbf{x}|^2 = \mathbf{x}^T Q^T Q \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{x} = |\mathbf{x}|^2$$

Worked Examples

Example 1: Gram-Schmidt with Two Vectors

Problem: Apply Gram-Schmidt to $\mathbf{a} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solution:

  1. $$\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b} = 1(1) + 1(0) + 1(2) = 3$$
  2. $$\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a} = 1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2 = 3$$
  3. $$\mathbf{B} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{3}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
  4. Verify: $$\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{B} = 1(0) + 1(-1) + 1(1) = 0$$ ✓
  5. Normalize: $$\mathbf{q}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{q}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Example 2: QR Factorization

Problem: Find $Q$ and $R$ such that $A = QR$ for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solution:

  1. From Example 1: $$\mathbf{q}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{q}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$
  2. $R = Q^T A$ (since $Q^T Q = I$)
  3. $$r_{11} = \mathbf{q}_1^T\mathbf{a}_1 = \sqrt{3}, \quad r_{12} = \mathbf{q}_1^T\mathbf{a}_2 = \sqrt{3}$$
  4. $$r_{22} = \mathbf{q}_2^T\mathbf{a}_2 = \sqrt{2}$$
  5. $$R = \begin{bmatrix} \sqrt{3} & \sqrt{3} \\ 0 & \sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}$$
  6. Verify: $QR = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = A$ ✓

Example 3: Orthogonal Matrix Verification

Problem: Show that $Q = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$ is orthogonal.

Solution:

  • $Q^T = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$
  • $$Q^T Q = \begin{bmatrix} \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta & -\cos\theta\sin\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta \\ -\sin\theta\cos\theta + \cos\theta\sin\theta & \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I$$
  • Columns are orthonormal: $| \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta \\ \sin\theta \end{bmatrix} | = 1$, $| \begin{bmatrix} -\sin\theta \\ \cos\theta \end{bmatrix} | = 1$, dot product $= 0$
  • $\det(Q) = \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$ (rotation matrix)

Practice Problems

Problem Set 17

Problem 1: Apply Gram-Schmidt to $\mathbf{a} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ to find orthonormal $\mathbf{q}_1, \mathbf{q}_2$.

Hint: Compute projections and normalize.

Solution

$$\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a} = 5, \quad \mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b} = 4$$

$$\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{b} - \frac{4}{5}\mathbf{a} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 8/5 \\ 4/5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2/5 \\ 1/5 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\|\mathbf{B}\| = \sqrt{4/25 + 1/25} = \sqrt{5}/5 = 1/\sqrt{5}$$

$$\mathbf{q}_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{q}_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Problem 2: Given three independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$, write the full Gram-Schmidt formula for the third orthogonal vector $\mathbf{C}$.

Hint: Subtract projections onto both $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$.

Solution

$$\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{c} - \frac{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{a} - \frac{\mathbf{b}^T\mathbf{c}}{\mathbf{b}^T\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{b}$$

Problem 3: Prove that if $A$ has independent columns, then $R$ in the QR factorization $A = QR$ is invertible.

Hint: Use the fact that $Q$ has orthonormal columns and $Q^T Q = I$.

Solution

Since $A = QR$ and $A$ has independent columns, $A$ is invertible. Also $Q^T Q = I$, so $R = Q^T A$ is a product of invertible matrices, hence invertible.

Problem 4: Let $Q$ be an orthogonal matrix. Show that $|Q\mathbf{x}| = |\mathbf{x}|$ for any $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n$.

Hint: Compute $|Q\mathbf{x}|^2 = (Q\mathbf{x})^T(Q\mathbf{x})$ and use $Q^T Q = I$.

Solution

$$|Q\mathbf{x}|^2 = (Q\mathbf{x})^T(Q\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{x}^T Q^T Q \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{x} = |\mathbf{x}|^2$$

Therefore $|Q\mathbf{x}| = |\mathbf{x}|$. Orthogonal matrices preserve lengths.
