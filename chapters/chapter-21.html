Chapter 21: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Introduction to eigenvalues and eigenvectors, the fundamental building blocks of linear algebra. This chapter explores how adding scalar multiples of the identity shifts eigenvalues, why eigenvalues of A plus B are not simply the sum of eigenvalues, how rotation matrices produce complex eigenvalues, and how repeated eigenvalues can lead to defective matrices with insufficient eigenvectors.

Detailed Topic Breakdowns

Shifting Eigenvalues by Adding cI

If A has eigenvalue lambda with eigenvector x, then A + cI has eigenvalue lambda + c with the same eigenvector x. This is because (A + cI)x = Ax + cIx = lambda x + c x = (lambda + c)x. The eigenvectors are unchanged; only the eigenvalues shift. For example, if A has eigenvalues 1 and -1, then A + 3I has eigenvalues 4 and 2. This property is special to adding a multiple of the identity matrix.

Why A + B Does Not Simply Add Eigenvalues

If Ax = lambda x and Bx = alpha x for the same vector x, then (A+B)x = (lambda + alpha)x. But in general, A and B have different eigenvectors. The equation Bx = alpha x holds only for specific eigenvectors of B, not for eigenvectors of A. Without a common eigenvector basis, there is no way to determine the eigenvalues of A + B from the eigenvalues of A and B alone. Eigenvalues are not linear: knowing det(A) and det(B) does not tell you det(A+B).

Rotation Matrices and Complex Eigenvalues

The 90-degree rotation matrix Q = [[0, -1], [1, 0]] is orthogonal but not symmetric. Its characteristic equation is det(Q - lambda I) = lambda^2 + 1 = 0, giving eigenvalues lambda = i and lambda = -i. These are purely imaginary and form a complex conjugate pair. A real matrix can have complex eigenvalues when it is not symmetric. Symmetric matrices always have real eigenvalues; antisymmetric matrices like Q have purely imaginary eigenvalues.

Defective Matrices and Repeated Eigenvalues

For a triangular matrix, the eigenvalues are exactly the diagonal entries. Consider A = [[3, 3], [0, 3]]. Its eigenvalues are both 3 (repeated). However, solving (A - 3I)x = 0 gives [[0, 3], [0, 0]]x = 0, which has only one independent eigenvector [1, 0]^T. A repeated eigenvalue does not guarantee multiple independent eigenvectors. Such a matrix is called defective. The number of independent eigenvectors can be less than the algebraic multiplicity of the eigenvalue.

Trace and Determinant as Eigenvalue Summaries

The trace of A equals the sum of its eigenvalues: tr(A) = lambda_1 + lambda_2 + ... + lambda_n. The determinant of A equals the product of its eigenvalues: det(A) = lambda_1 * lambda_2 * ... * lambda_n. These relationships hold for any square matrix and provide quick checks. For the rotation matrix Q, trace = 0 (consistent with eigenvalues i and -i summing to 0), and det(Q) = 1 (consistent with i * (-i) = 1).

Computation Examples

Derivation: Shift Property for Eigenvalues

Suppose Ax = lambda x with x != 0. Consider the matrix A + cI:

$$ (A + cI)x = Ax + cIx = \lambda x + c x = (\lambda + c)x $$

Since x != 0, lambda + c is an eigenvalue of A + cI with the same eigenvector x. Thus adding cI shifts every eigenvalue by c without changing the eigenvectors.

Derivation: Why Eigenvalues Are Not Linear

Suppose A has eigenvalues lambda_i with eigenvectors x_i, and B has eigenvalues alpha_j with eigenvectors y_j. If eigenvalues were linear, we would have det((A+B) - (lambda+alpha)I) = 0 whenever det(A - lambda I) = 0 and det(B - alpha I) = 0. But this fails because:

$$ \det(A + B - (\lambda + \alpha)I) \neq \det(A - \lambda I) + \det(B - \alpha I) $$

The determinant is not an additive function. Moreover, the eigenvectors x_i and y_j are generally different, so there is no common basis in which A and B are simultaneously diagonal. Only when A and B share a full set of eigenvectors (i.e., they commute and are simultaneously diagonalizable) can eigenvalues be combined.

Derivation: Complex Eigenvalues of a Rotation Matrix

For the 90-degree rotation matrix Q = [[0, -1], [1, 0]], the characteristic equation is:

$$ \det(Q - \lambda I) = \det\begin{pmatrix} -\lambda & -1 \\ 1 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 + 1 = 0 $$

The solutions are lambda = i and lambda = -i. These are complex conjugates. Since Q is real and orthogonal (Q^T Q = I), its eigenvalues must satisfy |lambda| = 1, which they do: |i| = 1. The eigenvectors are also complex: for lambda = i, solve [[-i, -1], [1, -i]]x = 0, giving x = [1, -i]^T up to scaling.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "Add cI, Shift Eigenvalues, Keep Eigenvectors"

The matrix A + cI has eigenvalues lambda + c and the same eigenvectors as A. Only the identity matrix has this special property.

Mnemonic: "Eigenvalues Are Not Linear"

Knowing eigenvalues of A and B does not give eigenvalues of A + B or AB. Eigenvectors must match, and they usually do not. Only for simultaneous diagonalizable matrices can you combine eigenvalues.

Mnemonic: "Symmetric Gives Real, Antisymmetric Gives Imaginary"

Symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues. Antisymmetric (Q^T = -Q) matrices have purely imaginary eigenvalues. Rotation matrices are antisymmetric and orthogonal, giving complex eigenvalues on the unit circle.

Study Tips

  • For triangular matrices, read eigenvalues directly from the diagonal - no determinant calculation needed
  • A repeated eigenvalue may have fewer independent eigenvectors than its multiplicity - always check
  • The trace is the sum of eigenvalues; the determinant is the product - use these as quick sanity checks
  • Rotation matrices are antisymmetric and orthogonal; expect complex eigenvalues on the unit circle
  • To find eigenvectors, solve (A - lambda I)x = 0 for each eigenvalue lambda
  • If A is symmetric, eigenvectors are orthogonal and eigenvalues are real

Visual Aids

Figure: Effect of Adding cI on the Eigenvalue Spectrum

If A has eigenvalues at positions lambda_1, lambda_2 on the complex plane, then A + cI shifts every eigenvalue by c in the real direction. The eigenvectors are unchanged:

$$ A: \quad \lambda_1 = 1, \quad \lambda_2 = -1 $$

$$ A + 3I: \quad \lambda_1 = 4, \quad \lambda_2 = 2 $$

The eigenvectors [1, 1]^T and [1, -1]^T remain the same for both matrices.

Figure: Rotation Matrix Eigenvalues on the Unit Circle

The 90-degree rotation matrix Q = [[0, -1], [1, 0]] rotates every vector by 90 degrees. No real vector can come out parallel to itself after a 90-degree rotation. The eigenvalues are complex:

$$ Q = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \lambda_1 = i, \quad \lambda_2 = -i $$

Both lie on the unit circle in the complex plane. The eigenvectors are also complex: for lambda = i, the eigenvector is [1, -i]^T.

Figure: Defective Matrix with Repeated Eigenvalue

The triangular matrix A = [[3, 3], [0, 3]] has eigenvalue lambda = 3 with algebraic multiplicity 2. However, the geometric multiplicity is only 1:

$$ A - 3I = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \implies \text{nullspace dimension} = 1 $$

Only one independent eigenvector exists: [1, 0]^T. The matrix is defective.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Shifting Eigenvalues

Problem: If A = [[1, 0], [0, -1]] has eigenvalues 1 and -1 with eigenvectors [1, 0]^T and [0, 1]^T, find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of A + 3I.

Solution:

  • A + 3I = [[4, 0], [0, 2]]
  • Eigenvalues: 1 + 3 = 4, -1 + 3 = 2
  • Eigenvectors: unchanged: [1, 0]^T and [0, 1]^T
  • Verification: (A+3I)[1,0]^T = [4,0]^T = 4[1,0]^T, (A+3I)[0,1]^T = [0,2]^T = 2[0,1]^T

Example 2: Complex Eigenvalues of a Rotation Matrix

Problem: Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the 90-degree rotation matrix Q = [[0, -1], [1, 0]].

Solution:

  • Characteristic equation: det(Q - lambda I) = det([[-lambda, -1], [1, -lambda]]) = lambda^2 + 1 = 0
  • Eigenvalues: lambda_1 = i, lambda_2 = -i
  • These are complex conjugates with |lambda| = 1
  • For lambda = i: solve [[-i, -1], [1, -i]]x = 0. From row 2: x_1 - i x_2 = 0, so x_1 = i x_2. Take x_2 = 1, then x = [i, 1]^T, or equivalently [1, -i]^T
  • For lambda = -i: eigenvector is [1, i]^T (complex conjugate of the first)

Example 3: Defective Triangular Matrix

Problem: Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of A = [[3, 3], [0, 3]]. Determine if A is diagonalizable.

Solution:

  • A is triangular, so eigenvalues are the diagonal entries: lambda_1 = 3, lambda_2 = 3
  • Solve (A - 3I)x = 0: [[0, 3], [0, 0]]x = 0
  • This gives 3x_2 = 0, so x_2 = 0, and x_1 is free
  • Eigenvector basis: {[1, 0]^T} - only one independent eigenvector
  • Algebraic multiplicity = 2, geometric multiplicity = 1
  • A is defective and NOT diagonalizable

Practice Problems

Problem Set 21

Problem 1: If A has eigenvalues 2, 5, and -1 with eigenvectors v1, v2, v3, what are the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of A + 4I? Justify using the shift property.

Hint: Adding cI shifts every eigenvalue by c and leaves eigenvectors unchanged.

Solution

Eigenvalues of A + 4I: 2+4=6, 5+4=9, -1+4=3. Eigenvectors remain v1, v2, v3. Verification: (A+4I)v1 = Av1 + 4Iv1 = 2v1 + 4v1 = 6v1.

Problem 2: Explain why knowing the eigenvalues of A and B separately does not allow you to determine the eigenvalues of A + B in general. Use the fact that eigenvectors of A and B are typically different.

Hint: The equation Ax = lambda x holds only for eigenvectors of A. B has its own eigenvectors.

Solution

If x is an eigenvector of A, then Ax = lambda x. But Bx is not generally equal to alpha x for some eigenvalue alpha of B, because x is not an eigenvector of B. The eigenvectors of A and B are typically different vectors. Therefore (A+B)x = lambda x + Bx is not simply (lambda + alpha)x. Eigenvalues are not additive.

Problem 3: Prove that for any triangular matrix, the eigenvalues are exactly the diagonal entries. Then find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of A = [[2, 5, 0], [0, 2, 3], [0, 0, 2]]. Is A diagonalizable?

Hint: The determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of diagonal entries. Set up det(A - lambda I) = 0.

Solution

For triangular A, det(A - lambda I) = (a_11 - lambda)(a_22 - lambda)...(a_nn - lambda). Setting to zero gives eigenvalues equal to diagonal entries: 2, 2, 2. Solve (A - 2I)x = 0: [[0, 5, 0], [0, 0, 3], [0, 0, 0]]x = 0. This gives x_2 = 0, x_3 = 0, x_1 free. Only one independent eigenvector: [1, 0, 0]^T. Algebraic multiplicity 3, geometric multiplicity 1. A is defective and NOT diagonalizable.

Problem 4: Let Q = [[0, -1], [1, 0]] be the 90-degree rotation matrix. Compute its trace and determinant, and verify that they match the sum and product of its eigenvalues.

Hint: The eigenvalues are i and -i. Trace = sum of eigenvalues, det = product of eigenvalues.

Solution

tr(Q) = 0 + 0 = 0. Sum of eigenvalues: i + (-i) = 0. They match. det(Q) = 0*0 - (-1)*1 = 1. Product of eigenvalues: i * (-i) = -i^2 = -(-1) = 1. They match. This confirms the general theorem: trace = sum of eigenvalues, determinant = product of eigenvalues.
