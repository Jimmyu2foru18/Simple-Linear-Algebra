Chapter 15: Projections onto Subspaces

Projecting vectors onto subspaces using projection matrices. This chapter derives the key projection formulas from the normal equations $A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b$, introduces the projection matrix $P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$, and applies these to least squares data fitting.

Detailed Topic Breakdowns

The Normal Equations

To project a vector $\mathbf{b}$ onto the column space of $A$, we seek $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ such that the error $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is perpendicular to the column space of $A$. The condition $A^T\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}$ means the error is in the null space of $A^T$. Multiplying out gives the normal equations: $A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$. This is the central equation of the subject. When $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ has no exact solution, we solve this equation instead to get the best possible $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$.

From Line to Subspace

In 1-D, projecting onto a line spanned by a single vector $\mathbf{a}$, the equation was $\mathbf{a}^T(\mathbf{b} - \hat{x}\mathbf{a}) = 0$, giving $\hat{x} = \frac{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}}$. For a subspace spanned by multiple columns of $A$, the same logic applies. The matrix $A^T A$ is square ($n \times n$ when $A$ is $m \times n$), and $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \mathbf{b}$ is the general formula. The key difference is that we now use matrix inverses instead of scalar division.

The Projection Matrix

The projection $\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ can be written as $\mathbf{p} = P\mathbf{b}$ where $P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$. This matrix $P$ projects any vector $\mathbf{b}$ onto the column space of $A$. It has two fundamental properties: (1) Symmetric: $P^T = P$, and (2) Idempotent: $P^2 = P$. The idempotent property reflects the geometric fact that projecting twice is the same as projecting once — once you are on the column space, a second projection leaves you unchanged.

Why A Must Not Be Square (Usually)

If $A$ were square and invertible, then $A^T A$ could be split as $A^{-1}(A^T)^{-1}$, and $P$ would reduce to the identity matrix $I$. This makes sense: if the column space is all of $\mathbb{R}^m$, projecting onto it does nothing. The interesting case is when $A$ is $m \times n$ with $m > n$ — more equations than unknowns — which is the least squares setting.

Connection to the Four Subspaces

The error vector $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ lies in the null space of $A^T$. This means $\mathbf{e} \perp C(A)$. The projected vector $\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ lies in the column space of $A$. Thus $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{e}$ decomposes into a component in $C(A)$ and a component in $N(A^T)$. These are orthogonal complements in $\mathbb{R}^m$. This is the geometric heart of the projection: we split $\mathbf{b}$ into the part that lives in the column space and the part that lives in the orthogonal complement.

Least Squares and Data Fitting

The most important application is fitting data to a model. Given points $(t_1, b_1), (t_2, b_2), \ldots, (t_m, b_m)$, we seek the best line $b = C + Dt$. This gives an overdetermined system $A\begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{b}$ where $A$ has columns $[1, 1, \ldots, 1]^T$ and $[t_1, t_2, \ldots, t_m]^T$. The normal equations $A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$ yield the best-fit line. The projection matrix $P$ then gives the fitted values $\hat{\mathbf{b}} = P\mathbf{b}$.

Computation Examples

Derivation: The Normal Equations

We want to find $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ minimizing $|\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}|^2$. The minimum occurs when the gradient with respect to $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is zero:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{x}}} |\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}|^2 = \frac{\partial}{\partial \hat{\mathbf{x}}} (\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}})^T(\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = -2A^T(\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \mathbf{0}$$

This gives $A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$. Alternatively, geometrically, the error $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ must be orthogonal to the column space of $A$: $A^T\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}$.

Derivation: The Projection Matrix

Substituting $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \mathbf{b}$ into $\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ gives:

$$\mathbf{p} = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T \mathbf{b}$$

Thus the projection matrix is $P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$.

Symmetry: $P^T = (A^T)^T((A^T A)^{-1})^T A^T = A((A^T A)^T)^{-1} A^T = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T = P$ since $A^T A$ is symmetric.

Idempotence: $P^2 = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T = P$.

Derivation: Projection Matrix in 1-D and n-D

In 1-D, projecting onto a line spanned by vector $\mathbf{a} \in \mathbb{R}^m$:

$$P = \frac{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}}$$

The numerator is an $m \times m$ matrix of rank 1. In n-D, with $A$ having $n$ columns:

$$P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$$

This generalizes the 1-D formula. The denominator $\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}$ (a number) becomes $(A^T A)^{-1}$ (an $n \times n$ matrix), and the outer product $\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T$ becomes $A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "A transpose A, A transpose b"

The normal equations are $A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$. Always remember the order: $A^T A$ on the left side multiplying $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$, and $A^T \mathbf{b}$ on the right. The transpose hits $A$ first, then $A$ again.

Mnemonic: "Projection Matrix: A, inverse, A transpose"

The projection matrix is $P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$. Read it left to right: $A$ on the left, inverse of $A^T A$ in the middle, $A^T$ on the right. The $A$'s bracket the inverse.

Mnemonic: "Symmetric and Squared Equals Itself"

Every projection matrix $P$ satisfies two key properties: $P^T = P$ (symmetric) and $P^2 = P$ (idempotent). Project once and you get $\mathbf{p}$. Project again and you get $\mathbf{p}$ again.

Study Tips

  • Always verify $A^T A$ is invertible before computing $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \mathbf{b}$
  • The projection matrix $P$ maps $\mathbf{b}$ to the closest point in $C(A)$
  • Remember that $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - P\mathbf{b}$ lies in $N(A^T)$ and $\mathbf{p} = P\mathbf{b}$ lies in $C(A)$
  • If $A$ is square and invertible, $P = I$ (projection onto whole space)
  • In least squares, the normal equations are always solvable because $A^T A$ is invertible when $A$ has independent columns
  • The error $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is perpendicular to every column of $A$

Figure: Projection onto a Plane

$$\mathbf{b} = \underbrace{A\hat{\mathbf{x}}}_{\text{projection } \mathbf{p} \in C(A)} + \underbrace{\mathbf{e}}_{\text{error } \in N(A^T)}$$

$\mathbf{e} \perp C(A)$ and $\mathbf{e} \in N(A^T)$. The decomposition is unique.

In 1-D: $P = \frac{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}}$ projects onto the line spanned by $\mathbf{a}$. In n-D: $P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ projects onto the subspace spanned by the columns of $A$.

$$1\text{-D: } P = \frac{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}} \quad (m \times m \text{ matrix of rank 1})$$ $$\text{n-D: } P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T \quad (m \times m \text{ matrix of rank } n)$$

Fitting points $(1,1), (2,2), (3,2)$ to a line $b = C + Dt$:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \hat{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$ $$A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}, \quad A^T \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}$$ $$\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 2/3 \\ 1/2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Best-fit line: $b = \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{2}t$

Worked Examples

Example 1: Projection onto a Line (1-D Review)

Problem: Project $\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$ onto the line spanned by $\mathbf{a} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Find the projection matrix $P$ and verify $P^2 = P$.

Solution:

  • $\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a} = 1 + 1 = 2$, $\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b} = 3 + 4 = 7$
  • $\hat{x} = \frac{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{b}}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}} = \frac{7}{2}$
  • Projection: $\mathbf{p} = \hat{x}\mathbf{a} = \frac{7}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3.5 \\ 3.5 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Projection matrix: $P = \frac{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Verify $P^2 = \frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = P$
  • Error: $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.5 \\ 0.5 \end{bmatrix}$, and $\mathbf{e} \perp \mathbf{a}$ since $\mathbf{e}^T\mathbf{a} = -0.5 + 0.5 = 0$

Example 2: Projection onto a Plane

Problem: Project $\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$ onto the plane spanned by $\mathbf{a}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{a}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Find the projection matrix $P$.

Solution:

  • $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
  • $A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I$, so $(A^T A)^{-1} = I$
  • $P = A I A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Projection: $\mathbf{p} = P\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Error: $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$, which is in $N(A^T)$

Example 3: Least Squares Line Fitting

Problem: Fit the points $(1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 2)$ to a line $b = C + Dt$ using least squares.

Solution:

  • $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$
  • $A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Determinant of $A^T A = 3(14) - 6(6) = 42 - 36 = 6$
  • $(A^T A)^{-1} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 14 & -6 \\ -6 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$
  • $A^T \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}$
  • $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T \mathbf{b} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 14 & -6 \\ -6 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{bmatrix} 70 - 66 \\ -30 + 33 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2/3 \\ 1/2 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Best-fit line: $b = \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{2}t$
  • Fitted values: $\hat{\mathbf{b}} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 7/6 \\ 5/3 \\ 13/6 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 1.167 \\ 1.667 \\ 2.167 \end{bmatrix}$

Example 4: Error Vector in $N(A^T)$

Problem: For the line fitting problem above, compute the error vector $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ and verify $\mathbf{e} \in N(A^T)$.

Solution:

  • $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 7/6 \\ 5/3 \\ 13/6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1/6 \\ 1/3 \\ -1/6 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Since $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ solves the normal equations, $A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$, which means $A^T(\mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \mathbf{0}$
  • Therefore $A^T\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}$, so $\mathbf{e} \in N(A^T)$
  • This confirms the geometric picture: the error vector is perpendicular to the column space of $A$ and lives in the left nullspace
  • Verify: $\mathbf{e}^T\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = -1/6 + 2/6 - 1/6 = 0$ and $\mathbf{e}^T\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = -1/6 + 4/6 - 3/6 = 0$

Practice Problems

Problem Set 15

Problem 1: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Find the projection matrix $P$ that projects any $\mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^2$ onto the line spanned by the column of $A$. Verify that $P^2 = P$.

Hint: Use the 1-D formula $P = \frac{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T}{\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a}}$.

Solution

$\mathbf{a}^T\mathbf{a} = 2$, $\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

$P = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

$P^2 = \frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = P$.

Problem 2: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Find the projection matrix $P$ and describe what subspace it projects onto.

Hint: Compute $A^T A$ and its inverse, then form $P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$.

Solution

$A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I$, so $(A^T A)^{-1} = I$.

$P = A I A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

$P$ projects onto the x-y plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Problem 3: Given $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$, find the least squares solution $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ to $A\hat{\mathbf{x}} \approx \mathbf{b}$.

Hint: Solve $A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$. You computed $A^T A$ and $A^T \mathbf{b}$ in the text.

Solution

$A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}$, $A^T \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solving gives $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2/3 \\ 1/2 \end{bmatrix}$.

The best-fit line is $b = \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{2}t$.

Problem 4: If $A$ is $m \times n$ with independent columns, prove that $A^T A$ is invertible.

Hint: Suppose $A^T A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. Multiply by $\mathbf{x}^T$ on the left and use the fact that $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ if and only if columns are independent.

Solution

If $A^T A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, then $\mathbf{x}^T A^T A \mathbf{x} = 0$, so $(A\mathbf{x})^T(A\mathbf{x}) = 0$, which means $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. Since $A$ has independent columns, $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ implies $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. Thus $A^T A$ has trivial null space and is invertible.

Problem 5: Show that if $P$ is a projection matrix onto subspace $S$, then $I - P$ is the projection matrix onto $S^\perp$.

Hint: Verify that $(I-P)^2 = I-P$ and $(I-P)^T = I-P$. Also check that $(I-P)P\mathbf{b} = 0$ for any $\mathbf{b}$, meaning $I-P$ annihilates vectors in $S$.

Solution

$(I-P)^2 = I - 2P + P^2 = I - 2P + P = I - P$. Also $(I-P)^T = I - P^T = I - P$. For any $\mathbf{b} \in S$, $P\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}$, so $(I-P)\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$. For $\mathbf{b} \perp S$, $P\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{0}$, so $(I-P)\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{b}$. Thus $I-P$ projects onto $S^\perp$.
