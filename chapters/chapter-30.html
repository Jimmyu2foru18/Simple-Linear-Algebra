Chapter 30: Singular Value Decomposition

The Singular Value Decomposition is the most powerful factorization in linear algebra: every real matrix $A$ can be written as $A = U\Sigma V^T$, where $U$ and $V$ have orthonormal columns and $\Sigma$ is diagonal with non-negative entries. The singular values $\sigma_i$ are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^T A$ (or $AA^T$). The SVD simultaneously diagonalizes the row space and column space, providing orthonormal bases for all four fundamental subspaces. For rank-$r$ matrices, the SVD reduces to $A = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i u_i v_i^T$, a sum of rank-1 matrices ordered by importance.

Detailed Topic Breakdowns

The SVD Factorization: $A = U\Sigma V^T$

Every real $m \times n$ matrix $A$ can be factored as $A = U\Sigma V^T$ where $U$ is $m \times m$ orthogonal, $V$ is $n \times n$ orthogonal, and $\Sigma$ is $m \times n$ diagonal with non-negative entries $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots \geq \sigma_r > 0$. The columns of $U$ are the left singular vectors; the columns of $V$ are the right singular vectors; the diagonal entries of $\Sigma$ are the singular values. The number of nonzero singular values equals the rank $r$ of $A$. The SVD exists for every matrix, even rectangular or rank-deficient ones.

Singular Values from $A^T A$

The singular values are the square roots of the eigenvalues of $A^T A$. Since $A^T A$ is symmetric positive semi-definite, its eigenvalues are real and non-negative. Let $A^T A = V\Lambda V^T$ be the eigen-decomposition, where $\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)$ with $\lambda_i \geq 0$. Then $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$. The non-zero singular values are the square roots of the positive eigenvalues. The columns of $V$ are the eigenvectors of $A^T A$. A key fact: the eigenvalues of $A^T A$ and $AA^T$ are the same (including zeros), so the singular values can also be found from $AA^T$.

Computing $U$ from $AA^T$ or from $AV\Sigma^{-1}$

Once $V$ and $\Sigma$ are known, there are two ways to find $U$. Method 1: compute $AA^T = U\Sigma\Sigma^T U^T = U\Lambda' U^T$ and find its eigenvectors. Method 2: use $u_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} A v_i$ for each $i = 1, \ldots, r$. Both methods give the same $U$ for the nonzero singular values. The remaining columns of $U$ (for the left null space) complete the orthonormal basis. The sign of each $u_i$ must match the sign of $A v_i$ to ensure consistency: $A v_i = \sigma_i u_i$.

The SVD and the Four Fundamental Subspaces

The SVD provides the "right" orthonormal bases for all four fundamental subspaces simultaneously. The first $r$ columns of $V$ form an orthonormal basis for the row space $C(A^T)$. The first $r$ columns of $U$ form an orthonormal basis for the column space $C(A)$. The remaining $n-r$ columns of $V$ form an orthonormal basis for the null space $N(A)$. The remaining $m-r$ columns of $U$ form an orthonormal basis for the left null space $N(A^T)$. The relationship $A v_i = \sigma_i u_i$ shows that $A$ maps each right singular vector to the corresponding left singular vector scaled by the singular value. The null space vectors map to zero.

Rank-One Matrices and the Outer Product Form

A rank-one matrix $A$ has only one nonzero singular value $\sigma_1$. The SVD reduces to $A = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T$, an outer product of the column-space unit vector and the row-space unit vector. For example, $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 8 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$ has rank 1. Its row space is spanned by $[4,3]^T$, so $v_1 = \frac{1}{5}[4,3]^T$. Its column space is spanned by $[4,8]^T = 4[1,2]^T$, so $u_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}[1,2]^T$. The singular value is $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{125} = 5\sqrt{5}$. Thus $A = 5\sqrt{5} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}[1,2]^T \cdot \frac{1}{5}[4,3] = [1,2]^T [4,3] = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 8 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$.

Computation Examples

Derivation: SVD of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$

Step 1: Compute $A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 25 & 7 \\ 7 & 25 \end{bmatrix}$. The characteristic equation is $\det(A^T A - \lambda I) = (25-\lambda)^2 - 49 = \lambda^2 - 50\lambda + 576 = 0$. The eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 32$ and $\lambda_2 = 18$. The singular values are $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{32} = 4\sqrt{2}$ and $\sigma_2 = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2}$.

Step 2: Find eigenvectors of $A^T A$. For $\lambda_1 = 32$: solve $\begin{bmatrix} -7 & 7 \\ 7 & -7 \end{bmatrix}x = 0$, giving $x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Normalize: $v_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. For $\lambda_2 = 18$: solve $\begin{bmatrix} 7 & 7 \\ 7 & 7 \end{bmatrix}x = 0$, giving $x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$. Normalize: $v_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$. Thus $V = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step 3: Compute $u_i = \frac{1}{\sigma_i} A v_i$ for each singular value. For $u_1$: $A v_1 = A \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, so $u_1 = \frac{1}{4\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 8 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. For $u_2$: $A v_2 = A \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -6 \end{bmatrix}$, so $u_2 = \frac{1}{3\sqrt{2}} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$. Thus $U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Step 4: Verify $A = U\Sigma V^T$: $U\Sigma V^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4\sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 0 & 3\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}^T = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 4\sqrt{2} & 4\sqrt{2} \\ -3\sqrt{2} & 3\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = A$.

Derivation: SVD of a Rank-One Matrix

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 8 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$, compute $A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 25 & 30 \\ 30 & 36 \end{bmatrix}$. The trace is 61 and the determinant is $25 \cdot 36 - 900 = 900 - 900 = 0$. So the eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 125$ and $\lambda_2 = 0$. The singular values are $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{125} = 5\sqrt{5}$ and $\sigma_2 = 0$.

For $\lambda_1 = 125$: solve $(A^T A - 125I)x = 0$, giving $x_1 = [4,3]^T$. Normalize: $v_1 = \frac{1}{5}[4,3]^T = [0.8, 0.6]^T$. For $\lambda_2 = 0$: the null space of $A^T A$ is spanned by $[3,-4]^T$ (perpendicular to $[4,3]^T$). Normalize: $v_2 = \frac{1}{5}[3,-4]^T = [0.6, -0.8]^T$.

The column space of $A$ is spanned by $[4,8]^T = 4[1,2]^T$, so $u_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}[1,2]^T$. The left null space is spanned by $[2,-1]^T$ (perpendicular to $[1,2]^T$), so $u_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}[2,-1]^T$.

Verify: $A = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T = 5\sqrt{5} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}[1,2]^T \cdot \frac{1}{5}[4,3] = [1,2]^T [4,3] = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 8 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$.

Derivation: Why Eigenvalues of $A^T A$ and $AA^T$ Are the Same

Let $\lambda \neq 0$ be an eigenvalue of $A^T A$ with eigenvector $x$: $A^T A x = \lambda x$. multiply by $A$: $AA^T(Ax) = A(A^T A)x = A(\lambda x) = \lambda(Ax)$. Since $\lambda \neq 0$, we have $Ax \neq 0$, so $Ax$ is an eigenvector of $AA^T$ with eigenvalue $\lambda$. Thus every nonzero eigenvalue of $A^T A$ is an eigenvalue of $AA^T$. The reverse direction is symmetric, and both matrices have the same number of zero eigenvalues (equal to $n - \text{rank}(A)$ and $m - \text{rank}(A)$ respectively). Therefore $A^T A$ and $AA^T$ have the same non-zero eigenvalues, which are the squares of the singular values.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "SVD: U, Sigma, V Transpose"

The order matters: $A = U\Sigma V^T$. $U$ comes from the column space, $V$ from the row space, $\Sigma$ contains the singular values. $V$ is on the right, so it comes from $A^T A$; $U$ is on the left, so it comes from $AA^T$ or $AV\Sigma^{-1}$.

Mnemonic: "Singular Values Are Square Roots of $A^T A$ Eigenvalues"

Compute $A^T A$, find its eigenvalues $\lambda_i$, then $\sigma_i = \sqrt{\lambda_i}$. The non-zero singular values are always real and non-negative. The number of non-zero singular values equals the rank.

Mnemonic: "Four Subspaces, Four Pieces: V Row, U Column, V Null, U Left Null"

The SVD gives orthonormal bases for all four fundamental subspaces: $V$'s first $r$ columns = row space, $U$'s first $r$ columns = column space, $V$'s last $n-r$ columns = null space, $U$'s last $m-r$ columns = left null space. The relationship $Av_i = \sigma_i u_i$ connects them.

Study Tips

  • Always compute $A^T A$ first to get $V$ and the singular values; then get $U$ from $AA^T$ or $AV\Sigma^{-1}$
  • Check that $A^T A$ and $AA^T$ have the same non-zero eigenvalues—this is a key sanity check
  • For rank-$r$ matrices, only the first $r$ singular values are non-zero; the rest are zero
  • The outer product form $A = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i u_i v_i^T$ is useful for understanding the SVD as a sum of rank-1 matrices
  • Sign consistency matters: if $v_i$ is negated, $u_i$ must also be negated to maintain $Av_i = \sigma_i u_i$
  • The SVD works for rectangular matrices where eigendecomposition fails

Visual Aids

Figure: The SVD Factorization

Every matrix $A$ factors as $A = U\Sigma V^T$. The geometry: $V^T$ rotates/reflects the input space, $\Sigma$ stretches along coordinate axes, and $U$ rotates/reflects the output space:

$$\mathbb{R}^n \xrightarrow{V^T} \mathbb{R}^n \xrightarrow{\Sigma} \mathbb{R}^m \xrightarrow{U} \mathbb{R}^m$$

The singular values $\sigma_i$ are the stretching factors. The right singular vectors $v_i$ are the input directions; the left singular vectors $u_i$ are the output directions. Each $v_i$ maps to $\sigma_i u_i$.

Figure: SVD for Full-Rank and Rank-One Matrices

Full-rank case ($A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, rank 2):

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4\sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 0 & 3\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}^T$$

Rank-one case ($A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 8 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$):

$$A = 5\sqrt{5} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \cdot \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 8 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

The rank-one matrix is a single outer product: one column vector times one row vector.

Figure: The Four Fundamental Subspaces via SVD

The SVD provides orthonormal bases for all four subspaces:

$$\begin{array}{c|c|c} \text{Space} & \text{Basis} & \text{Source} \\ \hline \text{Row space } C(A^T) & \{v_1, \ldots, v_r\} & \text{First } r \text{ cols of } V \\ \text{Null space } N(A) & \{v_{r+1}, \ldots, v_n\} & \text{Last } n-r \text{ cols of } V \\ \text{Column space } C(A) & \{u_1, \ldots, u_r\} & \text{First } r \text{ cols of } U \\ \text{Left null space } N(A^T) & \{u_{r+1}, \ldots, u_m\} & \text{Last } m-r \text{ cols of } U \end{array}$$

The key relationship: $A v_i = \sigma_i u_i$ for $i \leq r$, and $A v_j = 0$ for $j > r$.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Full SVD of a 2×2 Matrix

Problem: Find the SVD of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solution:

  • $A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 25 & 7 \\ 7 & 25 \end{bmatrix}$, eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 32, \lambda_2 = 18$
  • Singular values: $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{32} = 4\sqrt{2}$, $\sigma_2 = \sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2}$
  • Eigenvector for $\lambda_1 = 32$: $v_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1,1]^T$
  • Eigenvector for $\lambda_2 = 18$: $v_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1,-1]^T$
  • $V = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • $AA^T = \begin{bmatrix} 32 & 0 \\ 0 & 18 \end{bmatrix}$, eigenvectors are $e_1, e_2$
  • $U = I_2$
  • $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 4\sqrt{2} & 0 \\ 0 & 3\sqrt{2} \end{bmatrix}$
  • Verify: $U\Sigma V^T = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = A$ ✓

Example 2: SVD of a Rank-One Matrix

Problem: Find the SVD of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 8 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solution:

  • Rank $r = 1$. Only one nonzero singular value.
  • $A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 25 & 30 \\ 30 & 36 \end{bmatrix}$, eigenvalues $\lambda_1 = 125, \lambda_2 = 0$
  • $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{125} = 5\sqrt{5}$
  • Row space direction: $v_1 = \frac{1}{5}[4,3]^T = [0.8, 0.6]^T$
  • Null space direction: $v_2 = \frac{1}{5}[3,-4]^T = [0.6, -0.8]^T$
  • Column space direction: $u_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}[1,2]^T$
  • Left null space direction: $u_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}[2,-1]^T$
  • $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 5\sqrt{5} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Verify: $A = \sigma_1 u_1 v_1^T = 5\sqrt{5} \cdot \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}[1,2]^T \cdot \frac{1}{5}[4,3] = [1,2]^T[4,3] = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 3 \\ 8 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$ ✓

Example 3: Using SVD to Understand Matrix Action

Problem: For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 4 \\ -3 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, describe how $A$ transforms the unit circle under the SVD geometry. What are the singular values, and what do they tell you about stretching and compression?

Solution:

  • The SVD says: rotate by $V^T$, stretch by $\Sigma$, rotate by $U$
  • $V^T$ rotates the plane by $45°$ (since $V$ is a rotation/reflection matrix)
  • $\Sigma$ stretches the $x$-axis by $\sigma_1 = 4\sqrt{2} \approx 5.657$ and compresses the $y$-axis by $\sigma_2 = 3\sqrt{2} \approx 4.243$
  • $U = I$ does no further rotation
  • The unit circle becomes an ellipse with semi-axes $4\sqrt{2}$ and $3\sqrt{2}$
  • The ratio $\sigma_1/\sigma_2 \approx 1.333$ is the condition number of $A$
  • The SVD reveals that $A$ stretches most in the direction $v_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1,1]^T$ and compresses most in the direction $v_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1,-1]^T$

Practice Problems

Problem Set 30

Problem 1: Find the SVD of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$ by inspection.

Hint: $A$ is already diagonal. What are the singular values? What are $U$ and $V$?

Solution

Since $A$ is diagonal with entries $3$ and $-4$, the singular values are $\sigma_1 = 4, \sigma_2 = 3$ (ordered largest to smallest). The matrix $A^T A = \text{diag}(9, 16)$ has eigenvectors $e_1, e_2$, so $V = I$. The matrix $AA^T = \text{diag}(9, 16)$ also has eigenvectors $e_1, e_2$, but to maintain $Av_i = \sigma_i u_i$, we need $u_1 = [0,1]^T$ (corresponding to $\sigma_1=4$) and $u_2 = [1,0]^T$ (corresponding to $\sigma_2=3$). Thus $U = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Verify: $U\Sigma V^T = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} I = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ 4 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$... hmm, that's not $A$. Let me reconsider. Actually, for a diagonal matrix with a negative entry, we need to be careful. $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix}$. The SVD should have non-negative $\Sigma$. $A^T A = \text{diag}(9, 16)$, so $\sigma_1 = 4, \sigma_2 = 3$. $V = I$. Then $u_1 = \frac{1}{4}A v_1 = \frac{1}{4}A e_2 = [0, -1]^T$, and $u_2 = \frac{1}{3}A v_2 = \frac{1}{3}A e_1 = [1, 0]^T$. So $U = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$? No, that's not orthogonal. Let me reorder: $v_1 = e_2$ (for $\sigma_1=4$), $v_2 = e_1$ (for $\sigma_2=3$). Then $u_1 = \frac{1}{4}A e_2 = [0, -1]^T$, $u_2 = \frac{1}{3}A e_1 = [1, 0]^T$. So $V = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $U = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$... wait, $U$ needs to be orthogonal. $U = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has columns $[0,-1]^T$ and $[1,0]^T$, which are orthonormal. Yes. $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Verify: $U\Sigma V^T = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 3 \\ -4 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & -4 \end{bmatrix} = A$. ✓

Problem 2: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Compute $A^T A$, find its eigenvalues and eigenvectors, and determine the singular values and the matrix $V$.

Hint: $A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 5 \\ 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$. The eigenvalues are 11 and 1.

Solution

$A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 5 \\ 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$. Trace = 12, det = 36-25 = 11. Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 11, \lambda_2 = 1$. Singular values: $\sigma_1 = \sqrt{11}, \sigma_2 = 1$. For $\lambda_1 = 11$: eigenvector $[1,1]^T$, normalized $v_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1,1]^T$. For $\lambda_2 = 1$: eigenvector $[1,-1]^T$, normalized $v_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1,-1]^T$. $V = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$. To find $U$: $u_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{11}}A v_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{11}}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$, $u_2 = \frac{1}{1}A v_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Normalize $u_1$: length $\sqrt{9+9+4}/\sqrt{11} = \sqrt{22}/\sqrt{11} = \sqrt{2}$, so $u_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{22}}\begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 3 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Complete $U$ with a third orthonormal vector in the left null space.

Problem 3: Show that the SVD of a matrix $A$ is not unique. In what ways can the factors $U$, $\Sigma$, and $V$ vary while still representing the same matrix $A$?

Hint: Think about sign changes in eigenvectors and ordering of singular values. What happens if two singular values are equal?

Solution

The SVD is not unique in several ways: (1) If $\sigma_i \neq \sigma_j$, the columns of $U$ and $V$ corresponding to $\sigma_i$ and $\sigma_j$ can be negated independently: replacing $u_i \to -u_i$ and $v_i \to -v_i$ leaves $u_i v_i^T$ unchanged. (2) If $\sigma_i = \sigma_j$ (repeated singular value), the corresponding $2\times 2$ block in $U$ and $V$ can be rotated by any orthogonal matrix, giving infinitely many SVDs. (3) The order of singular values can be permuted if we simultaneously permute the corresponding columns of $U$ and $V$. The singular values themselves are unique, but the singular vectors are not.

Problem 4: For a rank-$r$ matrix $A$, the SVD gives the outer product expansion $A = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i u_i v_i^T$. Explain why this is the "best" rank-$r$ approximation to $A$ in the least squares sense. State Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem.

Hint: Truncate the SVD after $r$ terms. Show that any other rank-$r$ matrix $B$ has larger error $\|A-B\|$.

Solution

The Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem states that the truncated SVD $A_r = \sum_{i=1}^r \sigma_i u_i v_i^T$ minimizes $\|A - B\|_F$ over all matrices $B$ of rank $r$. The error is $\|A - A_r\|_F = \sqrt{\sigma_{r+1}^2 + \cdots + \sigma_m^2}$. This is optimal because the singular values are ordered $\sigma_1 \geq \sigma_2 \geq \cdots$. Any rank-$r$ approximation must discard at least the smallest $m-r$ singular values, and the Frobenius norm of the error is exactly the sum of squares of the discarded singular values. Intuitively, the SVD orders the information in $A$ by importance: the first term captures the most variance, the second term captures the next most, and so on.
