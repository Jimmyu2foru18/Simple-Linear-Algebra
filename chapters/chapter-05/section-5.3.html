Section 5.3: Diagonalization

Assigned problems: 15, 16, 17. Source: linearAlgebraSolutionsComplete.pdf (PDF Chapter 5, Section 5.3).

What is this section about?

What is diagonalization? A matrix A is diagonalizable if A = PDP⁻¹ where P is invertible and D is diagonal. This requires A to have n linearly independent eigenvectors.

Core Lecture, Gilbert Strang (MIT)

Concept Overview

A matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if there exists an invertible matrix $P$ and a diagonal matrix $D$ such that $A = PDP^{-1}$.

The columns of $P$ are eigenvectors of $A$, and the diagonal entries of $D$ are the corresponding eigenvalues.

Diagonalization Theorem: An $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors.

Worked Problems from linearAlgebraSolutionsComplete.pdf

Problem 15

Problem: Diagonalize $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ if possible.

Solution

Find eigenvalues: $\det(A - \lambda I) = \begin{vmatrix} 4-\lambda & -2 \\ 1 & 1-\lambda \end{vmatrix} = (4-\lambda)(1-\lambda) + 2 = \lambda^2 - 5\lambda + 6 = (\lambda - 2)(\lambda - 3)$

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 2$, $\lambda_2 = 3$

For $\lambda_1 = 2$: $(A - 2I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ gives $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -2 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, so $x_1 - x_2 = 0$. Eigenvector: $\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

For $\lambda_2 = 3$: $(A - 3I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ gives $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, so $x_1 - 2x_2 = 0$. Eigenvector: $\mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$

$$P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad D = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

$A = PDP^{-1}$ where $P = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $D = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Problem 16

Problem: Diagonalize $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ if possible.

Solution

Find eigenvalues: $\det(A - \lambda I) = \begin{vmatrix} 1-\lambda & 1 \\ 0 & 1-\lambda \end{vmatrix} = (1-\lambda)^2$

Eigenvalue: $\lambda = 1$ with multiplicity 2

For $\lambda = 1$: $(A - I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ gives $\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, so $x_2 = 0$. Only one free variable.

The eigenspace is one-dimensional: $\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$

Since we need 2 linearly independent eigenvectors for a $2 \times 2$ matrix but only have 1, $A$ is NOT diagonalizable.

Problem 17

Problem: Diagonalize $A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ -1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ if possible.

Solution

Find eigenvalues: $\det(A - \lambda I) = \begin{vmatrix} 5-\lambda & 2 \\ -1 & 4-\lambda \end{vmatrix} = (5-\lambda)(4-\lambda) + 2 = \lambda^2 - 9\lambda + 22$

Using quadratic formula: $\lambda = \frac{9 \pm \sqrt{81 - 88}}{2} = \frac{9 \pm i\sqrt{7}}{2}$

The eigenvalues are complex numbers, not real. For a real matrix to be diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$, all eigenvalues must be real.

$A$ is NOT diagonalizable over $\mathbb{R}$ (no real eigenvalues).
