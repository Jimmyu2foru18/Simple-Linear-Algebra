Section 5.1: Eigenvectors and Eigenvalues

Assigned problems: 5, 7, 15, 16, 19. Source: linearAlgebraSolutionsComplete.pdf (PDF Chapter 5, Section 5.1).

What is this section about?

What is an eigenvalue? An eigenvector of a matrix A is a nonzero vector x such that Ax = λx for some scalar λ, called the eigenvalue. The set of all eigenvectors for λ (plus 0) is the eigenspace.

Concept Overview

An eigenvector of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is a nonzero vector $\mathbf{x}$ such that $A\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x}$ for some scalar $\lambda$. The scalar $\lambda$ is called an eigenvalue of $A$.

The equation $A\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x}$ can be rewritten as $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. For $\lambda$ to be an eigenvalue, the matrix $(A - \lambda I)$ must be not invertible (i.e., $\det(A - \lambda I) = 0$).

The set of all solutions to $(A - \lambda I)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ is called the eigenspace of $A$ corresponding to $\lambda$.

Problem 5

Problem: Is $A\mathbf{x}$ a multiple of $\mathbf{x}$? Compute whether $\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is an eigenvector of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 7 & 9 \\ 4 & 5 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solution

Compute $A\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 7 & 9 \\ 4 & 5 & 1 \\ 2 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 12+21+9 \\ 16+15+1 \\ 8+12+1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 42 \\ 32 \\ 21 \end{bmatrix}$

Since $\begin{bmatrix} 42 \\ 32 \\ 21 \end{bmatrix} = 0 \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is NOT true (42/4 ≠ 32/3 ≠ 21/1), check more carefully:

$\begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ IS an eigenvector of $A$ for the eigenvalue 0.

Problem 7

Problem: To determine if 4 is an eigenvalue of $A$, decide if the matrix $4I - A$ is invertible. $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 1 \\ 4 & 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solution

$$4I - A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 4 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 1 \\ 4 & 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \\ -4 & 2 & -3 \\ -3 & -4 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Row reducing the augmented matrix for $(4I - A)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 & | & 0 \\ -4 & 2 & -3 & | & 0 \\ -3 & -4 & -1 & | & 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 & | & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & -7 & | & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & | & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

The equation has a nontrivial solution, so 4 IS an eigenvalue. Any nonzero solution of $(4I - A)\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ is a corresponding eigenvector. For example, take $x_3 = 1$, then $x_1 = 1$, $x_2 = 7/2$. An eigenvector is $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 7/2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ or equivalently $\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 7 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$.

Problem 15

Problem: Find a basis for the eigenspace corresponding to $\lambda = 3$ where $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 3 & 2 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solution

$$A - 3I = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 2 & 3 \\ 3 & -1 & 4 \\ 2 & 4 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

Row reducing: $\begin{bmatrix} -2 & 2 & 3 & | & 0 \\ 3 & -1 & 4 & | & 0 \\ 2 & 4 & 3 & | & 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 & | & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & | & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & | & 0 \end{bmatrix}$

Thus $x_1 + 2x_3 = 0$ and $x_2 + x_3 = 0$, so $x_1 = -2x_3$ and $x_2 = -x_3$ with $x_3$ free.

The general solution is:

$$\mathbf{x} = x_3\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

A basis for the eigenspace is $\left\{\begin{bmatrix} -2 \\ -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$.

Problem 16

Problem: Find a basis for the eigenspace corresponding to $\lambda = 4$ where $A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solution

$$A - 4I = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 2 \\ 1 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Row reducing: $\begin{bmatrix} -1 & 0 & 2 & | & 0 \\ 1 & -1 & 0 & | & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & | & 0 \end{bmatrix} \sim \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & -2 & | & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & -2 & | & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & | & 0 \end{bmatrix}$

Thus $x_1 - 2x_3 = 0$ and $x_2 - 2x_3 = 0$, so $x_1 = 2x_3$ and $x_2 = 2x_3$ with $x_3$ free.

The general solution is:

$$\mathbf{x} = x_3\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

A basis for the eigenspace is $\left\{\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}\right\}$.

Problem 19

Problem: The matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ is not invertible because its columns are linearly dependent. Show that 0 is an eigenvalue of $A$.

Solution

Since the columns of $A$ are linearly dependent, $A$ is not invertible, so $\det(A) = 0$.

By the Invertible Matrix Theorem, $A$ is not invertible if and only if $0$ is an eigenvalue of $A$.

Therefore, 0 IS an eigenvalue of $A$.

Eigenvectors for $\lambda = 0$ are solutions of $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. Any nonzero vector whose entries sum to 0 will work. For instance, $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$ are eigenvectors.
