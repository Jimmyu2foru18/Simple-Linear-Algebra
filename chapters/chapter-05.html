Detailed Topic Breakdowns

Matrix Transpose

The transpose of a matrix $A$, denoted $A^T$, is formed by flipping $A$ across its main diagonal. Rows become columns and columns become rows. The $(i, j)$ entry of $A^T$ is the $(j, i)$ entry of $A$.

Properties of Transposes

Key algebraic rules include $(A^T)^T = A$, $(A + B)^T = A^T + B^T$, and $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$. The order reversal in the product rule is essential and often counterintuitive.

Symmetric Matrices

A matrix is symmetric if $A^T = A$. Symmetric matrices have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors. They appear frequently in applications because they represent quadratic forms and covariance matrices.

Permutation Matrices

Permutation matrices are obtained by permuting the rows (or columns) of the identity matrix. They have exactly one '1' in each row and column, with zeros elsewhere. $P^T = P^{-1}$, so permutation matrices are orthogonal.

Vector Spaces and Subspaces

A vector space is a collection of vectors closed under addition and scalar multiplication. $\mathbb{R}^n$ is the $n$-dimensional real vector space. A subspace is a subset that is itself a vector space, requiring closure under linear combinations.

Computation Examples

Derivation: The Four Requirements of a Vector Space

A set $V$ with addition and scalar multiplication is a vector space if it satisfies:

$$1. \text{Closure under addition: } \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} \in V$$ $$2. \text{Closure under scalar multiplication: } c\mathbf{u} \in V$$ $$3. \text{Commutativity: } \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$$ $$4. \text{Existence of zero vector: } \mathbf{0} \in V$$

(plus associativity, distributivity, identity)

For subspaces, only closure properties (1 and 2) plus existence of zero vector need verification.

Derivation: Transpose of a Product

Let $C = AB$. The $(i, j)$ entry of $C^T$ is $c_{ji}$:

$$c_{ji} = \sum_k a_{jk} b_{ki} \quad \text{(entry of } AB\text{)}$$ $$= \sum_k b_{ki} a_{jk} \quad \text{(commute scalars)}$$ $$= \text{entry } (j, i) \text{ of } B^T A^T$$

Therefore $C^T = B^T A^T$.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "Flip Across the Diagonal"

To transpose a matrix, flip it across its main diagonal (top-left to bottom-right). Visualize turning the page of a book, what was on the left is now on the right.

Mnemonic: "Subspace CLIP"

A subset $S$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a subspace if it satisfies: Closure (under addition and scalar multiplication), contains the Left zero vector, is Inherited from $\mathbb{R}^n$, and is Properly closed under linear combinations.

Study Tips

  • Practice transposing matrices of different shapes to internalize the pattern
  • Memorize the key transpose properties, especially $(AB)^T = B^T A^T$
  • Draw permutation matrices for small $n$ to see the pattern
  • Verify subspaces by checking: zero vector, closure under addition, closure under scalar multiplication
  • Common subspaces: lines through origin, planes through origin, whole space, zero subspace

Worked Examples

Example 1: Computing a Transpose

Problem: Find $A^T$ and verify $(A^T)^T = A$.

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

Solution:

$$A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 5 \\ 3 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$$ $$(A^T)^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix} = A \checkmark$$

Example 2: Symmetric Matrix

Problem: Show that $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 3 & 5 & 4 \\ 1 & 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$ is symmetric.

Solution:

$$A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 3 & 5 & 4 \\ 1 & 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix} = A \checkmark$$

Since $A^T = A$, $A$ is symmetric.

Example 3: Verifying a Subspace

Problem: Is the set $S = \{(x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x + y = 0\}$ a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^2$?

Solution:

  • Zero vector: $(0, 0)$ satisfies $0 + 0 = 0$ ✓
  • Closure: If $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ satisfy $x + y = 0$, then $(x_1+x_2) + (y_1+y_2) = (x_1+y_1) + (x_2+y_2) = 0 + 0 = 0$ ✓
  • Scalar multiplication: If $x + y = 0$, then $cx + cy = c(x+y) = c \cdot 0 = 0$ ✓
  • $S$ is a subspace (specifically, a line through the origin)

Practice Problems

Problem Set 5

Problem 1: Compute $(AB)^T$ and verify it equals $B^T A^T$ for:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

Hint: Compute $AB$ first, then transpose both sides.

Solution $$AB = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 9 \\ 0 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad (AB)^T = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 9 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$ $$B^T = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$ $$B^T A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 2 & 3 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 \\ 1 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 2 & 1 \cdot 0 + 4 \cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 9 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \checkmark$$

Problem 2: Find all $3 \times 3$ permutation matrices and determine their inverses.

Hint: There are $3! = 6$ permutation matrices. Each is its own inverse or transpose.

Solution

The 6 permutation matrices correspond to the 6 orderings of $(1,2,3)$. Each satisfies $P^T = P^{-1}$. For transpositions (swaps), $P^2 = I$ so $P^{-1} = P$. For 3-cycles, $P^3 = I$.

Problem 3: Is $S = \{(x, y, z) : x + y + z = 1\}$ a subspace of $\mathbb{R}^3$?

Hint: Check if the zero vector is in $S$.

Solution

No. The zero vector $(0, 0, 0)$ does not satisfy $x + y + z = 1$. Subspaces must contain the zero vector.

Problem 4: Show that the set of all symmetric $n \times n$ matrices forms a subspace of the vector space of all $n \times n$ matrices.

Hint: Check closure under addition and scalar multiplication.

Solution

If $A^T = A$ and $B^T = B$, then $(A+B)^T = A^T + B^T = A + B$. Also $(cA)^T = cA^T = cA$. The zero matrix is symmetric. Thus, symmetric matrices form a subspace.
