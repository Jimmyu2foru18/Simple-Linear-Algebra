Chapter 24: Markov Matrices; Fourier Series

This chapter connects matrix powers to real-world population models through Markov matrices. Then it makes a profound leap from finite-dimensional to infinite-dimensional vector spaces via Fourier series.

You will see how the eigenvalues of a Markov matrix reveal the steady-state distribution of a population. You will see how orthonormal bases make projections trivial. You will see how the same inner-product machinery that works for vectors extends to functions, giving birth to Fourier series and the expansion of arbitrary functions in terms of sines and cosines.

Detailed Topic Breakdowns

Markov Matrices and Population Movement

A Markov matrix describes the movement of populations between states with no gain or loss. Total population is conserved. Each column sums to 1, and all entries are non-negative.

For a 2-state population model with matrix:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix} $$

the columns sum to 1 and all entries are between 0 and 1. The determinant is 0.9 × 0.8 - 0.1 × 0.2 = 0.72 - 0.02 = 0.7, confirming the matrix is invertible and well-behaved. The trace is 0.9 + 0.8 = 1.7, which equals the sum of the eigenvalues.

Eigenvalues of a Markov Matrix

Every Markov matrix has λ = 1 as an eigenvalue. For a column-stochastic matrix, AT has eigenvalue 1 because rows of AT sum to 1.

For the matrix:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix} $$

the eigenvalues are λ1 = 1 and λ2 = 0.7. The characteristic polynomial is λ2 - 1.7λ + 0.7 = (λ - 1)(λ - 0.7) = 0.

The eigenvalue λ = 1 corresponds to the steady state. The eigenvalue λ = 0.7 < 1 produces a term that decays to zero as steps increase.

Eigenvectors and the Steady State

For λ1 = 1, solve (A - I)x = 0:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} -0.1 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & -0.2 \end{bmatrix} x = 0 $$

The null space is spanned by:

$$ x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

For λ2 = 0.7, solve (A - 0.7I)x = 0:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.1 \end{bmatrix} x = 0 $$

The null space is spanned by:

$$ x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

The general solution after k steps is:

$$ u_k = c_1(1)^k \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2(0.7)^k \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

With initial condition u0 = [0, 1000]T: 2c1 + c2 = 0 and c1 - c2 = 1000, giving c1 = 1000/3 and c2 = 2000/3.

The steady state as k → ∞ is:

$$ u_\infty = \frac{1000}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2000/3 \\ 1000/3 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 667 \\ 333 \end{bmatrix} $$

Row Vectors vs Column Vectors

In many textbooks, particularly in electrical engineering and probability, row vectors are used instead of column vectors.

When multiplying from the left (vk+1 = vkA), it is the rows of A that sum to 1 rather than the columns. The transpose AT then becomes the Markov matrix in the column-vector convention.

Both conventions describe the same dynamics; the choice is a matter of taste and convention. The eigenvalues of A and AT are the same, so the long-term behavior is identical regardless of convention.

From Finite to Infinite Dimensions: Fourier Series

The second half of the lecture makes a profound conceptual leap.

In finite dimensions, a vector v is expanded in an orthonormal basis {q1, q2, ..., qn}: v = x1q1 + x2q2 + ... + xnqn. The coefficients are obtained by inner products: xi = qiTv.

Fourier series applies this same idea to function spaces. A periodic function f(x) is expanded in an infinite orthonormal basis {1, cos x, sin x, cos 2x, sin 2x, ...}.

The "inner product" of two functions is an integral over one period:

$$ \langle f, g \rangle = \int_0^{2\pi} f(x)g(x)\,dx $$

The orthogonality of sines and cosines means:

$$ \int_0^{2\pi} \cos(mx)\cos(nx)\,dx = 0 \quad \text{when } m \neq n $$

and similarly for other cross-terms.

This makes Fourier series the natural extension of orthonormal expansion from finite-dimensional vectors to infinite-dimensional functions.

Computing Fourier Coefficients

The Fourier series of a periodic function f(x) with period 2π is:

$$ f(x) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} [a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx)] $$

To find the constant term a0, take the inner product of both sides with the constant function 1:

$$ \int_0^{2\pi} f(x) \cdot 1\,dx = a_0 \int_0^{2\pi} 1\,dx = 2\pi a_0 $$

Thus:

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\,dx $$

which is the average value of f.

For a1, take the inner product with cos x:

$$ \int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos x\,dx = a_1 \int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2 x\,dx = a_1 \cdot \pi $$

Thus:

$$ a_1 = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos x\,dx $$

In general:

$$ a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx $$

and:

$$ b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx $$

These formulas are exact analogues of xi = qiTv in finite dimensions, with the integral replacing the sum.

Computation Examples

Derivation: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of a Markov Matrix

For:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix} $$

the characteristic equation is:

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = \det\begin{bmatrix} 0.9-\lambda & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.8-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (0.9-\lambda)(0.8-\lambda) - 0.02 = 0 $$

Expanding: λ2 - 1.7λ + 0.72 - 0.02 = λ2 - 1.7λ + 0.7 = 0. Factoring: (λ - 1)(λ - 0.7) = 0. The eigenvalues are λ1 = 1 and λ2 = 0.7.

For λ1 = 1, solve (A-I)x = 0: -0.1x1 + 0.1x2 = 0, so x1 = x2, and the eigenvector is:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

For λ2 = 0.7, solve (A-0.7I)x = 0: 0.2x1 + 0.1x2 = 0, so x2 = -2x1, and the eigenvector is:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} $$

Derivation: Solving the Markov Chain and Finding the Steady State

With initial condition:

$$ u_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1000 \end{bmatrix} $$

the general solution is:

$$ u_k = c_1(1)^k \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2(0.7)^k \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \end{bmatrix} $$

At k=0: c1 + c2 = 0 and c1 - 2c2 = 1000. From the first equation, c2 = -c1. Substituting into the second: c1 + 2c1 = 1000, so c1 = 1000/3 and c2 = -1000/3.

As k → ∞, the second term vanishes and:

$$ u_\infty = \frac{1000}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1000/3 \\ 1000/3 \end{bmatrix} $$

Derivation: The Fourier Coefficient Formula from Orthonormality

For an orthonormal basis {q1, q2, ..., qn} in Rn, the expansion v = Σxiqi gives xi = qiTv by orthonormality.

For Fourier series, the basis functions on [0, 2π] are {1, cos x, sin x, cos 2x, sin 2x, ...}. The inner product is:

$$ \langle f, g \rangle = \int_0^{2\pi} f(x)g(x)\,dx $$

The normalization constants are ⟨1, 1⟩ = 2π, ⟨cos nx, cos nx⟩ = π, and ⟨sin nx, sin nx⟩ = π for n ≥ 1.

Taking the inner product of f(x) = a0 + Σ[an cos nx + bn sin nx] with cos mx:

$$ \int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos(mx)\,dx = a_m \int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2(mx)\,dx = a_m \cdot \pi $$

All other terms vanish by orthogonality. Thus:

$$ a_m = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos(mx)\,dx $$

Similarly:

$$ b_m = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\sin(mx)\,dx $$

and:

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\,dx $$

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "Markov: Columns Sum to One, Eigenvalue One"

Every Markov matrix has eigenvalue λ = 1. The corresponding eigenvector (steady state) has positive entries that sum to the total population. The other eigenvalues have |λ| < 1, so their contributions decay to zero.

Mnemonic: "Steady State = c₁x₁ Where c₁ Is Fixed by Total Population"

The steady state is c1x1 where x1 is the λ = 1 eigenvector. The constant c1 is determined by requiring that the components of c1x1 sum to the initial total population.

Mnemonic: "Fourier: Integral Replaces Sum, Functions Replace Vectors"

In Fourier series, the dot product vTw = Σviwi becomes ∫f(x)g(x) dx. The expansion coefficient xi = qiTv becomes:

$$ a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx $$

The orthonormal basis {qi} becomes {1, cos x, sin x, cos 2x, sin 2x, ...}.

Visual Aids

Figure: Markov Chain Population Dynamics

For:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix} $$

with:

$$ u_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1000 \end{bmatrix} $$

$$ u_k = \frac{1000}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{2000}{3}(0.7)^k\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

The first term (steady state) persists forever. The second term decays by factor 0.7k. After 10 steps, 0.710 ≈ 0.028, so the system is 97.3% of the way to equilibrium.

Figure: Eigenvalue Spectrum of a Markov Matrix

The eigenvalues of a Markov matrix always lie inside or on the unit circle in the complex plane:

$$ |\lambda_1| = 1 \quad (\text{steady state}) $$

$$ |\lambda_2| = 0.7 < 1 \quad (\text{decaying mode}) $$

If any eigenvalue had |λ| > 1, the Markov matrix would not conserve total population. The second eigenvalue determines the convergence rate: |λ2|k is the fraction of the initial condition remaining after k steps.

Figure: From Vector Spaces to Function Spaces

The expansion of a vector in an orthonormal basis generalizes directly to Fourier series:

$$ \text{Finite: } v = \sum_{i=1}^n (q_i^T v)\, q_i $$

$$ \text{Infinite: } f(x) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} [a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx)] $$

where:

$$ a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx $$

and:

$$ b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx $$

The dot product becomes an integral; the finite sum becomes an infinite series; the basis vectors become basis functions.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Markov Chain Population Model

Problem: For:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix} $$

with:

$$ u_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1000 \end{bmatrix} $$

find the eigenvalues, eigenvectors, general solution, steady state, and solution after 100 steps.

Solution:

  • Eigenvalues: λ1 = 1, λ2 = 0.7 (from characteristic polynomial λ2 - 1.7λ + 0.7 = 0)
  • Eigenvector for λ1 = 1:
  • $$ x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{null space of } A-I) $$
  • Eigenvector for λ2 = 0.7:
  • $$ x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{null space of } A-0.7I) $$
  • General solution:
  • $$ u_k = c_1(1)^k\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2(0.7)^k\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
  • Initial condition: 2c1 - c2 = 0, c1 + c2 = 1000 ⇒ c1 = 1000/3, c2 = 2000/3
  • Solution:
  • $$ u_k = \frac{1000}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{2000}{3}(0.7)^k\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$
  • Steady state (k → ∞):
  • $$ u_\infty = \begin{bmatrix} 2000/3 \\ 1000/3 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 667 \\ 333 \end{bmatrix} $$
  • After 100 steps: (0.7)100 ≈ 3.2 × 10-16, so the system is essentially at steady state

Example 2: Fourier Coefficient of a Simple Function

Problem: Find the Fourier series of f(x) = x on [-π, π] (period 2π).

Solution:

  • f(x) = x is odd, so a0 = 0 and all an = 0
  • bn:
  • $$ b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x\sin(nx)\,dx = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} x\sin(nx)\,dx $$
  • Integration by parts:
  • $$ \int x\sin(nx)\,dx = -\frac{x}{n}\cos(nx) + \frac{1}{n^2}\sin(nx) $$
  • bn:
  • $$ b_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\left[-\frac{\pi}{n}\cos(n\pi) + 0\right] = \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n} $$
  • Fourier series:
  • $$ f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\sin(nx) $$
  • At x = π/2: π/2 = 2(1 - 1/3 + 1/5 - 1/7 + ...), the Leibniz formula for π

Example 3: Projection onto an Orthonormal Basis in Function Space

Problem: Expand f(x) = 1 + cos x in the Fourier basis {1, cos x, sin x, cos 2x, sin 2x, ...}.

Solution:

  • a0:
  • $$ a_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} (1+\cos x)\,dx = \frac{1}{2\pi}[2\pi + 0] = 1 $$
  • a1:
  • $$ a_1 = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} (1+\cos x)\cos x\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}[\pi + 0] = 1 $$
  • an = 0 for n ≥ 2 (orthogonality of cos nx with cos x when n ≠ 1)
  • bn = 0 for all n (integral of odd function sin nx against even 1+cos x)
  • Fourier series: f(x) = 1 + cos x (exact. Only two terms are needed)
  • This is the function-space analogue of projecting a vector onto an orthonormal basis and finding it lies entirely in the span of the first two basis vectors

Practice Problems

Problem Set 24

Problem 1: For:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix} $$

find the eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and steady state. Start with:

$$ u_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 500 \\ 500 \end{bmatrix} $$

Hint: The matrix is Markov (columns sum to 1), so λ = 1 is an eigenvalue. Find the other eigenvalue from the trace.

Solution

Eigenvalues: λ1 = 1, λ2 = 0.7. Eigenvectors:

$$ x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Initial condition: 2c1 - c2 = 500, c1 + c2 = 500 ⇒ c1 = 500, c2 = 0.

Solution:

$$ u_k = 500\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 0 \cdot (0.7)^k\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1000 \\ 500 \end{bmatrix} $$

Steady state:

$$ \begin{bmatrix} 1000 \\ 500 \end{bmatrix} \quad (\text{total 1500}) $$

Problem 2: Let:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix} $$

be a Markov matrix. Find its eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and describe what happens to any initial population u0.

Hint: Compute the determinant and trace. One eigenvalue is 1. What is the other?

Solution

Trace = 1, determinant = 0.25 - 0.25 = 0. Eigenvalues: λ1 = 1, λ2 = 0. Eigenvector for λ = 1:

$$ x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Eigenvector for λ = 0:

$$ x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

Solution:

$$ u_k = c_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 0 \cdot c_2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} $$

The second term vanishes immediately. The steady state is c1[1, 1]T where c1 is half the total population. Every initial condition converges to equal population in both states in one step.

Problem 3: Find the Fourier series of f(x) = |sin x| on [-π, π].

Hint: f(x) is even, so all bn = 0. Compute a0 and an using ∫0π sin x cos(nx) dx.

Solution

f(x) = |sin x| is even and 2π-periodic. All bn = 0.

a0:

$$ a_0 = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} \sin x\,dx = \frac{2}{\pi} $$

For n ≥ 1:

$$ a_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} \sin x \cos(nx)\,dx $$

Using sin x cos(nx) = ½[sin((1+n)x) + sin((1-n)x)], integrate:

$$ a_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \cdot \frac{1}{1-n^2}[(-1)^n - (-1)] $$

For even n: an = 0. For odd n:

$$ a_n = \frac{4}{\pi(1-n^2)} $$

Fourier series:

$$ f(x) = \frac{2}{\pi} - \frac{4}{3\pi}\cos(2x) - \frac{4}{15\pi}\cos(4x) - \cdots $$

Problem 4: Explain why the rows of a Markov matrix sum to 1 in the row-vector convention, and show that the eigenvalues of A and AT are the same.

Hint: For the row-vector convention vk+1 = vkA, the total population is vk+1[1,1]T. Use the fact that det(A - λI) = det(AT - λI).

Solution

In the row-vector convention, vk+1 = vkA. If v is a row vector of all ones, then vA = v means each entry of vA is the sum of a row of A. For vA = v to hold, each row must sum to 1. This is the row-stochastic condition.

For eigenvalues:

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = \det((A - \lambda I)^T) = \det(A^T - \lambda I) $$

Since the characteristic polynomials of A and AT are identical, they have the same eigenvalues (though eigenvectors may differ).
