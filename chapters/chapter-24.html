Detailed Topic Breakdowns

Markov Matrices and Population Movement

A Markov matrix describes the movement of populations between states with no gain or lossâ€”total population is conserved. Each column sums to 1, and all entries are non-negative. For a 2-state population model with matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix}$, the columns sum to 1 and all entries are between 0 and 1. The determinant is $0.9 \times 0.8 - 0.1 \times 0.2 = 0.72 - 0.02 = 0.7$, confirming the matrix is invertible and well-behaved. The trace is $0.9 + 0.8 = 1.7$, which equals the sum of the eigenvalues.

Eigenvalues of a Markov Matrix

Every Markov matrix has $\lambda = 1$ as an eigenvalue. This is because $A$ is singular when you subtract $I$? Noâ€”actually, for a column-stochastic matrix, $A^T$ has eigenvalue 1 (since rows of $A^T$ sum to 1, so $A^T[1,1]^T = [1,1]^T$). For the matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix}$, the eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 = 0.7$. The characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2 - 1.7\lambda + 0.7 = (\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 0.7) = 0$. The eigenvalue $\lambda = 1$ corresponds to the steady state, while $\lambda = 0.7 < 1$ produces a term that decays to zero as steps increase.

Eigenvectors and the Steady State

For $\lambda_1 = 1$, solve $(A - I)x = 0$: $\begin{bmatrix} -0.1 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & -0.2 \end{bmatrix} x = 0$. The null space is spanned by $x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. For $\lambda_2 = 0.7$, solve $(A - 0.7I)x = 0$: $\begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.1 \end{bmatrix} x = 0$. The null space is spanned by $x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$. The general solution after $k$ steps is $u_k = c_1(1)^k \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2(0.7)^k \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$. With initial condition $u_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1000 \end{bmatrix}$: $2c_1 + c_2 = 0$ and $c_1 - c_2 = 1000$, giving $c_1 = 1000/3$ and $c_2 = 2000/3$. The steady state as $k \to \infty$ is $u_\infty = \frac{1000}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2000/3 \\ 1000/3 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 667 \\ 333 \end{bmatrix}$.

Row Vectors vs Column Vectors

In many textbooks, particularly in electrical engineering and probability, row vectors are used instead of column vectors. When multiplying from the left ($v_{k+1} = v_k A$), it is the rows of $A$ that sum to 1 rather than the columns. The transpose $A^T$ then becomes the Markov matrix in the column-vector convention. Both conventions describe the same dynamics; the choice is a matter of taste and convention. The eigenvalues of $A$ and $A^T$ are the same, so the long-term behavior is identical regardless of convention.

From Finite to Infinite Dimensions: Fourier Series

The second half of the lecture makes a profound conceptual leap. In finite dimensions, a vector $v$ is expanded in an orthonormal basis $\{q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_n\}$: $v = x_1 q_1 + x_2 q_2 + \cdots + x_n q_n$. The coefficients are obtained by inner products: $x_i = q_i^T v$. Fourier series applies this same idea to function spaces. A periodic function $f(x)$ is expanded in an infinite orthonormal basis $\{1, \cos x, \sin x, \cos 2x, \sin 2x, \ldots\}$. The "inner product" of two functions is an integral over one period: $\langle f, g \rangle = \int_0^{2\pi} f(x)g(x)\,dx$. The orthogonality of sines and cosines means $\int_0^{2\pi} \cos(mx)\cos(nx)\,dx = 0$ when $m \neq n$, and similarly for other cross-terms. This makes Fourier series the natural extension of orthonormal expansion from finite-dimensional vectors to infinite-dimensional functions.

Computing Fourier Coefficients

The Fourier series of a periodic function $f(x)$ with period $2\pi$ is $f(x) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} [a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx)]$. To find the constant term $a_0$, take the inner product of both sides with the constant function 1: $\int_0^{2\pi} f(x) \cdot 1\,dx = a_0 \int_0^{2\pi} 1\,dx = 2\pi a_0$. Thus $a_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\,dx$, which is the average value of $f$. For $a_1$, take the inner product with $\cos x$: $\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos x\,dx = a_1 \int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2 x\,dx = a_1 \cdot \pi$. Thus $a_1 = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos x\,dx$. In general, $a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx$ and $b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx$. These formulas are exact analogues of $x_i = q_i^T v$ in finite dimensions, with the integral replacing the sum.

Computation Examples

Derivation: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of a Markov Matrix

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix}$, the characteristic equation is:

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = \det\begin{bmatrix} 0.9-\lambda & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.8-\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (0.9-\lambda)(0.8-\lambda) - 0.02 = 0$$

Expanding: $\lambda^2 - 1.7\lambda + 0.72 - 0.02 = \lambda^2 - 1.7\lambda + 0.7 = 0$. Factoring: $(\lambda - 1)(\lambda - 0.7) = 0$. The eigenvalues are $\lambda_1 = 1$ and $\lambda_2 = 0.7$. For $\lambda_1 = 1$, solve $(A-I)x = 0$: $-0.1x_1 + 0.1x_2 = 0$, so $x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. For $\lambda_2 = 0.7$, solve $(A-0.7I)x = 0$: $0.2x_1 + 0.1x_2 = 0$, so $x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Derivation: Solving the Markov Chain and Finding the Steady State

With initial condition $u_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1000 \end{bmatrix}$, the general solution is $u_k = c_1(1)^k \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2(0.7)^k \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$. At $k=0$: $2c_1 + c_2 = 0$ and $c_1 - c_2 = 1000$. Adding: $3c_1 = 1000$, so $c_1 = 1000/3$. Then $c_2 = -2c_1 = -2000/3$. Waitâ€”the transcript says $c_2 = +2000/3$ with eigenvector $[1,-1]$, giving $u_k = \frac{1000}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{2000}{3}(0.7)^k\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$. At $k=0$: $\frac{2000}{3} - \frac{2000}{3} = 0$ for the first component, and $\frac{1000}{3} - \frac{2000}{3} = -\frac{1000}{3}$... Hmm, let me recheck. Actually, the transcript says $c_2$ times $[-1, 1]$ or $[1, -1]$ depending on convention. Using $x_2 = [-1, 1]^T$: $2c_1 - c_2 = 0$, $c_1 + c_2 = 1000$, giving $c_1 = 1000/3$, $c_2 = 2000/3$. Then $u_k = \frac{1000}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{2000}{3}(0.7)^k\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. As $k \to \infty$, the second term vanishes and $u_\infty = \begin{bmatrix} 2000/3 \\ 1000/3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Derivation: The Fourier Coefficient Formula from Orthonormality

For an orthonormal basis $\{q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_n\}$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the expansion $v = \sum x_i q_i$ gives $x_i = q_i^T v$ by orthonormality. For Fourier series, the basis functions on $[0, 2\pi]$ are $\{1, \cos x, \sin x, \cos 2x, \sin 2x, \ldots\}$. The inner product is $\langle f, g \rangle = \int_0^{2\pi} f(x)g(x)\,dx$. The normalization constants are $\langle 1, 1 \rangle = 2\pi$, $\langle \cos nx, \cos nx \rangle = \pi$, and $\langle \sin nx, \sin nx \rangle = \pi$ for $n \geq 1$. Taking the inner product of $f(x) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} [a_n \cos nx + b_n \sin nx]$ with $\cos mx$:

$$\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos(mx)\,dx = a_m \int_0^{2\pi} \cos^2(mx)\,dx = a_m \cdot \pi$$

All other terms vanish by orthogonality. Thus $a_m = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos(mx)\,dx$. Similarly, $b_m = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\sin(mx)\,dx$ and $a_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\,dx$.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "Markov: Columns Sum to One, Eigenvalue One"

Every Markov matrix has eigenvalue $\lambda = 1$. The corresponding eigenvector (steady state) has positive entries that sum to the total population. The other eigenvalues have $|\lambda| < 1$, so their contributions decay to zero.

Mnemonic: "Steady State = câ‚xâ‚ Where câ‚ Is Fixed by Total Population"

The steady state is $c_1 x_1$ where $x_1$ is the $\lambda = 1$ eigenvector. The constant $c_1$ is determined by requiring that the components of $c_1 x_1$ sum to the initial total population.

Mnemonic: "Fourier: Integral Replaces Sum, Functions Replace Vectors"

In Fourier series, the dot product $v^T w = \sum v_i w_i$ becomes $\int f(x)g(x)\,dx$. The expansion coefficient $x_i = q_i^T v$ becomes $a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx$. The orthonormal basis $\{q_i\}$ becomes $\{1, \cos x, \sin x, \cos 2x, \sin 2x, \ldots\}$.

Visual Aids

Figure: Markov Chain Population Dynamics

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix}$ with $u_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1000 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$u_k = \frac{1000}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{2000}{3}(0.7)^k\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

The first term (steady state) persists forever. The second term decays by factor $0.7^k$. After 10 steps, $0.7^{10} \approx 0.028$, so the system is 97.3% of the way to equilibrium.

Figure: Eigenvalue Spectrum of a Markov Matrix

The eigenvalues of a Markov matrix always lie inside or on the unit circle in the complex plane:

$$|\lambda_1| = 1 \quad (\text{steady state})$$ $$|\lambda_2| = 0.7 < 1 \quad (\text{decaying mode})$$

If any eigenvalue had $|\lambda| > 1$, the Markov matrix would not conserve total population. The second eigenvalue determines the convergence rate: $|\lambda_2|^k$ is the fraction of the initial condition remaining after $k$ steps.

Figure: From Vector Spaces to Function Spaces

The expansion of a vector in an orthonormal basis generalizes directly to Fourier series:

$$\text{Finite: } v = \sum_{i=1}^n (q_i^T v)\, q_i$$

$$\text{Infinite: } f(x) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} [a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx)]$$

where $a_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\cos(nx)\,dx$ and $b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} f(x)\sin(nx)\,dx$. The dot product becomes an integral; the finite sum becomes an infinite series; the basis vectors become basis functions.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Markov Chain Population Model

Problem: For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix}$ with $u_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1000 \end{bmatrix}$, find the eigenvalues, eigenvectors, general solution, steady state, and solution after 100 steps.

Solution:

  • Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 1$, $\lambda_2 = 0.7$ (from characteristic polynomial $\lambda^2 - 1.7\lambda + 0.7 = 0$)
  • Eigenvector for $\lambda_1 = 1$: $x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (null space of $A-I$)
  • Eigenvector for $\lambda_2 = 0.7$: $x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ (null space of $A-0.7I$)
  • General solution: $u_k = c_1(1)^k\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2(0.7)^k\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Initial condition: $2c_1 - c_2 = 0$, $c_1 + c_2 = 1000 \Rightarrow c_1 = 1000/3$, $c_2 = 2000/3$
  • Solution: $u_k = \frac{1000}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{2000}{3}(0.7)^k\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Steady state ($k \to \infty$): $u_\infty = \begin{bmatrix} 2000/3 \\ 1000/3 \end{bmatrix} \approx \begin{bmatrix} 667 \\ 333 \end{bmatrix}$
  • After 100 steps: $(0.7)^{100} \approx 3.2 \times 10^{-16}$, so the system is essentially at steady state

Example 2: Fourier Coefficient of a Simple Function

Problem: Find the Fourier series of $f(x) = x$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$ (period $2\pi$).

Solution:

  • $f(x) = x$ is odd, so $a_0 = 0$ and all $a_n = 0$
  • $b_n = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi} x\sin(nx)\,dx = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} x\sin(nx)\,dx$
  • Integration by parts: $\int x\sin(nx)\,dx = -\frac{x}{n}\cos(nx) + \frac{1}{n^2}\sin(nx)$
  • $b_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\left[-\frac{\pi}{n}\cos(n\pi) + 0\right] = \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$
  • Fourier series: $f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{2(-1)^{n+1}}{n}\sin(nx)$
  • At $x = \pi/2$: $\pi/2 = 2(1 - 1/3 + 1/5 - 1/7 + \cdots)$, the Leibniz formula for $\pi$

Example 3: Projection onto an Orthonormal Basis in Function Space

Problem: Expand $f(x) = 1 + \cos x$ in the Fourier basis $\{1, \cos x, \sin x, \cos 2x, \sin 2x, \ldots\}$.

Solution:

  • $a_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} (1+\cos x)\,dx = \frac{1}{2\pi}[2\pi + 0] = 1$
  • $a_1 = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} (1+\cos x)\cos x\,dx = \frac{1}{\pi}[\pi + 0] = 1$
  • $a_n = 0$ for $n \geq 2$ (orthogonality of $\cos nx$ with $\cos x$ when $n \neq 1$)
  • $b_n = 0$ for all $n$ (integral of odd function $\sin nx$ against even $1+\cos x$)
  • Fourier series: $f(x) = 1 + \cos x$ (exactâ€”only two terms needed)
  • This is the function-space analogue of projecting a vector onto an orthonormal basis and finding it lies entirely in the span of the first two basis vectors

Practice Problems

Problem Set 24

Problem 1: For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.9 & 0.1 \\ 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix}$, find the eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and steady state. Start with $u_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 500 \\ 500 \end{bmatrix}$.

Hint: The matrix is Markov (columns sum to 1), so $\lambda = 1$ is an eigenvalue. Find the other eigenvalue from the trace.

Solution

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 1$, $\lambda_2 = 0.7$. Eigenvectors: $x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Initial condition: $2c_1 - c_2 = 500$, $c_1 + c_2 = 500 \Rightarrow c_1 = 500$, $c_2 = 0$. Solution: $u_k = 500\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 0 \cdot (0.7)^k\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1000 \\ 500 \end{bmatrix}$. Steady state: $\begin{bmatrix} 1000 \\ 500 \end{bmatrix}$ (total 1500).

Problem 2: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0.5 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.5 \end{bmatrix}$ be a Markov matrix. Find its eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and describe what happens to any initial population $u_0$.

Hint: Compute the determinant and trace. One eigenvalue is 1. What is the other?

Solution

Trace $= 1$, determinant $= 0.25 - 0.25 = 0$. Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 1$, $\lambda_2 = 0$. Eigenvector for $\lambda = 1$: $x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Eigenvector for $\lambda = 0$: $x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$. Solution: $u_k = c_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + 0 \cdot c_2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$. The second term vanishes immediately. The steady state is $c_1\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ where $c_1$ is half the total population. Every initial condition converges to equal population in both states in one step.

Problem 3: Find the Fourier series of $f(x) = |\sin x|$ on $[-\pi, \pi]$.

Hint: $f(x)$ is even, so all $b_n = 0$. Compute $a_0$ and $a_n$ using $\int_0^{\pi} \sin x \cos(nx)\,dx$.

Solution

$f(x) = |\sin x|$ is even and $2\pi$-periodic. All $b_n = 0$. $a_0 = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} \sin x\,dx = \frac{2}{\pi}$. For $n \geq 1$: $a_n = \frac{2}{\pi}\int_0^{\pi} \sin x \cos(nx)\,dx$. Using $\sin x \cos(nx) = \frac{1}{2}[\sin((1+n)x) + \sin((1-n)x)]$, integrate: $a_n = \frac{2}{\pi} \cdot \frac{1}{1-n^2}[(-1)^n - (-1)]$. For even $n$: $a_n = 0$. For odd $n$: $a_n = \frac{4}{\pi(1-n^2)}$. Fourier series: $f(x) = \frac{2}{\pi} - \frac{4}{3\pi}\cos(2x) - \frac{4}{15\pi}\cos(4x) - \cdots$.

Problem 4: Explain why the rows of a Markov matrix sum to 1 in the row-vector convention, and show that the eigenvalues of $A$ and $A^T$ are the same.

Hint: For the row-vector convention $v_{k+1} = v_k A$, the total population is $v_{k+1}[1,1]^T$. Use the fact that $\det(A - \lambda I) = \det(A^T - \lambda I)$.

Solution

In the row-vector convention, $v_{k+1} = v_k A$. If $v$ is a row vector of all ones, then $vA = v$ means each entry of $vA$ is the sum of a row of $A$. For $vA = v$ to hold, each row must sum to 1. This is the row-stochastic condition. For eigenvalues: $\det(A - \lambda I) = \det((A - \lambda I)^T) = \det(A^T - \lambda I)$. Since the characteristic polynomials of $A$ and $A^T$ are identical, they have the same eigenvalues (though eigenvectors may differ).
