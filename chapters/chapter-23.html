Chapter 23: Differential Equations and exp(At)

Solving systems of first-order, constant-coefficient linear differential equations through matrix exponentials. This chapter reveals the beautiful connection between differential equations and linear algebra: the solutions to constant coefficient linear equations are exponentials, and finding those exponentials is exactly the eigenvalue/eigenvector problem. The matrix exponential $e^{At}$ generalizes the scalar exponential $e^{at}$ to matrices, producing solutions of the form $u(t) = e^{At}u(0)$ that describe the evolution of dynamic systems over continuous time.

Detailed Topic Breakdowns

From Difference Equations to Differential Equations

Chapter 22 solved $u_{k+1} = Au_k$ by finding eigenvalues and eigenvectors, giving $u_k = c_1\lambda_1^k x_1 + c_2\lambda_2^k x_2$. Now we solve the continuous version: $\frac{du}{dt} = Au$. The powers $\lambda^k$ become exponentials $e^{\lambda t}$. The formula becomes $u(t) = c_1 e^{\lambda_1 t} x_1 + c_2 e^{\lambda_2 t} x_2$. The structure is identical: eigenvalues control growth or decay, eigenvectors give the directions.

The Key Insight: Solutions Are Exponentials

The central idea is that solutions to constant coefficient linear differential equations are exponentials. If we look for a solution of the form $u(t) = e^{\lambda t} x$, then substituting into $\frac{du}{dt} = Au$ gives $\lambda e^{\lambda t} x = A e^{\lambda t} x$, which simplifies to $Ax = \lambda x$. This is exactly the eigenvalue equation! The exponentials handle the time dependence, and the eigenvalue problem handles the spatial direction. All the linear algebra machinery from Chapter 21 applies directly.

Steady State and Stability

The eigenvalues determine the long-term behavior. A zero eigenvalue ($\lambda = 0$) produces a constant term $e^{0t} = 1$ that never decays—this is the steady state. A negative real eigenvalue ($\lambda < 0$) produces decay $e^{\lambda t} \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$—this gives stability. A positive real eigenvalue ($\lambda > 0$) produces exponential growth—this is instability. For complex eigenvalues $\lambda = \alpha + i\beta$, only the real part $\alpha$ matters for growth/decay: $|e^{\lambda t}| = e^{\alpha t}$. The imaginary part $\beta$ causes oscillation without changing magnitude.

Worked Example: A 2×2 System

Consider $A = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$ with initial condition $u(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. The matrix is singular (column 2 is $-2$ times column 1), so $\lambda_1 = 0$. The trace is $-3$, so $\lambda_2 = -3$. The characteristic polynomial is $\lambda(\lambda+3) = 0$. The eigenvector for $\lambda = 0$ is in the null space of $A$: $x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. For $\lambda = -3$, solve $(A+3I)x = 0$: $x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. The general solution is $u(t) = c_1 e^{0t} \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2 e^{-3t} \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Using $u(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$: $2c_1 - c_2 = 1$ and $c_1 + c_2 = 0$, giving $c_1 = c_2 = 1/3$. Thus $u(t) = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{1}{3}e^{-3t}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. As $t \to \infty$, the exponential term vanishes and the steady state is $u_\infty = \begin{bmatrix} 2/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Summary: Three Possible Long-Term Behaviors

For any system $\frac{du}{dt} = Au$, the eigenvalues of $A$ determine the fate of the solution. (1) Stability (decay to zero): All eigenvalues have negative real parts. Every term $e^{\lambda t} \to 0$, so $u(t) \to 0$ regardless of initial conditions. (2) Steady state: One eigenvalue is zero and all others have negative real parts. The zero eigenvalue produces a constant component; the others decay. The solution approaches a fixed non-zero vector. (3) Instability (blow-up): At least one eigenvalue has positive real part. The corresponding exponential grows without bound, and $u(t) \to \infty$ in that direction.

Computation Examples

Derivation: Why $e^{\lambda t} x$ Solves the Differential Equation

Suppose $u(t) = e^{\lambda t} x$ where $x$ is a constant vector. Then:

$$\frac{du}{dt} = \frac{d}{dt}(e^{\lambda t} x) = \lambda e^{\lambda t} x$$

The right-hand side of the differential equation is:

$$Au(t) = A(e^{\lambda t} x) = e^{\lambda t} (Ax)$$

For these to be equal for all $t$, we need:

$$\lambda e^{\lambda t} x = e^{\lambda t} (Ax) \implies Ax = \lambda x$$

This is the eigenvalue equation. If $x$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda$, then $u(t) = e^{\lambda t} x$ is a solution to $\frac{du}{dt} = Au$.

Derivation: The General Solution as a Linear Combination

If $A$ has $n$ independent eigenvectors $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ with eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_n$, then each $e^{\lambda_i t} x_i$ is a solution. By superposition, any linear combination is also a solution:

$$u(t) = c_1 e^{\lambda_1 t} x_1 + c_2 e^{\lambda_2 t} x_2 + \cdots + c_n e^{\lambda_n t} x_n$$

The constants $c_1, c_2, \ldots, c_n$ are determined by the initial condition $u(0) = u_0$:

$$u_0 = c_1 x_1 + c_2 x_2 + \cdots + c_n x_n$$

This is just expressing $u_0$ in the eigenvector basis. The matrix form is $u(t) = S e^{\Lambda t} S^{-1} u_0$, where $S = [x_1 \; x_2 \; \cdots \; x_n]$ and $e^{\Lambda t} = \text{diag}(e^{\lambda_1 t}, e^{\lambda_2 t}, \ldots, e^{\lambda_n t})$.

Derivation: Stability Test via Eigenvalues

The solution $u(t) = \sum c_i e^{\lambda_i t} x_i$ tends to zero as $t \to \infty$ if and only if every exponential factor tends to zero. For a real eigenvalue $\lambda$: $e^{\lambda t} \to 0$ iff $\lambda < 0$. For a complex eigenvalue $\lambda = \alpha + i\beta$: $|e^{\lambda t}| = |e^{\alpha t} e^{i\beta t}| = e^{\alpha t} \cdot 1 = e^{\alpha t}$, so $e^{\lambda t} \to 0$ iff $\alpha < 0$. Therefore, $u(t) \to 0$ for all initial conditions if and only if every eigenvalue of $A$ has negative real part. This is the continuous-time analogue of $|\lambda| < 1$ for discrete-time stability.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "Powers Become Exponentials"

Chapter 22 had $u_k = c_1\lambda_1^k x_1 + c_2\lambda_2^k x_2$ for difference equations. Chapter 23 replaces powers with exponentials: $u(t) = c_1 e^{\lambda_1 t} x_1 + c_2 e^{\lambda_2 t} x_2$ for differential equations. Same structure, just replace $\lambda^k$ with $e^{\lambda t}$.

Mnemonic: "Real Part Rules Stability"

For stability, we need all exponentials to decay to zero. A real eigenvalue $\lambda < 0$ decays; $\lambda > 0$ blows up. A complex eigenvalue $\alpha + i\beta$ decays if $\alpha < 0$, regardless of $\beta$. The imaginary part only adds oscillation; the real part controls growth.

Mnemonic: "Zero Eigenvalue = Steady State"

A zero eigenvalue means $e^{0t} = 1$, which is a constant that never decays. This produces the steady state: the part of the solution that persists as $t \to \infty$. All other eigenvalues must have negative real parts for a true steady state.

Study Tips

  • Always find eigenvalues and eigenvectors first—they are the heart of the solution
  • Check the trace and determinant as quick sanity checks for your eigenvalues
  • For singular matrices, $\lambda = 0$ is immediate—find the null space for that eigenvector
  • When solving for $c_1, c_2$, set up the system at $t = 0$ where all exponentials equal 1
  • For complex eigenvalues, write the solution using real and imaginary parts, or use the magnitude-angle form
  • The steady state is the null space component: solve $Au = 0$ for the persistent mode

Worked Examples

Example 1: Solving a 2×2 System with a Zero Eigenvalue

Problem: Solve $\frac{du}{dt} = Au$ with $A = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $u(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Describe the long-term behavior.

Solution:

  • $A$ is singular (column 2 = $-2 \times$ column 1), so $\lambda_1 = 0$
  • Trace $= -3$, so $\lambda_2 = -3$
  • Eigenvector for $\lambda = 0$: null space of $A$ gives $x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Eigenvector for $\lambda = -3$: null space of $A+3I$ gives $x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • General solution: $u(t) = c_1 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2 e^{-3t} \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • At $t=0$: $2c_1 - c_2 = 1$, $c_1 + c_2 = 0 \Rightarrow c_1 = c_2 = 1/3$
  • Solution: $u(t) = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{1}{3}e^{-3t}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Steady state: $u_\infty = \begin{bmatrix} 2/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix}$ as $t \to \infty$

Example 2: Stability Analysis with Complex Eigenvalues

Problem: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ (90° rotation). Solve $\frac{du}{dt} = Au$ with $u(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Is the system stable?

Solution:

  • Eigenvalues: $\lambda = \pm i$ (purely imaginary)
  • Real parts are zero, so $|e^{\lambda t}| = 1$—no growth, no decay
  • Solution traces a circle: $u(t) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos t \\ \sin t \end{bmatrix}$
  • The solution is bounded but does not approach any steady state
  • This is marginally stable: the origin is a center
  • For stability (decay to zero), we need $\text{Re}(\lambda) < 0$ for all eigenvalues

Example 3: Blow-Up and the Effect of Positive Eigenvalues

Problem: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$. Solve $\frac{du}{dt} = Au$ with $u(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Describe what happens as $t \to \infty$.

Solution:

  • Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 2$ (positive), $\lambda_2 = -1$ (negative)
  • Eigenvectors: $x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • General solution: $u(t) = c_1 e^{2t} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + c_2 e^{-t} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • At $t=0$: $c_1 = 1$, $c_2 = 1$
  • Solution: $u(t) = \begin{bmatrix} e^{2t} \\ e^{-t} \end{bmatrix}$
  • As $t \to \infty$: first component blows up ($e^{2t} \to \infty$), second component decays ($e^{-t} \to 0$)
  • This system is unstable because of the positive eigenvalue $\lambda_1 = 2$

Practice Problems

Problem Set 23

Problem 1: Solve $\frac{du}{dt} = Au$ with $A = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & 2 \\ 1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $u(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Find the steady state as $t \to \infty$.

Hint: Find eigenvalues and eigenvectors first. The matrix is singular, so one eigenvalue is zero.

Solution

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 0$, $\lambda_2 = -3$. Eigenvectors: $x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. General solution: $u(t) = c_1 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + c_2 e^{-3t} \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Initial condition: $2c_1 - c_2 = 1$, $c_1 + c_2 = 0 \Rightarrow c_1 = c_2 = 1/3$. Solution: $u(t) = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} + \frac{1}{3}e^{-3t}\begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Steady state: $u_\infty = \begin{bmatrix} 2/3 \\ 1/3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Problem 2: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 0 \\ 0 & -5 \end{bmatrix}$. Solve $\frac{du}{dt} = Au$. Is the system stable?

Hint: Both eigenvalues are real and negative. What does $e^{\lambda t}$ do when $\lambda < 0$?

Solution

Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = -2$, $\lambda_2 = -5$. Eigenvectors: $x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Solution: $u(t) = c_1 e^{-2t} \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + c_2 e^{-5t} \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Since both eigenvalues are negative, $u(t) \to 0$ as $t \to \infty$ for any initial condition. The system is stable.

Problem 3: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Solve $\frac{du}{dt} = Au$ with $u(0) = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Describe the trajectory.

Hint: The eigenvalues are purely imaginary. Write the solution using sine and cosine.

Solution

Eigenvalues: $\lambda = \pm i$. The solution is $u(t) = c_1 e^{it} x_1 + c_2 e^{-it} x_2$. Using Euler's formula $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta$, the real solution is $u(t) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos t \\ \sin t \end{bmatrix}$. The trajectory is a circle of radius 1 centered at the origin. The solution is bounded but does not approach a steady state.

Problem 4: For what values of $a$ is the system $\frac{du}{dt} = \begin{bmatrix} a & 1 \\ -1 & a \end{bmatrix} u$ stable? Unstable? When does it have a steady state?

Hint: Compute the eigenvalues. The characteristic equation is $(a-\lambda)^2 + 1 = 0$.

Solution

Eigenvalues: $\lambda = a \pm i$. The real part is $a$. If $a < 0$, the system is stable (solutions spiral into the origin). If $a > 0$, the system is unstable (solutions spiral outward). If $a = 0$, the system has purely imaginary eigenvalues $\pm i$ and exhibits neutral stability (circular orbits). There is never a zero eigenvalue, so there is no steady state for any value of $a$.
