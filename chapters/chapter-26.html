Chapter 26: Symmetric Matrices and Positive Definiteness

This chapter establishes why symmetric matrices are the "good" matrices: they have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors. The spectral theorem reveals that every symmetric matrix can be decomposed into a sum of mutually perpendicular projection matrices. The second half introduces positive definite matrices—the subclass of symmetric matrices with all positive eigenvalues and pivots—and shows how to test for positive definiteness using pivots, eigenvalues, or the determinant of every leading principal submatrix.

Detailed Topic Breakdowns

Real Eigenvalues and Orthogonal Eigenvectors

For a real symmetric matrix $A$ ($A = A^T$), the eigenvalues are always real and the eigenvectors are always orthogonal. The proof uses the key identity $x^T A x = \lambda x^T x$. If $A$ has eigenvectors $x$ and $y$ with distinct eigenvalues $\lambda$ and $\alpha$, then $x^T A y = \lambda x^T y$ and $x^T A y = y^T A x = \alpha y^T x = \alpha x^T y$. Since $x^T A y = y^T A x$ by symmetry, we get $\lambda x^T y = \alpha x^T y$, so $(\lambda - \alpha)x^T y = 0$. Since $\lambda \neq \alpha$, we have $x^T y = 0$: the eigenvectors are orthogonal.

Hermitian Matrices: The Complex Generalization

For complex matrices, the correct generalization of symmetry is the Hermitian property: $A^H = A$, where $A^H = \bar{A}^T$ is the conjugate transpose. Hermitian matrices also have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors (with respect to the complex inner product $\bar{x}^T x$). For real matrices, $A^H = A$ reduces to $A^T = A$, so Hermitian includes symmetric as a special case. The key identity becomes $\bar{x}^T A x = \lambda \bar{x}^T x$, and the length squared of a complex vector $x$ is $\bar{x}^T x$.

The Spectral Theorem: Decomposition into Projection Matrices

The spectral theorem states that every symmetric matrix $A$ can be written as a sum of rank-1 projection matrices onto its eigenvectors: $A = \lambda_1 q_1 q_1^T + \lambda_2 q_2 q_2^T + \cdots + \lambda_n q_n q_n^T$, where the $q_i$ are orthonormal eigenvectors and $\lambda_i$ are the corresponding eigenvalues. Each term $q_i q_i^T$ is a symmetric projection matrix onto the line spanned by $q_i$. This decomposition reveals that a symmetric matrix is a "combination of mutually perpendicular projection matrices"—exactly as Strang describes.

Pivots and Eigenvalues Have the Same Sign

For a symmetric matrix, the number of positive pivots equals the number of positive eigenvalues, and the number of negative pivots equals the number of negative eigenvalues. This is a profound fact: you can determine the sign pattern of all eigenvalues by computing only the pivots from LU factorization—no need to find the eigenvalues explicitly. The product of the pivots equals the determinant, which equals the product of the eigenvalues, but the individual signs are what matter. This gives a practical numerical method for estimating eigenvalue distributions in large matrices.

Positive Definite Matrices

A symmetric matrix is positive definite if all its eigenvalues are positive. Equivalently, all its pivots are positive. Equivalently, all leading principal minors (determinants of the top-left $k \times k$ submatrices for $k = 1, 2, \ldots, n$) are positive. For a $2 \times 2$ matrix $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{bmatrix}$, the tests are: $a > 0$ (first pivot), $ac - b^2 > 0$ (determinant), and trace $a + c > 0$ (sum of eigenvalues). Computation Examples

Derivation: Orthogonality of Eigenvectors for Symmetric Matrices

Let $A$ be symmetric with $Ax = \lambda x$ and $Ay = \alpha y$ where $\lambda \neq \alpha$. Compute $y^T A x$ two ways:

$$y^T A x = y^T (\lambda x) = \lambda y^T x$$ $$y^T A x = (A^T y)^T x = (Ay)^T x = (\alpha y)^T x = \alpha y^T x$$

Since $A$ is symmetric, $A^T = A$, so both expressions equal $y^T A x$. Therefore $\lambda y^T x = \alpha y^T x$, giving $(\lambda - \alpha)y^T x = 0$. Since $\lambda \neq \alpha$, we have $y^T x = 0$. Thus eigenvectors corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal.

Derivation: The Spectral Theorem Decomposition

Let $A$ be symmetric with orthonormal eigenvectors $q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_n$ and eigenvalues $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_n$. Form the orthogonal matrix $Q = [q_1 \; q_2 \; \cdots \; q_n]$. Then $Q^T Q = I$ and $A Q = Q \Lambda$ where $\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n)$. Thus $A = Q \Lambda Q^T$. Expanding: $A = \sum_{i=1}^n \lambda_i q_i q_i^T$. Each term $\lambda_i q_i q_i^T$ is a scalar multiple of the projection matrix $P_i = q_i q_i^T$ onto the line spanned by $q_i$. The projection matrix $P_i$ satisfies $P_i^2 = P_i$ (idempotent), $P_i^T = P_i$ (symmetric), and has rank 1. Therefore every symmetric matrix is a weighted sum of orthogonal rank-1 projection matrices.

Derivation: Pivots and Eigenvalues Have the Same Sign

For a symmetric matrix $A$, the LU factorization $A = LU$ (with no row exchanges) produces pivots $d_1, d_2, \ldots, d_n$ on the diagonal of $U$. The determinant of every leading principal submatrix equals the product of the first $k$ pivots. The number of positive pivots equals the number of positive eigenvalues because: (1) the product of all pivots equals the determinant, which equals the product of all eigenvalues; (2) the sign of each pivot can be determined independently through the signs of the leading principal minors; (3) for symmetric matrices, the inertia theorem states that the numbers of positive, negative, and zero eigenvalues are preserved under congruence transformations. Thus the pivot signs exactly match the eigenvalue signs.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "Symmetric: Real Lambdas, Perpendicular X's"

Every real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors. This is the foundation of the spectral theorem. If you get complex eigenvectors for a real matrix, check whether it is actually symmetric.

Mnemonic: "Hermitian: Conjugate Transpose Equals Itself"

For complex matrices, replace transpose with conjugate transpose: $A^H = \bar{A}^T = A$. Hermitian matrices are the complex generalization of symmetric matrices and share the same properties: real eigenvalues, orthogonal eigenvectors.

Mnemonic: "Positive Definite: All Positive Eigenvalues, All Positive Pivots"

A symmetric matrix is positive definite if and only if all eigenvalues are positive if and only if all pivots are positive if and only if all leading principal minors are positive. These four conditions are equivalent.

Study Tips

  • Always check symmetry first: if $A = A^T$, then eigenvalues are real and eigenvectors are orthogonal
  • For the spectral theorem, remember the form: $A = \sum \lambda_i q_i q_i^T$ where each $q_i q_i^T$ is a projection matrix
  • The number of positive pivots equals the number of positive eigenvalues for symmetric matrices
  • To test positive definiteness, compute pivots in order: if any pivot is zero or negative, the matrix is not positive definite
  • The determinant alone is insufficient: all leading principal minors must be positive
  • For $2 \times 2$ symmetric matrices $\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ b & c \end{bmatrix}$, positive definiteness requires $a > 0$ and $ac > b^2$

Visual Aids

Figure: The Spectral Theorem Decomposition

Every symmetric matrix $A$ decomposes into a sum of orthogonal projection matrices weighted by eigenvalues:

$$A = \lambda_1 q_1 q_1^T + \lambda_2 q_2 q_2^T + \cdots + \lambda_n q_n q_n^T$$

Each term $q_i q_i^T$ projects onto the $i$-th eigenvector direction. The eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ scales that projection. The orthogonality of the $q_i$ ensures the projections are mutually perpendicular.

Figure: Pivot Signs Match Eigenvalue Signs

For a symmetric matrix, the signs of the pivots from LU factorization match the signs of the eigenvalues:

$$\text{Positive pivots: } n_+ \quad \text{Negative pivots: } n_- \quad \text{Zero pivots: } n_0$$

$$\text{Positive eigenvalues: } n_+ \quad \text{Negative eigenvalues: } n_- \quad \text{Zero eigenvalues: } n_0$$

This means you can determine the eigenvalue sign pattern without computing the eigenvalues themselves—just compute the pivots.

Figure: Positive Definiteness Tests

For a symmetric matrix, these four conditions are equivalent:

1. All eigenvalues $\lambda_i > 0$

2. All pivots $d_i > 0$

3. All leading principal minors $\det(A_k) > 0$ for $k = 1, 2, \ldots, n$

4. $x^T A x > 0$ for all nonzero $x$

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$: pivot 1 = 5 > 0, pivot 2 = 11/5 > 0, $\det(A) = 4 > 0$, eigenvalues are positive. All four tests pass.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Orthogonal Eigenvectors of a Symmetric Matrix

Problem: For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors, and verify that the eigenvectors are orthogonal.

Solution:

  • Characteristic equation: $\det(A - \lambda I) = (1-\lambda)^2 - 4 = \lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 3 = 0$
  • Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 3, \lambda_2 = -1$
  • Eigenvector for $\lambda_1 = 3$: $(A-3I)x = 0 \Rightarrow \begin{bmatrix} -2 & 2 \\ 2 & -2 \end{bmatrix}x = 0$, giving $x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Eigenvector for $\lambda_2 = -1$: $(A+I)x = 0 \Rightarrow \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}x = 0$, giving $x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Orthogonality check: $x_1^T x_2 = [1, 1] \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = 1 - 1 = 0$ ✓
  • Normalized: $q_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1, 1]^T$, $q_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1, -1]^T$

Example 2: Spectral Theorem Decomposition

Problem: Decompose $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ into a sum of projection matrices using the spectral theorem.

Solution:

  • Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 3, \lambda_2 = -1$
  • Orthonormal eigenvectors: $q_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1, 1]^T$, $q_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[1, -1]^T$
  • Projection matrices: $P_1 = q_1 q_1^T = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $P_2 = q_2 q_2^T = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Spectral decomposition: $A = 3P_1 + (-1)P_2 = \frac{3}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} - \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  • Compute: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ ✓
  • Each $P_i$ is a rank-1 symmetric projection matrix; $P_1^2 = P_1$, $P_2^2 = P_2$, $P_1^T = P_1$, $P_2^T = P_2$

Example 3: Testing Positive Definiteness

Problem: Determine whether $A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 3 \\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix} -1 & -3 \\ -2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ are positive definite.

Solution:

  • For $A$: pivot 1 = 5 > 0, $\det(A) = 10 - 6 = 4 > 0$, both pivots positive ⇒ positive definite
  • For $B$: pivot 1 = -1 < 0 ⇒ not positive definite
  • Eigenvalues of $A$: $\lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 4 = 0$, both roots positive (approximately 6.24 and 0.76)
  • Eigenvalues of $B$: $\lambda^2 - 0\lambda - 7 = 0$, one positive and one negative
  • The determinant of $B$ is -7 (negative), so $B$ is not positive definite
  • Key insight: all leading principal minors of a positive definite matrix must be positive

Practice Problems

Problem Set 26

Problem 1: For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors, verify orthogonality, and determine if $A$ is positive definite.

Hint: Compute the characteristic polynomial, then check if all eigenvalues are positive. Alternatively, check the pivots.

Solution

Characteristic equation: $\det(A - \lambda I) = (4-\lambda)(3-\lambda) - 1 = \lambda^2 - 7\lambda + 11 = 0$. Eigenvalues: $\lambda = \frac{7 \pm \sqrt{49-44}}{2} = \frac{7 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2} \approx 5.618, 1.382$. Both positive, so $A$ is positive definite. Eigenvectors are orthogonal by the symmetric property. Pivots: 4 and $11/4 = 2.75$, both positive.

Problem 2: State and prove the spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices: every real symmetric matrix can be written as $A = Q \Lambda Q^T$ where $Q$ is orthogonal and $\Lambda$ is diagonal.

Hint: Use induction on the dimension. Find one eigenvector $q_1$, complete it to an orthonormal basis, and restrict $A$ to the orthogonal complement.

Solution

Since $A$ is real symmetric, it has at least one real eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ with unit eigenvector $q_1$. Complete $\{q_1\}$ to an orthonormal basis $\{q_1, q_2, \ldots, q_n\}$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Let $Q = [q_1 \; q_2 \; \cdots \; q_n]$. Then $Q^T Q = I$. For any vector $x = Qy$, we have $Ax = AQy = Q(Q^T A Q)y$. The matrix $Q^T A Q$ is symmetric and has first column $Q^T A q_1 = Q^T (\lambda_1 q_1) = \lambda_1 e_1$. By symmetry, it has first row $\lambda_1 e_1^T$. Thus $Q^T A Q = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & B \end{bmatrix}$ where $B$ is symmetric. By induction, $B = Q_2 \Lambda_2 Q_2^T$. Thus $A = Q \Lambda Q^T$ with $\Lambda = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \Lambda_2)$.

Problem 3: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 & -1 \\ 0 & -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Determine if $A$ is positive definite by computing its pivots and eigenvalues.

Hint: This is the tridiagonal matrix from Chapter 19. Compute pivots using LU factorization without row exchanges.

Solution

Pivot 1: 2. Eliminate: row 2 + (1/2)row 1 gives $[0, 1.5, -1]$. Pivot 2: 1.5. Eliminate: row 3 + (2/3)row 2 gives $[0, 0, 4/3]$. Pivot 3: 4/3. All pivots are positive (2, 1.5, 4/3), so $A$ is positive definite. Eigenvalues of this tridiagonal matrix are $2(1 - \cos(k\pi/4))$ for $k = 1, 2, 3$, giving approximately 0.586, 2.0, 3.414—all positive. Determinant = $2 \cdot 1.5 \cdot (4/3) = 4$.

Problem 4: Explain why the matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ is NOT positive definite, even though its determinant is negative. Use both the pivot test and the eigenvalue test.

Hint: Compute the eigenvalues or just the first pivot. What does a negative eigenvalue tell you?

Solution

Eigenvalues: $\lambda^2 - 2\lambda - 3 = 0$, giving $\lambda_1 = 3 > 0$ and $\lambda_2 = -1 < 0$. Since one eigenvalue is negative, $A$ is not positive definite. Pivot test: pivot 1 = 1 > 0, but $\det(A) = 1 - 4 = -3 < 0$, so the second pivot is negative. A symmetric matrix is positive definite if and only if all pivots are positive. Here the second pivot is negative, so $A$ is indefinite (one positive eigenvalue, one negative eigenvalue). Geometrically, $x^T A x = x_1^2 + 4x_1 x_2 + x_2^2$ takes negative values for some nonzero $x$.
