Detailed Topic Breakdowns

The Orthogonality Framework

This is a ninety-degree chapter. Orthogonality means perpendicularity in n-dimensional space. The chapter covers what it means for vectors to be orthogonal, what it means for subspaces to be orthogonal, and what it means for bases to be orthogonal. The beauty of the four-subspace picture is that the row space and null space are orthogonal, and the column space and left nullspace are orthogonal. The dimensions are already known: r and n-r, r and m-r. Now we learn that the angle between these subspaces is ninety degrees.

Orthogonal Vectors

Two vectors x and y are orthogonal if their dot product is zero: $x^T y = 0$. The dot product is also written as $x \cdot y$ and equals $x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2 + \cdots + x_n y_n$. The zero vector is orthogonal to every vector. The length squared of a vector x is $x^T x = x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_n^2$, which is always positive unless x is the zero vector.

Pythagorean Theorem Connection

For a right triangle with orthogonal vectors x and y, the Pythagorean theorem states $|x|^2 + |y|^2 = |x+y|^2$. Expanding the right side: $(x+y)^T(x+y) = x^T x + x^T y + y^T x + y^T y$. When $x^T y = 0$, the cross terms vanish and we get $|x|^2 + |y|^2 = |x+y|^2$. This confirms that $x^T y = 0$ is exactly the condition for orthogonality.

Orthogonal Subspaces

Two subspaces S and T are orthogonal if every vector in S is orthogonal to every vector in T. Formally: $S \perp T$ means for all $s \in S$ and $t \in T$, we have $s^T t = 0$. Merely not intersecting is not enough - every pair of vectors, one from each subspace, must be orthogonal. If subspaces share a nonzero vector, they cannot be orthogonal because that vector would not be orthogonal to itself.

Examples of Orthogonal Subspaces

In $\mathbb{R}^3$, the x-axis and the y-z plane are orthogonal. The x-axis and the z-axis are orthogonal. The x-axis and the line $y=x$ in the x-y plane are NOT orthogonal (they meet at 45 degrees). The blackboard plane and the floor plane are NOT orthogonal because they intersect along a line. In $\mathbb{R}^2$, a line through the origin is orthogonal to another line through the origin if and only if they meet at 90 degrees.

The Four Subspaces Are Orthogonal

The Fundamental Theorem of Linear Algebra has an orthogonal complement version: In $\mathbb{R}^n$, the row space $C(A^T)$ and the null space $N(A)$ are orthogonal complements. This means $C(A^T) \perp N(A)$ and $C(A^T) \cup N(A) = \mathbb{R}^n$. Similarly, in $\mathbb{R}^m$, the column space $C(A)$ and the left nullspace $N(A^T)$ are orthogonal complements. The dimensions match perfectly: $dim(C(A^T)) = r$, $dim(N(A)) = n-r$, and $r + (n-r) = n$.

Why the Row Space Is Orthogonal to the Null Space

If $x \in N(A)$, then $Ax = 0$. Writing this out row by row, if the rows of A are $r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_m$, then $Ax = 0$ means $r_1 x = 0, r_2 x = 0, \ldots, r_m x = 0$. Every row of A dotted with x equals zero. Since the row space is the span of the rows, x is orthogonal to every vector in the row space. Thus $N(A) \perp C(A^T)$.

Computation Examples

Derivation: Dot Product Test for Orthogonality

For vectors $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n$, expand $|x+y|^2$:

$$|x+y|^2 = (x+y)^T(x+y) = x^T x + x^T y + y^T x + y^T y$$

Since $x^T y = y^T x$ for real vectors:

$$|x+y|^2 = |x|^2 + |y|^2 + 2x^T y$$

By the Pythagorean theorem, $|x+y|^2 = |x|^2 + |y|^2$ if and only if $x^T y = 0$. Therefore x and y are orthogonal if and only if $x^T y = 0$.

Derivation: Row Space Orthogonal to Null Space

Let $A$ be an $m \times n$ matrix with rows $r_1, r_2, \ldots, r_m \in \mathbb{R}^n$. If $x \in N(A)$, then:

$$Ax = \begin{bmatrix} r_1 \\ r_2 \\ \vdots \\ r_m \end{bmatrix} x = \begin{bmatrix} r_1 x \\ r_2 x \\ \vdots \\ r_m x \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \vdots \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Each row dotted with x is zero: $r_i x = 0$ for all $i = 1, \ldots, m$. Since the row space $C(A^T) = span\{r_1, \ldots, r_m\}$, every vector $v \in C(A^T)$ is a linear combination $v = \sum c_i r_i$. Then:

$$v^T x = \sum c_i (r_i x) = \sum c_i \cdot 0 = 0$$

Thus $x \perp v$ for all $v \in C(A^T)$, so $N(A) \perp C(A^T)$.

Derivation: Dimensions of Orthogonal Complement Subspaces

In $\mathbb{R}^n$, if subspace $S$ has dimension $d$, then its orthogonal complement $S^{\perp}$ has dimension $n-d$. For the four subspaces of an $m \times n$ matrix A with rank r:

$$dim(C(A^T)) = r, \quad dim(N(A)) = n - r, \quad dim(C(A^T)) + dim(N(A)) = n$$ $$dim(C(A)) = r, \quad dim(N(A^T)) = m - r, \quad dim(C(A)) + dim(N(A^T)) = m$$

The dimensions add to the ambient space dimension, confirming they are complementary.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "Dot Product Zero Means Perpendicular"

Two vectors are orthogonal if and only if their dot product is zero. Compute $x^T y$ entrywise: $x_1 y_1 + x_2 y_2 + \cdots$. If the sum is zero, they are perpendicular.

Mnemonic: "Every Vector in S Orthogonal to Every Vector in T"

Subspaces S and T are orthogonal if every pair of vectors, one from each subspace, has dot product zero. It is not enough that they merely do not intersect - every vector in S must be perpendicular to every vector in T.

Mnemonic: "Row Space Perpendicular to Null Space"

The equation $Ax = 0$ says every row of A dotted with x equals zero. So the null space is orthogonal to every row, and therefore orthogonal to the entire row space.

Study Tips

  • Always verify orthogonality by computing the dot product $x^T y$ and checking if it equals zero
  • For subspaces, test the definition: pick a basis for each subspace and verify every cross-pair is orthogonal
  • Remember that the zero vector is orthogonal to everything
  • If two subspaces share a nonzero vector, they cannot be orthogonal
  • Use the dimension check: if $S \perp T$ and $S \cap T = \{0\}$, then $dim(S) + dim(T) \leq n$
  • The four subspaces form two orthogonal complement pairs in their respective spaces $\mathbb{R}^n$ and $\mathbb{R}^m$

For orthogonal vectors x and y, the right triangle relationship holds:

$$|x|^2 + |y|^2 = |x+y|^2 \quad \text{if and only if} \quad x^T y = 0$$

Example in $\mathbb{R}^3$: Let $x = [1, 2, 3]^T$ with $|x|^2 = 1+4+9 = 14$. Let $y = [1, -1, 0]^T$ with $|y|^2 = 1+1+0 = 2$. Since $x^T y = 1 - 2 + 0 = -1 \neq 0$, they are NOT orthogonal. But if we take $y = [1, -2, 1]^T$, then $x^T y = 1 - 4 + 3 = 0$, and $|x+y|^2 = |[2, 0, 4]^T|^2 = 4 + 0 + 16 = 20 = 14 + 2$.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Testing Vector Orthogonality

Problem: Determine whether the vectors $x = [1, 2, 3]^T$ and $y = [2, -1, 0]^T$ are orthogonal.

Solution:

  • Compute the dot product: $x^T y = 1(2) + 2(-1) + 3(0) = 2 - 2 + 0 = 0$
  • Since $x^T y = 0$, the vectors are orthogonal
  • Verify with Pythagoras: $|x|^2 = 1 + 4 + 9 = 14$, $|y|^2 = 4 + 1 + 0 = 5$, $|x+y|^2 = |[3, 1, 3]^T|^2 = 9 + 1 + 9 = 19 = 14 + 5$

Example 2: Orthogonal Subspaces in $\mathbb{R}^2$

Problem: In $\mathbb{R}^2$, let $S = span\{[1, 2]^T\}$ and $T = span\{[2, -1]^T\}$. Are S and T orthogonal subspaces?

Solution:

  • Take $s = [1, 2]^T \in S$ and $t = [2, -1]^T \in T$
  • Compute $s^T t = 1(2) + 2(-1) = 2 - 2 = 0$
  • Since the basis vectors are orthogonal, every vector in S is orthogonal to every vector in T
  • Yes, $S \perp T$
  • $dim(S) = 1$, $dim(T) = 1$, $dim(S) + dim(T) = 2 = dim(\mathbb{R}^2)$

Example 3: Row Space Orthogonal to Null Space

Problem: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$. Show that the row space of A is orthogonal to the null space of A.

Solution:

  • Row space: $C(A^T) = span\{[1, 2, 3], [2, 4, 6]\} = span\{[1, 2, 3]\}$ (rank 1)
  • Null space: Solve $Ax = 0$: $x_1 + 2x_2 + 3x_3 = 0$
  • Basis for $N(A)$: $[-2, 1, 0]^T$, $[-3, 0, 1]^T$
  • Check: $[1, 2, 3] \cdot [-2, 1, 0] = -2 + 2 + 0 = 0$
  • Check: $[1, 2, 3] \cdot [-3, 0, 1] = -3 + 0 + 3 = 0$
  • The row space vector is orthogonal to both null space basis vectors, confirming $C(A^T) \perp N(A)$

Example 4: Non-Orthogonal Subspaces

Problem: In $\mathbb{R}^3$, let $S$ be the x-y plane and $T$ be the plane $x = y$. Are S and T orthogonal subspaces?

Solution:

  • S contains $[1, 0, 0]^T$ and $[0, 1, 0]^T$
  • T contains $[1, 1, 0]^T$ (since $x=y$)
  • The vector $[1, 1, 0]^T$ is in both S and T
  • Since they share a nonzero vector, they cannot be orthogonal
  • Also, $[1, 0, 0]^T \in S$ and $[1, 1, 0]^T \in T$, but $[1, 0, 0]^T \cdot [1, 1, 0]^T = 1 \neq 0$
  • S and T are NOT orthogonal

Practice Problems

Problem Set 14

Problem 1: Determine whether each pair of vectors is orthogonal: (a) $x = [1, 3]^T$, $y = [3, -1]^T$; (b) $x = [1, 2, 3]^T$, $y = [-2, 1, 0]^T$; (c) $x = [1, 1, 1]^T$, $y = [1, -2, 1]^T$.

Hint: Compute $x^T y$ for each pair. If the result is zero, they are orthogonal.

Solution
  • (a) $x^T y = 1(3) + 3(-1) = 3 - 3 = 0$. Orthogonal.
  • (b) $x^T y = 1(-2) + 2(1) + 3(0) = -2 + 2 + 0 = 0$. Orthogonal.
  • (c) $x^T y = 1(1) + 1(-2) + 1(1) = 1 - 2 + 1 = 0$. Orthogonal.

Problem 2: In $\mathbb{R}^4$, let $S = span\{[1, 0, 0, 0]^T, [0, 1, 0, 0]^T\}$ and $T = span\{[0, 0, 1, 0]^T, [0, 0, 0, 1]^T\}$. Show that $S \perp T$.

Hint: Take arbitrary vectors from each subspace and compute their dot product.

Solution

Any vector in S has the form $[a, b, 0, 0]^T$. Any vector in T has the form $[0, 0, c, d]^T$. Their dot product is $a(0) + b(0) + 0(c) + 0(d) = 0$. Thus $S \perp T$.

Problem 3: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Find a basis for the null space of A and verify that it is orthogonal to the row space.

Hint: Solve $Ax = 0$. The row space is spanned by the nonzero rows of A.

Solution

Row reduce A: $RREF = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Rank = 2. Free variable: $x_3$. Special solution: $[-1, -1, 1]^T$. Basis for $N(A) = \{[-1, -1, 1]^T\}$. Row space basis: $\{[1, 0, 1], [0, 1, 1]\}$. Check: $[1, 0, 1] \cdot [-1, -1, 1] = -1 + 0 + 1 = 0$. $[0, 1, 1] \cdot [-1, -1, 1] = 0 - 1 + 1 = 0$. Verified.

Problem 4: True or False: If subspaces S and T are orthogonal and $S \cap T = \{0\}$, then $dim(S) + dim(T) \leq dim(V)$ where V is the ambient space. Explain.

Hint: Think about the orthogonal complement theorem.

Solution

True. If $S \perp T$ and $S \cap T = \{0\}$, then $S + T$ is a direct sum. In fact, if $S \perp T$, then $S \cap T = \{0\}$ automatically. The dimension formula gives $dim(S+T) = dim(S) + dim(T) - dim(S \cap T) = dim(S) + dim(T)$. Since $S+T \subseteq V$, we have $dim(S) + dim(T) \leq dim(V)$. If $S+T = V$, they are orthogonal complements.

Problem 5: Prove that the null space of $A^T$ is orthogonal to the column space of A.

Hint: If $y \in N(A^T)$, then $A^T y = 0$. For any $b \in C(A)$, there exists x with $Ax = b$.

Solution

If $y \in N(A^T)$, then $A^T y = 0$. For any $b \in C(A)$, $b = Ax$ for some x. Then $y^T b = y^T Ax = (A^T y)^T x = 0^T x = 0$. Thus y is orthogonal to every vector in $C(A)$, so $N(A^T) \perp C(A)$.
