Detailed Topic Breakdowns

Matrix Multiplication

Matrix multiplication is defined by taking the dot product of rows of the first matrix with columns of the second. The product $AB$ exists only when the number of columns of $A$ equals the number of rows of $B$.

Inverse of a Matrix

A square matrix $A$ has an inverse $A^{-1}$ if there exists a matrix such that $AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I$. Not all square matrices are invertible — those that are not are called singular or degenerate.

Diagonal and Elimination Matrices

Diagonal matrices have nonzero entries only on the main diagonal. They are easy to invert: simply invert each diagonal entry. Elimination matrices perform row operations and their inverses reverse those operations.

Gauss-Jordan Elimination

By augmenting $A$ with the identity matrix $[A \mid I]$ and reducing to $[I \mid A^{-1}]$, we can compute the inverse directly. If $A$ cannot be reduced to the identity, it is singular.

Computation Examples

Derivation: Inverse of a $2 \times 2$ Matrix

For a $2 \times 2$ matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$, the inverse exists when $ad - bc \neq 0$:

$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad-bc} \begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}$$

The determinant $ad - bc$ must be nonzero for the inverse to exist. Verify by multiplying $A \cdot A^{-1}$:

$$\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{d}{\det} & \frac{-b}{\det} \\ \frac{-c}{\det} & \frac{a}{\det} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Derivation: Properties of Matrix Inverses

Key algebraic properties:

$$(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1} A^{-1} \quad \text{(reverse order!)}$$ $$(A^{-1})^{-1} = A$$ $$(A^T)^{-1} = (A^{-1})^T$$ $$(A + B)^{-1} \neq A^{-1} + B^{-1} \quad \text{(warning: does not distribute!)}$$

The reversal of order in $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$ is crucial: you must undo the last operation first when inverting a product.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "S-O-S" — Same Order, Switch, Swap

For a $2 \times 2$ inverse: Same diagonal entries stay, Off-diagonals switch positions, Swap signs of off-diagonals, then divide by determinant.

Mnemonic: "Last In, First Out" for Inverses

When inverting a product $AB$, undo $B$ first ($B^{-1}$), then undo $A$ ($A^{-1}$). This is why $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$.

Study Tips

  • Always check dimensions before multiplying — incompatible sizes mean the product doesn't exist
  • Memorize the $2 \times 2$ inverse formula; for larger matrices, use Gauss-Jordan
  • Verify inverses by checking $AA^{-1} = I$, not just $A^{-1}A = I$
  • Remember: a zero determinant means singularity and no inverse
  • Practice with permutation matrices — their inverses are their transposes

Worked Examples

Example 1: Matrix Multiplication

Problem: Compute $AB$ and $BA$ and determine if they are equal.

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

Solution:

$$AB = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 1 & 1 \cdot 0 + 2 \cdot 4 \\ 0 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 1 & 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 8 \\ 1 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$ $$BA = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 & 3 \cdot 2 + 0 \cdot 1 \\ 1 \cdot 1 + 4 \cdot 0 & 1 \cdot 2 + 4 \cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 1 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

$AB \neq BA$. Matrix multiplication is not commutative.

Example 2: Finding an Inverse

Problem: Find $A^{-1}$ using the formula and verify.

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Solution:

  1. Determinant: $\det(A) = 2 \cdot 2 - 3 \cdot 1 = 1$
  2. Apply formula: $A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$
  3. Verify: $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -3 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ ✓

Example 3: Singular Matrix

Problem: Show that $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ has no inverse.

Solution:

  • Determinant: $1 \cdot 4 - 2 \cdot 2 = 4 - 4 = 0$
  • Row 2 = $2 \times$ Row 1
  • Columns are linearly dependent
  • Rank = 1 $< 2$, so $A$ is singular
  • No inverse exists.

Practice Problems

Problem Set 3

Problem 1: Compute the product $AB$:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 3 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Hint: Multiply row by column for each entry.

Solution $$AB = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 1 \\ 3 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

Problem 2: Find the inverse of $A = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 7 \\ 2 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$ and verify.

Hint: Calculate the determinant first.

Solution

$\det = 4 \cdot 6 - 7 \cdot 2 = 24 - 14 = 10$

$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{10} \begin{bmatrix} 6 & -7 \\ -2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.6 & -0.7 \\ -0.2 & 0.4 \end{bmatrix}$

Problem 3: Show that if $A$ and $B$ are invertible, then $AB$ is invertible with $(AB)^{-1} = B^{-1}A^{-1}$.

Hint: Multiply $(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1})$ and use associativity.

Solution $$(AB)(B^{-1}A^{-1}) = A(BB^{-1})A^{-1} = AIA^{-1} = AA^{-1} = I$$

Problem 4: For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 & 4 \\ 5 & 6 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, use Gauss-Jordan to find $A^{-1}$.

Hint: Augment $[A \mid I]$ and reduce the left side to $I$.

Solution

After Gauss-Jordan elimination: $A^{-1} = \begin{bmatrix} -24 & 18 & 5 \\ 20 & -15 & -4 \\ -5 & 4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
