Chapter 22: Diagonalization and Powers of A

Computing matrix powers through diagonalization. This chapter reveals how any diagonalizable matrix can be written as A = SΛ$S^{-1}$, which immediately gives $A^k$ = $S\Lambda^k S^{-1}$. The Fibonacci sequence serves as the central example, showing how eigenvalues control the growth rate of dynamical systems and how a second-order recurrence can be converted to a first-order matrix system.

Detailed Topic Breakdowns

The Diagonalization Formula

If A is diagonalizable with eigenvector matrix S and eigenvalue matrix Λ, then A = SΛ$S^{-1}$. Consequently, $A^k$ = $S\Lambda^k S^{-1}$. This formula means that to compute $A^k$, you expand the initial vector u0 into eigenvectors, multiply each eigencomponent by its eigenvalue raised to the k-th power, and recombine. The matrix S contains the eigenvectors as columns, and Λ is the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues.

From Recurrence to Dynamical Systems

The Fibonacci sequence satisfies Fk+2 = Fk+1 + Fk, which is a second-order scalar recurrence. To convert it to a first-order system, define the state vector uk = [Fk+1, Fk]T. Then uk+1 = Auk where $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. This transforms a single second-order equation into a 2×2 matrix system. The matrix A captures the recurrence in first-order form.

Eigenvalues of the Fibonacci Matrix

For A = $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the characteristic equation is det(A - $\lambda$I) = $\lambda^2 - \lambda - 1 = 0$. The eigenvalues are $\lambda_1$ = (1+√5)/2 ≠ 1.618 and $\lambda_2$ = (1-√5)/2 ≠ -0.618. They add to the trace (1) and multiply to the determinant (-1). The dominant eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ is the golden ratio, and it controls the exponential growth of the Fibonacci numbers. The second eigenvalue $\lambda_2$ has magnitude less than 1, so its contribution vanishes as k increases.

Eigenvectors and the Expansion of u0

The eigenvectors of A are x1 = [$\lambda_1$, 1]T and x2 = [$\lambda_2$, 1]T. The initial vector u0 = [F1, F0]T = [1, 0]T is expressed as a linear combination u0 = c1x1 + c2x2. Then uk = $A^k u_0$ = c1$\lambda_1$kx1 + c2$\lambda_2$kx2. As k grows, the term with |$\lambda_1$|k dominates because |$\lambda_1$| > 1 and |$\lambda_2$| < 1.

Dynamic Systems vs Static Problems

Static problems solve Ax = b for a fixed right-hand side. Dynamic problems follow uk+1 = Auk over time. The eigenvalues determine the long-term behavior: if |$\lambda$| < 1, solutions decay to zero; if |$\lambda$| > 1, solutions grow exponentially; if |$\lambda$| = 1, solutions remain bounded. For the Fibonacci matrix, $\lambda_1$ ≠ 1.618 > 1 means exponential growth, and the growth rate is approximately $\lambda_1$k.

Computation Examples

Derivation: The Diagonalization Formula for $A^k$

If A has n independent eigenvectors, they form the columns of S = [x1 x2 ... xn]. Then A = SΛ$S^{-1}$ where Λ = diag($\lambda_1$, $\lambda_2$, ..., $\lambda$n). Raising to the k-th power:

$$ A^k = (S\Lambda S^{-1})^k = S\Lambda S^{-1} S\Lambda S^{-1} \cdots S\Lambda S^{-1} $$

Since $S^{-1}$S = I in the middle, all interior terms cancel:

$$ A^k = S\Lambda^k S^{-1} $$

Here $\Lambda^k$ = diag($\lambda_1$k, $\lambda_2$k, ..., $\lambda$nk). This is the fundamental formula for matrix powers.

Derivation: Fibonacci Recurrence as a Matrix System

The scalar recurrence Fk+2 = Fk+1 + Fk with F0 = 0, F1 = 1 is converted to first-order form by defining uk = [Fk+1, Fk]T. Then:

$$ u_{k+1} = \begin{bmatrix} F_{k+2} \\ F_{k+1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} F_{k+1} + F_k \\ F_{k+1} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} F_{k+1} \\ F_k \end{bmatrix} = Au_k $$

Thus uk = $A^k u_0$ with u0 = [1, 0]T. The Fibonacci numbers appear as components of uk: Fk is the second component of uk-1.

Derivation: Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors of the Fibonacci Matrix

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the characteristic equation is:

$$ \det(A - \lambda I) = \det\begin{pmatrix} 1-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \lambda^2 - \lambda - 1 = 0 $$

Using the quadratic formula:

$$ \lambda_{1,2} = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{5}}{2} $$

For each eigenvalue, the eigenvector satisfies (A - $\lambda$I)x = 0. For a 2×2 matrix, if $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue, then $\begin{bmatrix} \lambda \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}^T$ is an eigenvector because:

$$ \begin{pmatrix} 1-\lambda & 1 \\ 1 & -\lambda \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \lambda \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \lambda - \lambda^2 + 1 \\ \lambda - \lambda^2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} $$

since $\lambda^2 - \lambda - 1 = 0$. Thus x1 = [$\lambda_1$, 1]T and x2 = [$\lambda_2$, 1]T.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "A to the k = S Lambda to the k S inverse"

To compute $A^k$, diagonalize A = SΛ$S^{-1}$, then raise the diagonal matrix Λ to the k-th power entrywise, and sandwich it between S and $S^{-1}$. The hard work is finding S and Λ; raising $\Lambda^k$ is trivial.

Mnemonic: "Second Order to First Order: Stack the State"

To convert a recurrence like Fk+2 = Fk+1 + Fk into a matrix system, define uk = [Fk+1, Fk]T. This stacks the current and previous terms into a vector, turning a scalar recurrence into a first-order system.

Mnemonic: "Dominant Eigenvalue Controls Growth"

For large k, the term with the largest |$\lambda$| dominates $A^k$. If |$\lambda_1$| > |$\lambda_2$| > ... > |$\lambda$n|, then $A^k$ ≠ $\lambda_1$k times a rank-1 matrix. The Fibonacci growth rate is $\lambda_1$ ≠ 1.618.

Study Tips

  • Always check that A has n independent eigenvectors before applying $A^k$ = $S\Lambda^k S^{-1}$
  • For a 2×2 matrix, the eigenvectors often have the simple form $\begin{bmatrix} \lambda \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}^T$
  • The trace equals the sum of eigenvalues; the determinant equals the product - use these to check your work
  • When converting recurrences to matrix form, the state vector must contain enough history to make the system first-order
  • The dominant eigenvalue (largest magnitude) determines the long-term growth or decay rate
  • If all |$\lambda$i| < 1, then $A^k$ → 0 as k → ∞

Figure: Eigenvalue Spectrum and Long-Term Behavior

For different dynamical systems, the eigenvalue locations control the behavior:

$$ |\lambda| < 1: \text{decay to zero} \quad |\lambda| > 1: \text{exponential growth} \quad |\lambda| = 1: \text{oscillation or steady state} $$

For the Fibonacci matrix, $\lambda_1$ ≠ 1.618 lies outside the unit circle (growth), while $\lambda_2$ ≠ -0.618 lies inside (decay). The long-term behavior is dominated by the larger magnitude.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Diagonalizing a 2×2 Matrix

Problem: Diagonalize $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and compute $A^5$ using the formula $A^k = S\Lambda^k S^{-1}$.

Solution:

  • Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = (1+\sqrt{5})/2 \approx 1.618$, $\lambda_2 = (1-\sqrt{5})/2 \approx -0.618$
  • Eigenvectors: $x_1 = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}^T$, $x_2 = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}^T$
  • $S = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & \lambda_2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $\Lambda = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2 \end{bmatrix}$
  • $A^5 = S\Lambda^5 S^{-1}$. Rather than multiply $A$ five times, compute $\Lambda^5 = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1^5 & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_2^5 \end{bmatrix}$ and sandwich between $S$ and $S^{-1}$
  • This gives F6 = 8 and F5 = 5 in the matrix entries

Example 2: Fibonacci Numbers via Diagonalization

Problem: Use diagonalization to find a closed formula for Fk and explain why F100 ≠ c1$\lambda_1$100.

Solution:

  • u0 = [1, 0]T = c1x1 + c2x2
  • Solving for c1, c2 gives c1 = 1/√5, c2 = -1/√5
  • Thus uk = (1/√5)$\lambda_1$kx1 - (1/√5)$\lambda_2$kx2
  • The second term $\lambda_2$100 ≠ (-0.618)100 ≠ 10-25 is negligible
  • Therefore F100 ≠ (1/√5)$\lambda_1$100 ≠ (1/√5)(1.618)100

Example 3: Dynamics of $A^k$ for Different Eigenvalues

Problem: Describe the long-term behavior of uk = $A^k u_0$ for matrices with eigenvalues (a) 0.5 and 0.3, (b) 2 and -3, (c) 1 and 1.

Solution:

  • (a) Both |$\lambda$| < 1: uk → 0 exponentially fast
  • (b) |$\lambda_1$| = 2 > 1 and |$\lambda_2$| = 3 > 1: uk grows exponentially, dominated by (-3)k with sign oscillations
  • (c) |$\lambda$| = 1: uk stays bounded but may oscillate; growth rate is polynomial, not exponential

Practice Problems

Problem Set 22

Problem 1: Let A = $\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. Diagonalize A and compute $A^{10}$ using the formula $A^k$ = $S\Lambda^k S^{-1}$.

Hint: Find eigenvalues 3 and 1 with eigenvectors [1,1]T and [1,-1]T. Then $S = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solution

Λ = $\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $\Lambda^{10} = $\begin{bmatrix} 3^{10} & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. $S^{-1}$ = $\frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$. $A^{10}$ = $S\Lambda^{10} S^{-1}$ gives the result without multiplying A ten times.

Problem 2: For the Fibonacci matrix A = $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, verify that the eigenvectors are $\begin{bmatrix} \lambda_1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}^T$ and $\begin{bmatrix} \lambda_2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}^T$ by direct multiplication.

Hint: Show that (A - $\lambda$I)[$\lambda$, 1]T = 0 using the fact that $\lambda^2 - \lambda - 1 = 0$.

Solution

$(A - \lambda I)\begin{bmatrix} \lambda \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}^T$ = [1-$\lambda$, 1; 1, -$\lambda$][$\lambda$, 1]T = [$\lambda$ - $\lambda$2 + 1, $\lambda$ - $\lambda$2]T. Since $\lambda$2 = $\lambda$ + 1, both entries are zero. Thus [$\lambda$, 1]T is indeed an eigenvector.

Problem 3: Convert the recurrence Gk+2 = 2Gk+1 + 3Gk with G0 = 1, G1 = 2 into a first-order matrix system. Find the matrix A and the initial vector u0.

Hint: Define uk = [Gk+1, Gk]T and write uk+1 = Auk.

Solution

$u_k = \begin{bmatrix} G_{k+1} \\ G_k \end{bmatrix}^T$. Then $u_{k+1} = \begin{bmatrix} G_{k+2} \\ G_{k+1} \end{bmatrix}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 2G_{k+1} + 3G_k \\ G_{k+1} \end{bmatrix}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}u_k$. So $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ and $u_0 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}^T$.

Problem 4: Explain why $A^k$ → 0 as k → ∞ when all eigenvalues satisfy |$\lambda$i| < 1. What happens if one eigenvalue has |$\lambda$| = 1 and all others have |$\lambda$| < 1?

Hint: Consider the diagonal matrix $\Lambda^k$ and what happens to each diagonal entry as k grows.

Solution

If |$\lambda$i| < 1 for all i, then $\lambda$ik → 0 as k → ∞, so $\Lambda^k$ → 0. Since $A^k$ = $S\Lambda^k S^{-1}$, we have $A^k$ → 0. If one eigenvalue has |$\lambda$| = 1 and others have |$\lambda$| < 1, then $A^k$ does not converge to zero; the term with |$\lambda$| = 1 persists, and $A^k$ approaches a rank-1 projection onto the corresponding eigenvector direction.
