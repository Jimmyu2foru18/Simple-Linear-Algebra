Detailed Topic Breakdowns

The Problem: Systems of Linear Equations

A system of linear equations consists of multiple equations that must be satisfied simultaneously. The fundamental question is: Can we find values for the variables that satisfy all equations at once?

Geometrically, each equation represents a geometric object (line, plane, hyperplane), and the solution is the point(s) where all these objects intersect.

The Matrix Form: $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$

The matrix equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ compactly represents a system of linear equations where:

  • $A$ is the $m \times n$ coefficient matrix containing the coefficients of the variables
  • $\mathbf{x}$ is the $n \times 1$ vector of unknowns we want to solve for
  • $\mathbf{b}$ is the $m \times 1$ right-hand side vector

This form generalizes to any number of equations and variables, not just the 2 or 3 variables we can visualize.

Row Picture vs. Column Picture

Professor Strang emphasizes two essential perspectives:

  • Row Picture: Each row of $A$ represents one equation. We are looking for a point $\mathbf{x}$ that satisfies all equations simultaneously.
  • Column Picture: We are taking linear combinations of the columns of $A$ to produce the vector $\mathbf{b}$. This is fundamental to understanding linear algebra.

Dimensional Space

While we can visualize in 2D and 3D, linear algebra extends seamlessly to $n$-dimensional space. The same principles of linear combinations, span, and solution spaces apply in higher dimensions.

Mathematical Rigor

Derivation: From Equations to Matrix Form

Consider a system of 2 equations with 2 unknowns:

$$a_{11}x + a_{12}y = b_1 \quad \text{(Equation 1)}$$

$$a_{21}x + a_{22}y = b_2 \quad \text{(Equation 2)}$$

This can be written in matrix form as:

$$\begin{bmatrix} a_{11} & a_{12} \\ a_{21} & a_{22} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Multiplying out the matrix product gives:

$$\text{Row 1: } a_{11} \cdot x + a_{12} \cdot y = b_1$$

$$\text{Row 2: } a_{21} \cdot x + a_{22} \cdot y = b_2$$

This shows the equivalence between the system of equations and the matrix equation $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$.

Geometric Interpretation in 2D

For a $2 \times 2$ system, the column picture is:

$$x \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} \\ a_{12} \end{bmatrix} + y \begin{bmatrix} a_{21} \\ a_{22} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

This means: Find scalars $x$ and $y$ such that the linear combination of column vectors equals the target vector $\mathbf{b}$.

The solution exists if and only if $\mathbf{b}$ lies in the span of the columns of $A$ (the column space $C(A)$).

Learning Strategies

Visualization Techniques

Mnemonic: "Rows are Rules, Columns are Choices"

Remember: Each row gives you a constraint (rule), while each column gives you a direction you can move (choice). The solution is finding the right combination of choices that satisfy all rules.

  • Always draw the row picture first for 2D and 3D systems
  • Visualize vectors as arrows from the origin
  • Think of linear combinations as "walking" along vector directions

Study Tips

  • Practice converting between row and column pictures
  • Draw diagrams for every example before solving algebraically
  • Use different colors for different vectors in your diagrams
  • Check solutions by substituting back into the original equations

Visual Aids

Figure: Two Lines Intersecting

The solution to a $2 \times 2$ system is the intersection point of two lines. If the lines are parallel, there is no solution. If they coincide, there are infinitely many solutions.

$$\text{Line 1: } 2x - y = 0 \quad (\text{slope} = 2, \text{ passes through origin})$$

$$\text{Line 2: } x + y = 3 \quad (\text{slope} = -1, \text{ y-intercept} = 3)$$

$$\text{Solution: Intersection at } (x, y) = (2, 1)$$

Figure: Column Picture in 2D

Visualizing the column vectors and their linear combination:

$$\mathbf{v}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{v}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

$$\text{Target: } \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

We need $x$ and $y$ such that $x\mathbf{v}_1 + y\mathbf{v}_2 = \mathbf{b}$. Solving gives $x = 1, y = 2$.

$$\text{Check: } 1\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} + 2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2+2 \\ -1+2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \neq \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

Note: The actual solution requires solving the system $2x + y = 0$ and $-x + y = 3$, which gives $x = -1, y = 2$.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Geometric Interpretation

Problem: Solve the system and interpret geometrically.

$$2x - y = 0$$

$$x + y = 3$$

Solution:

  1. From equation 1: $y = 2x$
  2. Substitute into equation 2: $x + 2x = 3$
  3. Therefore: $3x = 3 \Rightarrow x = 1$
  4. And: $y = 2(1) = 2$

Verification:

  • Equation 1: $2(1) - 2 = 0$ ✓
  • Equation 2: $1 + 2 = 3$ ✓

Geometric Interpretation:

  • First equation: Line through origin with slope $2$
  • Second equation: Line with y-intercept $3$ and slope $-1$
  • Solution $(1, 2)$: The intersection point of the two lines

Example 2: Column Picture

Problem: Solve $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ using the column picture.

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \text{Find } \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ x_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Solution:

We need to find scalars $x_1$ and $x_2$ such that:

$$x_1 \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} + x_2 \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

This gives us the system:

$$2x_1 + x_2 = 5$$

$$-x_1 + x_2 = 1$$

Subtracting the equations: $3x_1 = 4 \Rightarrow x_1 = \frac{4}{3}$

Substituting back: $x_2 = 1 + \frac{4}{3} = \frac{7}{3}$

Verification:

  • $2\left(\frac{4}{3}\right) + \frac{7}{3} = \frac{8}{3} + \frac{7}{3} = \frac{15}{3} = 5$ ✓
  • $-\frac{4}{3} + \frac{7}{3} = \frac{3}{3} = 1$ ✓

Example 3: 3D System Analysis

Problem: Consider a $3 \times 3$ system and analyze its solution space.

$$x + 2y + 3z = 6$$

$$2x + 4y + 6z = 12$$

$$3x + 6y + 9z = 18$$

Analysis:

  • Equation $2 = 2 \times$ Equation $1$
  • Equation $3 = 3 \times$ Equation $1$
  • All three equations represent the same plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$
  • The column space is 1-dimensional (a line)
  • The system has infinitely many solutions forming a plane

The coefficient matrix is:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \\ 3 & 6 & 9 \end{bmatrix}$$

Since $\text{rank}(A) = 1$ and $m = 3$, the column space is a line through the origin in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

Practice Problems

Problem Set 1

Problem 1: Solve the following system geometrically and algebraically:

$$x + y = 4$$

$$x - y = 2$$

Hint: Draw the lines first, then solve algebraically.

Solution

Adding the equations: $2x = 6 \Rightarrow x = 3$

Substituting: $3 + y = 4 \Rightarrow y = 1$

Solution: $(3, 1)$

Geometric: Two lines intersecting at $(3, 1)$.

Problem 2: For the system below, determine if there is a unique solution, no solution, or infinitely many solutions:

$$x + 2y = 3$$

$$2x + 4y = 7$$

Hint: Check if the lines are parallel, coincident, or intersecting.

Solution

Equation $2 = 2 \times$ Equation $1$ would require $2 \times 3 = 7$, but $6 \neq 7$.

The lines are parallel with different intercepts: No solution.

Problem 3: Write the following system in matrix form $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$:

$$3x - y + 2z = 5$$

$$x + y - z = 1$$

Hint: Identify the coefficient matrix $A$, the unknown vector $\mathbf{x}$, and the right-hand side $\mathbf{b}$.

Solution

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & -1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Problem 4: In the column picture, interpret geometrically what it means for $\mathbf{b}$ to NOT be in the column space of $A$.

Solution

It means no linear combination of the columns of $A$ can produce $\mathbf{b}$. Geometrically, the target vector $\mathbf{b}$ points in a direction that cannot be reached by combining the column vectors. This occurs when the columns of $A$ do not span the full space.

Problem 5: For the system $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ below, find the solution using both the row picture and the column picture:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}$$

Solution

Row Picture: Solve the system $x + 2y = 5$ and $3x + 4y = 11$. From the first equation, $x = 5 - 2y$. Substituting into the second: $3(5 - 2y) + 4y = 11 \Rightarrow 15 - 6y + 4y = 11 \Rightarrow -2y = -4 \Rightarrow y = 2$. Then $x = 5 - 2(2) = 1$. Solution: $(1, 2)$.

Column Picture: Find $x$ and $y$ such that $x\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} + y\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}$. This gives the same system and solution $(1, 2)$.
