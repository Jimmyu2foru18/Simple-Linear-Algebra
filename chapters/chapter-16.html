Chapter 16: Projection Matrices and Least Squares

Solving least squares problems through the normal equations. This chapter completes the numerical solution of the projection problem. It verifies the projection and error vectors. It proves that $A^T A$ is invertible when $A$ has independent columns, and introduces orthonormal vectors as the ideal basis for computation.

Detailed Topic Breakdowns

The Normal Equations: Numerical Solution

For the line-fitting problem with points $(1,1), (2,2), (3,2)$:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$

Computing $A^T A$ gives $\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}$, and $A^T \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}$.

The normal equations are $3C + 6D = 5$ and $6C + 14D = 11$.

Solving by elimination: subtract $2 \times$ (first equation) from the second to get $2D = 1$, so $D = 1/2$. Back-substituting gives $3C + 3 = 5$, so $C = 2/3$.

The best-fit line is $b = \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{2}t$.

Verification via Calculus

The same result comes from minimizing the total squared error directly.

The error function is

$$E(C,D) = (C+D-1)^2 + (C+2D-2)^2 + (C+3D-2)^2$$

Taking partial derivatives with respect to $C$ and $D$ and setting them to zero yields exactly the normal equations.

The beauty of least squares is that minimizing a sum of squares always produces linear equations, regardless of the data.

The Projection and Error Vectors

With $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = [2/3, 1/2]^T$, the projection vector is

$$\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = [7/6, 5/3, 13/6]^T \approx [1.167, 1.667, 2.167]^T$$

The error vector is

$$\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p} = [-1/6, 1/3, -1/6]^T \approx [-0.167, 0.333, -0.167]^T$$

The key facts: $\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b}$, $\mathbf{p}$ lies in the column space of $A$, $\mathbf{e}$ lies in the null space of $A^T$, and $\mathbf{p} \perp \mathbf{e}$.

The dot product

$$\mathbf{p}^T\mathbf{e} = (7/6)(-1/6) + (5/3)(1/3) + (13/6)(-1/6) = -7/36 + 10/36 - 13/36 = 0$$

confirms orthogonality.

Properties of $A^T A$

The matrix $A^T A$ has three crucial properties: it is always symmetric, it is always invertible when $A$ has independent columns, and it is positive definite.

Symmetry holds because $(A^T A)^T = A^T A$.

Invertibility is proved by showing that if $A^T A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, then

$$\mathbf{x}^T A^T A \mathbf{x} = (A\mathbf{x})^T(A\mathbf{x}) = |A\mathbf{x}|^2 = 0$$

so $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, and since columns of $A$ are independent, $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$.

Thus $A^T A$ has trivial null space and is invertible.

Orthonormal Vectors: The Best Columns

When columns of $A$ are perpendicular unit vectors (orthonormal), then $A^T A = I$.

This is the ideal case: the normal equations become $I\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$, so $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$. No matrix inversion is needed.

Examples of orthonormal vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$: $[1, 0]^T$ and $[0, 1]^T$, or $[\cos\theta, \sin\theta]^T$ and $[-\sin\theta, \cos\theta]^T$ for any angle $\theta$. These are perpendicular unit vectors, and their Gram matrix $A^T A$ is the identity.

Computation Examples

Derivation: Numerical Solution of the Normal Equations

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$:

$$A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}$$ $$A^T \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}$$

Solving $A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} C \\ D \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}$$

Elimination: row 2 $- 2 \times$ row 1 gives $2D = 1$, so $D = 1/2$. Then $3C + 6(1/2) = 5$ gives $3C = 2$, so $C = 2/3$. Best-fit line: $b = \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{2}t$.

Derivation: Calculus Verification via Partial Derivatives

Minimize

$$E(C,D) = (C+D-1)^2 + (C+2D-2)^2 + (C+3D-2)^2$$ $$\frac{\partial E}{\partial C} = 2(C+D-1) + 2(C+2D-2) + 2(C+3D-2) = 6C + 12D - 10 = 0$$

Dividing by 2: $3C + 6D = 5$.

$$\frac{\partial E}{\partial D} = 2(C+D-1) + 4(C+2D-2) + 6(C+3D-2) = 12C + 28D - 22 = 0$$

Dividing by 2: $6C + 14D = 11$. These are exactly the normal equations.

Derivation: Proof that $A^T A$ is Invertible

Suppose $A$ has independent columns. We prove $A^T A$ is invertible by showing its null space is $\{\mathbf{0}\}$.

Assume $A^T A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. Multiply by $\mathbf{x}^T$ on the left:

$$\mathbf{x}^T A^T A \mathbf{x} = (A\mathbf{x})^T(A\mathbf{x}) = |A\mathbf{x}|^2 = 0$$

Since $|A\mathbf{x}|^2 = 0$, we have $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. Because $A$ has independent columns, $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ implies $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. Therefore the only solution to $A^T A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ is $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, so $A^T A$ is invertible.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "Normal Equations: $A^T A \hat{x} = A^T b$"

The normal equations always have this form. $A^T A$ is square and symmetric. The right-hand side is $A^T \mathbf{b}$. Solve for $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ to get the least squares solution.

Mnemonic: "Error in $N(A^T)$, Projection in $C(A)$"

The error vector $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ satisfies $A^T\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{0}$, so $\mathbf{e} \in N(A^T)$. The projection $\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ is in $C(A)$. They are orthogonal complements: $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{e}$ with $\mathbf{p} \perp \mathbf{e}$.

Mnemonic: "Orthonormal: Perpendicular Unit Vectors"

Orthonormal vectors are perpendicular and have length 1. For orthonormal columns, $A^T A = I$. This is the best possible case: no matrix inversion needed, just $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$.

Study Tips

  • Always check that $A^T A$ is symmetric before solving — it should be
  • Compute $A^T A$ and $A^T \mathbf{b}$ carefully; a mistake here propagates everywhere
  • Verify your solution by checking $A^T A \hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$
  • The error vector should be perpendicular to every column of $A$ — test with dot products
  • For the proof of invertibility, the key step is $\mathbf{x}^T A^T A \mathbf{x} = |A\mathbf{x}|^2$
  • Orthonormal columns make everything simple: $A^T A = I$ and $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$

Visual Aids

Figure: The Complete Least Squares Solution

For points $(1,1), (2,2), (3,2)$ fitted to $b = C + Dt$:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \hat{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 2/3 \\ 1/2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$ $$\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \begin{bmatrix} 7/6 \\ 5/3 \\ 13/6 \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix} -1/6 \\ 1/3 \\ -1/6 \end{bmatrix}$$

Check: $\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{e} = [7/6 - 1/6, 5/3 + 1/3, 13/6 - 1/6]^T = [1, 2, 2]^T = \mathbf{b}$. And $\mathbf{p}^T\mathbf{e} = -7/36 + 10/36 - 13/36 = 0$.

Figure: Error Decomposition and Orthogonality

The error vector $\mathbf{e}$ is perpendicular not just to the projection $\mathbf{p}$, but to every vector in the column space:

$$\mathbf{e} \perp \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}: \quad \mathbf{e}^T\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = -\frac{1}{6} + \frac{2}{6} - \frac{1}{6} = 0$$ $$\mathbf{e} \perp \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}: \quad \mathbf{e}^T\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = -\frac{1}{6} + \frac{4}{6} - \frac{3}{6} = 0$$

Since $\mathbf{e} \in N(A^T)$, it is orthogonal to every linear combination of the columns of $A$.

Figure: Orthonormal Vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$

The absolute favorite pair of orthonormal vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$:

$$\mathbf{q}_1 = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta \\ \sin\theta \end{bmatrix}, \quad \mathbf{q}_2 = \begin{bmatrix} -\sin\theta \\ \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$$

For $\theta = 0$: $\mathbf{q}_1 = [1, 0]^T$, $\mathbf{q}_2 = [0, 1]^T$ (the standard basis). For any $\theta$, $\mathbf{q}_1^T\mathbf{q}_2 = -\cos\theta\sin\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta = 0$ and $|\mathbf{q}_1| = |\mathbf{q}_2| = 1$. The Gram matrix $A^T A = I$.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Complete Numerical Solution

Problem: Solve the normal equations for $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$, $\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$ by both elimination and calculus. Verify orthogonality of $\mathbf{p}$ and $\mathbf{e}$.

Solution:

  • $$A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{bmatrix}, \quad A^T \mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 11 \end{bmatrix}$$
  • Elimination: $2D = 1 \Rightarrow D = 1/2$, then $3C + 3 = 5 \Rightarrow C = 2/3$
  • Best-fit line: $b = \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{2}t$
  • Calculus: $\partial E/\partial C = 6C + 12D - 10 = 0$, $\partial E/\partial D = 12C + 28D - 22 = 0$ — same equations
  • $$\mathbf{p} = [7/6, 5/3, 13/6]^T, \quad \mathbf{e} = [-1/6, 1/3, -1/6]^T$$
  • $$\mathbf{p}^T\mathbf{e} = -7/36 + 10/36 - 13/36 = 0$$
  • $$\mathbf{e}^T\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = 0 \text{ and } \mathbf{e}^T\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix} = 0$$

Example 2: Error Vector in $N(A^T)$

Problem: Verify that the error vector $\mathbf{e} = [-1/6, 1/3, -1/6]^T$ lies in the null space of $A^T$ for the matrix above.

Solution:

  • $$A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$$
  • $$A^T \mathbf{e} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} -1/6 \\ 1/3 \\ -1/6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -1/6 + 2/6 - 1/6 \\ -1/6 + 4/6 - 3/6 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
  • Therefore $\mathbf{e} \in N(A^T)$

Example 3: Proof that $A^T A$ is Invertible

Problem: Prove that if $A$ has independent columns, then $A^T A$ is invertible.

Solution:

  • To show $A^T A$ is invertible, show its null space is $\{\mathbf{0}\}$
  • Assume $A^T A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$
  • Multiply by $\mathbf{x}^T$: $$\mathbf{x}^T A^T A \mathbf{x} = (A\mathbf{x})^T(A\mathbf{x}) = |A\mathbf{x}|^2 = 0$$
  • Thus $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$
  • Since $A$ has independent columns, $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ implies $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$
  • Therefore $N(A^T A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ and $A^T A$ is invertible

Example 4: Orthonormal Vectors Make $A^T A = I$

Problem: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Verify that the columns are orthonormal and compute $A^T A$. Then let $A = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$ and verify $A^T A = I$.

Solution:

  • First $A$: columns are $[1,0,0]^T$ and $[0,1,0]^T$. They are perpendicular and unit length.
  • $$A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I$$
  • Second $A$ (rotation matrix): $$A^T = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$$
  • $$A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} \cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta & -\cos\theta\sin\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta \\ -\sin\theta\cos\theta + \cos\theta\sin\theta & \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I$$

Problem 2: Minimize $E(C,D) = (C+D-1)^2 + (C+2D-2)^2 + (C+3D-2)^2$ using calculus. Show that the critical point gives the normal equations.

Hint: Compute $\partial E/\partial C$ and $\partial E/\partial D$, set both to zero, and compare with Problem 1.

$$\frac{\partial E}{\partial C} = 2(C+D-1) + 2(C+2D-2) + 2(C+3D-2) = 6C + 12D - 10 = 0$$

$$\frac{\partial E}{\partial D} = 2(C+D-1) + 4(C+2D-2) + 6(C+3D-2) = 12C + 28D - 22 = 0$$

Dividing by 2: $3C + 6D = 5$ and $6C + 14D = 11$ — exactly the normal equations.

Problem 3: Compute the projection vector $\mathbf{p}$ and error vector $\mathbf{e}$ for the line-fitting problem. Verify that $\mathbf{p} \perp \mathbf{e}$, $\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b}$, and $\mathbf{e} \perp [1,1,1]^T$.

Hint: $\mathbf{p} = A\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ and $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{p}$.

Solution

$$\mathbf{p} = [7/6, 5/3, 13/6]^T, \quad \mathbf{e} = [-1/6, 1/3, -1/6]^T$$

$$\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{e} = [1, 2, 2]^T = \mathbf{b}$$

$$\mathbf{p}^T\mathbf{e} = -7/36 + 10/36 - 13/36 = 0$$

$$\mathbf{e}^T\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = -1/6 + 2/6 - 1/6 = 0$$

Problem 4: Prove that $A^T A$ is invertible if $A$ has independent columns. Use the key step $\mathbf{x}^T A^T A \mathbf{x} = |A\mathbf{x}|^2$.

Hint: Suppose $A^T A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. What does $\mathbf{x}^T A^T A \mathbf{x}$ equal? Why must this imply $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$?

Solution

If $A^T A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, then $\mathbf{x}^T A^T A \mathbf{x} = 0$, so $(A\mathbf{x})^T(A\mathbf{x}) = |A\mathbf{x}|^2 = 0$. Thus $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. Since $A$ has independent columns, $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$ implies $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$. So $N(A^T A) = \{\mathbf{0}\}$ and $A^T A$ is invertible.

Problem 5: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}$. Show that the columns of $A$ are orthonormal and that $A^T A = I$. What does this mean for solving $A\hat{\mathbf{x}} \approx \mathbf{b}$?

Hint: Compute the dot product of the two columns and their lengths. Compute $A^T A$ directly.

Solution

Column 1: $[\cos\theta, \sin\theta]^T$, length $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$. Column 2: $[-\sin\theta, \cos\theta]^T$, length $\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$. Dot product: $-\cos\theta\sin\theta + \sin\theta\cos\theta = 0$. Columns are orthonormal.

$$A^T = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & \sin\theta \\ -\sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}, \quad \text{so } A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I$$

For $A\hat{\mathbf{x}} \approx \mathbf{b}$, the normal equations give $I\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$, so $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = A^T \mathbf{b}$. No matrix inversion needed.
