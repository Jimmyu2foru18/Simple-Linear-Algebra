Detailed Topic Breakdowns

The LU Factorization

When we perform Gaussian elimination without row exchanges, the process naturally produces a factorization $A = LU$, where $L$ is a lower triangular matrix containing the multipliers used during elimination, and $U$ is the resulting upper triangular matrix.

The L Matrix

$L$ is lower triangular with ones on the diagonal. Its subdiagonal entries $\ell_{ij}$ are exactly the multipliers used to eliminate the entries below the pivots during Gaussian elimination. $L$ captures the row operations in compact form.

Computational Cost

The cost of Gaussian elimination for an $n \times n$ matrix is approximately $n^3/3$ operations. This is far more efficient than computing $A^{-1}$ (which costs $n^3$ operations) and then multiplying by $\mathbf{b}$.

Permutation Matrices

When row exchanges are needed, we introduce permutation matrices $P$. The factorization becomes $PA = LU$, where $P$ reorders the rows of $A$ to enable safe elimination. $P$ is orthogonal: $P^{-1} = P^T$.

Computation Examples

Derivation: Constructing $L$ from Multipliers

For a $3 \times 3$ system, suppose the multipliers are $\ell_{21}, \ell_{31}, \ell_{32}$. The elimination matrices multiply as:

$$E_{32} E_{31} E_{21} A = U$$

Therefore:

$$A = E_{21}^{-1} E_{31}^{-1} E_{32}^{-1} U = LU$$

Where $L = E_{21}^{-1} E_{31}^{-1} E_{32}^{-1}$ is lower triangular with the multipliers in the appropriate positions.

Derivation: Solving $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ via LU

Once $A = LU$, solving $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ becomes a two-step process:

$$\text{Step 1: Forward substitution } L\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b} \text{ (solve for } \mathbf{y}\text{)}$$ $$\text{Step 2: Back substitution } U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y} \text{ (solve for } \mathbf{x}\text{)}$$

Forward substitution is efficient because $L$ is lower triangular. Each step requires only one division and a few subtractions.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "L Stores the Lifts"

$L$ contains the multipliers that "lift" the lower entries to zero during elimination. The entry $\ell_{ij}$ tells you how much of row $i$ to subtract from row $j$.

Mnemonic: "Forward then Back"

LU decomposition splits one solve into two easy solves: first go down through $L$ (forward), then up through $U$ (backward). Each is triangular and fast.

Study Tips

  • Practice computing $L$ by hand for small $3 \times 3$ matrices
  • Verify $LU = A$ after computing both factors
  • Understand that row exchanges require $P$: $PA = LU$
  • Note that computing $A^{-1}$ costs ~$3 \times$ more than solving $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ multiple times
  • Recognize when a matrix cannot be factored without permutations

Worked Examples

Example 1: $2 \times 2$ LU Factorization

Problem: Factor $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & -6 \end{bmatrix}$ into $LU$.

Solution:

  1. Pivot: $a_{11} = 2$
  2. Multiplier: $\ell_{21} = 4/2 = 2$
  3. Eliminate: $R_2 - 2R_1 = [0, -6-2] = [0, -8]$
  4. $U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & -8 \end{bmatrix}$
  5. $L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$
  6. Verify: $LU = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 4 & -6 \end{bmatrix} = A$ ✓

Example 2: $3 \times 3$ LU Factorization

Problem: Factor $A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solution:

  1. Pivot $a_{11} = 2$, multiplier $\ell_{21} = 1/2 = 0.5$
  2. $R_2 - 0.5R_1 = [0, 2.5, 1]$
  3. $U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2.5 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 3 \end{bmatrix}$
  4. Next pivot $u_{22} = 2.5$, multiplier $\ell_{32} = 1/2.5 = 0.4$
  5. $R_3 - 0.4R_2 = [0, 0, 2.6]$
  6. Final $U = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2.5 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 2.6 \end{bmatrix}$
  7. $L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0.5 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0.4 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

Example 3: Solving $A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$ via LU

Problem: Use the LU factors from Example 1 to solve $A\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$.

Solution:

  1. Forward solve $L\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}$: $y_1 = 1$, $2 \cdot 1 + y_2 = 5 \Rightarrow y_2 = 3$
  2. Back solve $U\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}$: $-8x_2 = 3 \Rightarrow x_2 = -3/8$
  3. $2x_1 + (-3/8) = 1 \Rightarrow x_1 = 11/16$

Practice Problems

Problem Set 4

Problem 1: Factor $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 7 \end{bmatrix}$ into $LU$.

Hint: Find the multiplier for eliminating the $(2,1)$ entry.

Solution

$\ell_{21} = 2/1 = 2$

$U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

$L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

Problem 2: For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, find the LU factorization.

Hint: Two pivots needed; track both multipliers.

Solution

$\ell_{21} = 2$, $\ell_{31} = 1$

After first column elimination: $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

$\ell_{32} = 1$

$U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$

$L = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 2 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$

Problem 3: Show that if $A$ is singular, the LU factorization (without permutations) fails.

Hint: What happens when a pivot is zero?

Solution

If $A$ is singular, elimination produces a zero pivot. Without row exchange, we cannot proceed. A permutation matrix $P$ reorders rows: $PA = LU$.

Problem 4: Compute the number of multiplications needed to factor an $n \times n$ matrix into $LU$ (no permutations).

Hint: Count operations in the elimination loop.

Solution

Approximately $n^3/3$ multiplications/divisions and a similar number of additions/subtractions. Total $\sim 2n^3/3$ flops.
