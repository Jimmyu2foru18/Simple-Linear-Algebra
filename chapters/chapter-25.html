Detailed Topic Breakdowns

Cofactor Expansion and Determinant Recurrences

Cofactor expansion along the first row of a tri-diagonal matrix of ones produces a recurrence relation for the determinant.

For the n × n matrix An with 1s on the main diagonal and the first super-diagonal and sub-diagonal, expanding along row 1 gives:

$$ D_n = D_{n-1} - D_{n-2} $$

The base cases are D1 = 1 and D2 = 0. This recurrence is similar to the Fibonacci recurrence but with a minus sign, and it produces a periodic sequence with period 6: 1, 0, -1, -1, 0, 1, 1, 0, ...

From Recurrence to Matrix Powers

Any second-order recurrence can be written as a first-order matrix system. For Dn = Dn-1 - Dn-2, define the state vector:

$$ u_n = \begin{bmatrix} D_n \\ D_{n-1} \end{bmatrix} $$

Then un = Aun-1 where:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

This converts the scalar recurrence into a matrix system that can be analyzed with eigenvalues and eigenvectors. The matrix A encodes the recurrence in first-order form.

Complex Eigenvalues and Periodicity

The characteristic equation of:

$$ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

is:

$$ \lambda^2 - \lambda + 1 = 0 $$

The eigenvalues are:

$$ \lambda = \frac{1 \pm i\sqrt{3}}{2} $$

These are complex numbers on the unit circle: their magnitude is:

$$ \sqrt{(1/2)^2 + (\sqrt{3}/2)^2} = 1 $$

In polar form:

$$ \lambda = e^{\pm i\pi/3} $$

Since |λ| = 1, the powers λ6 = (e±iπ/3)6 = e±2πi = 1. Therefore A6 = I, and the sequence repeats with period 6.

The determinants cycle: D1=1, D2=0, D3=-1, D4=-1, D5=0, D6=1, D7=1, ...

A Family of Symmetric Matrices

The lecture introduces a family of symmetric matrices An with zeros on the diagonal and increasing integers on the off-diagonals.

For n=3:

$$ A_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} $$

This matrix is singular because column 3 is twice column 1. Its eigenvalues are 0, √5, -√5.

For n=4: A4 follows the same pattern but with entries 1, 2, 3 on the off-diagonals. Unlike A3, A4 is invertible with determinant 9. Since A4 is invertible, its column space is all of R4, and the projection matrix onto the column space is the identity matrix I4.

A pattern emerges: odd-indexed matrices are singular, even-indexed matrices are invertible.

Projection Matrix onto the Column Space

For any matrix A, the projection matrix onto the column space C(A) is:

$$ P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T $$

provided ATA is invertible. This requires that A has independent columns.

If A is square and invertible, then C(A) = Rn and P = I.

For A4, which is invertible, the projection matrix onto its column space is simply the 4×4 identity matrix.

For A3, which is singular, the column space is a 2-dimensional subspace of R3, and the projection matrix is a 3×3 matrix of rank 2.

Computation Examples

Derivation: Cofactor Expansion and the Determinant Recurrence

For the n × n tri-diagonal matrix of ones An with 1s on the main diagonal and the first super/sub-diagonals, expand det(An) along the first row. The first entry is 1 with cofactor Dn-1 (the determinant of the (n-1) × (n-1) submatrix). The second entry is 1 with cofactor -det(B) where B is the matrix obtained by deleting row 1 and column 2. But B has a first column that is all zeros except for a single 1, so expanding B along its first column gives Dn-2 with a positive sign. The overall sign from row 1, column 2 is (-1)1+2 = -1. Thus:

$$ D_n = 1 \cdot D_{n-1} - 1 \cdot D_{n-2} = D_{n-1} - D_{n-2} $$

With D1 = 1 and D2 = 0, the sequence is 1, 0, -1, -1, 0, 1, 1, 0, ...

Derivation: Eigenvalues of the Recurrence Matrix

For $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the characteristic equation is:

$$\det(A - \lambda I) = \det\begin{bmatrix} 1-\lambda & -1 \\ 1 & -\lambda \end{bmatrix} = (1-\lambda)(-\lambda) + 1 = \lambda^2 - \lambda + 1 = 0$$

Using the quadratic formula:

$$\lambda = \frac{1 \pm \sqrt{1-4}}{2} = \frac{1 \pm i\sqrt{3}}{2}$$

These are $e^{\pm i\pi/3}$ in polar form. The magnitude is $\sqrt{(1/2)^2 + (\sqrt{3}/2)^2} = 1$. Since $|\lambda| = 1$, the powers $\lambda^6 = 1$, so $A^6 = I$. The sequence of determinants has period 6.

Derivation: Eigenvalues and Singularity of the Matrix Family $A_n$

For $A_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the characteristic equation is $\det(A_3 - \lambda I) = -\lambda(\lambda^2 - 5) = 0$. The eigenvalues are $\lambda = 0, \pm\sqrt{5}$. Since $\lambda = 0$ is an eigenvalue, $A_3$ is singular. Column 3 is $[0, 2, 0]^T = 2 \times [0, 1, 0]^T$, confirming singularity. The trace is 0 (matching $0 + \sqrt{5} - \sqrt{5} = 0$) and the determinant is 0 (matching $0 \cdot \sqrt{5} \cdot (-\sqrt{5}) = 0$). For $A_4$, which follows the same pattern with entries 1, 2, 3, the matrix is invertible with determinant 9. Since $A_4$ is invertible, $C(A_4) = \mathbb{R}^4$ and the projection matrix is $P = I_4$.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "Cofactor Along First Row: Expand and Recurse"

When expanding a tri-diagonal determinant along the first row, the first entry gives $D_{n-1}$ and the second gives $-D_{n-2}$. The sign alternates, producing the recurrence $D_n = D_{n-1} - D_{n-2}$.

Mnemonic: "Complex on Unit Circle Means Periodic"

If eigenvalues are complex with magnitude 1, they lie on the unit circle: $\lambda = e^{i\theta}$. Then $\lambda^{2\pi/\theta} = 1$, so the matrix raised to that power is the identity. For $\lambda = e^{\pm i\pi/3}$, the period is 6.

Mnemonic: "Invertible Matrix Projects onto Whole Space"

If $A$ is invertible, $C(A) = \mathbb{R}^n$ and the projection matrix onto $C(A)$ is the identity $I$. Only singular matrices have nontrivial projection matrices onto proper subspaces.

Study Tips

  • For determinant recurrences, always verify with the first few terms: $D_1, D_2, D_3, \ldots$
  • When converting a recurrence to a matrix system, the state vector must contain enough history
  • For complex eigenvalues $\lambda = \alpha + i\beta$, compute magnitude as $\sqrt{\alpha^2 + \beta^2}$; if magnitude is 1, look for periodicity
  • Check trace and determinant as sanity checks for eigenvalues: trace = sum, det = product
  • For a symmetric matrix, eigenvalues are always real—if you get complex eigenvalues, recheck your work
  • A matrix is singular if and only if 0 is an eigenvalue; check this before computing inverses or projections

Visual Aids

Figure: Determinant Recurrence and Period-6 Cycle

The tri-diagonal determinant recurrence $D_n = D_{n-1} - D_{n-2}$ with $D_1 = 1, D_2 = 0$ produces a periodic sequence:

$$D_1=1, D_2=0, D_3=-1, D_4=-1, D_5=0, D_6=1, D_7=1, D_8=0, \ldots$$

The sequence repeats every 6 terms. This reflects the fact that the recurrence matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ satisfies $A^6 = I$.

Figure: Eigenvalues on the Unit Circle

The eigenvalues $\lambda = \frac{1 \pm i\sqrt{3}}{2}$ lie on the unit circle in the complex plane at angles $\pm\pi/3$:

$$\lambda = e^{\pm i\pi/3} = \cos(\pi/3) \pm i\sin(\pi/3) = \frac{1}{2} \pm i\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$

Since $|\lambda| = 1$, the powers cycle with period 6: $\lambda^6 = 1$. The matrix $A^6 = I$ means applying the recurrence 6 times returns to the starting state.

Figure: Singular vs Invertible in the Matrix Family $A_n$

The family of symmetric matrices $A_n$ shows a clear pattern:

$A_3$: singular ($\det = 0$), eigenvalues $0, \pm\sqrt{5}$, column space is 2D in $\mathbb{R}^3$

$A_4$: invertible ($\det = 9$), column space is $\mathbb{R}^4$, projection matrix $P = I_4$

Odd-indexed matrices are singular; even-indexed matrices are invertible. The projection onto the column space of an invertible matrix is always the identity.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Determinant Recurrence and Periodicity

Problem: For the tri-diagonal matrix of ones $A_n$, the determinant satisfies $D_n = D_{n-1} - D_{n-2}$ with $D_1 = 1, D_2 = 0$. Find $D_3$ through $D_{10}$, verify the period is 6, and explain why using the recurrence matrix.

Solution:

  • $D_3 = D_2 - D_1 = 0 - 1 = -1$
  • $D_4 = D_3 - D_2 = -1 - 0 = -1$
  • $D_5 = D_4 - D_3 = -1 - (-1) = 0$
  • $D_6 = D_5 - D_4 = 0 - (-1) = 1$
  • $D_7 = D_6 - D_5 = 1 - 0 = 1$
  • $D_8 = D_7 - D_6 = 1 - 1 = 0$
  • Period is 6: $1, 0, -1, -1, 0, 1$ repeats
  • Recurrence matrix: $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, eigenvalues $\lambda = e^{\pm i\pi/3}$
  • $|\lambda| = 1$ and $\lambda^6 = 1$, so $A^6 = I$, confirming period 6

Example 2: Eigenvalues of a Singular Symmetric Matrix

Problem: For $A_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, find the eigenvalues, verify the trace and determinant, and explain why it is singular.

Solution:

  • Characteristic equation: $\det(A_3 - \lambda I) = -\lambda(\lambda^2 - 5) = 0$
  • Eigenvalues: $\lambda_1 = 0, \lambda_2 = \sqrt{5}, \lambda_3 = -\sqrt{5}$
  • Trace: $0 + 0 + 0 = 0$, matches sum of eigenvalues $0 + \sqrt{5} - \sqrt{5} = 0$
  • Determinant: $0 \cdot \sqrt{5} \cdot (-\sqrt{5}) = 0$, matrix is singular
  • Geometric reason: column 3 = $2 \times$ column 1, so columns are dependent
  • Eigenvector for $\lambda = 0$: null space of $A_3$ is span$\{[2, 0, -1]^T\}$ (row 2 gives $x_1 + 2x_3 = 0$)

Example 3: Projection Matrix for an Invertible Matrix

Problem: Explain why the projection matrix onto the column space of $A_4$ is the identity matrix, and describe what this means geometrically.

Solution:

  • $A_4$ is invertible (determinant = 9)
  • For an invertible $n \times n$ matrix, $C(A) = \mathbb{R}^n$
  • The projection matrix onto $\mathbb{R}^n$ is $P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$
  • Since $A$ is invertible, $A^T A$ is invertible, and $P = A(A^T A)^{-1} A^T = I$
  • Geometrically: projecting any vector $b$ onto the whole space $\mathbb{R}^n$ leaves $b$ unchanged
  • Every vector is already in the column space, so projection does nothing

Practice Problems

Problem Set 25

Problem 1: For the determinant recurrence $D_n = D_{n-1} - D_{n-2}$ with $D_1 = 1, D_2 = 0$, compute $D_3$ through $D_8$. Prove by induction that the sequence has period 6.

Hint: Compute the first 8 terms and look for the pattern $1, 0, -1, -1, 0, 1$ repeating.

Solution

$D_3 = -1, D_4 = -1, D_5 = 0, D_6 = 1, D_7 = 1, D_8 = 0$. The sequence is $1, 0, -1, -1, 0, 1, 1, 0, \ldots$ which has period 6. The recurrence matrix $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $e^{\pm i\pi/3}$, so $A^6 = I$, confirming the period.

Problem 2: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Find its eigenvalues, compute $A^6$, and verify that $A^6 = I$.

Hint: The eigenvalues are $e^{\pm i\pi/3}$. Use the diagonalization $A = S\Lambda S^{-1}$ to compute $A^6 = S\Lambda^6 S^{-1}$.

Solution

Eigenvalues: $\lambda = \frac{1 \pm i\sqrt{3}}{2} = e^{\pm i\pi/3}$. Since $\lambda^6 = (e^{\pm i\pi/3})^6 = e^{\pm 2\pi i} = 1$, we have $\Lambda^6 = I$. Thus $A^6 = S I S^{-1} = I$. The matrix raised to the 6th power is the identity.

Problem 3: For $A_3 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, verify that it is singular by finding a nontrivial vector in its null space. Then find all eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

Hint: Column 3 is a multiple of column 1. Use this to find the null space directly.

Solution

Column 3 = 2 — column 1, so columns are dependent and $\det(A_3) = 0$. Null space: solve $A_3x = 0$. From row 2: $x_1 + 2x_3 = 0$, so $x_1 = -2x_3$. Let $x_3 = 1$, then $x_1 = -2$, and $x_2 = 0$ from row 1. Null space basis: $[-2, 0, 1]^T$. Eigenvalues: characteristic polynomial is $-\lambda(\lambda^2 - 5) = 0$, giving $\lambda = 0, \pm\sqrt{5}$. Eigenvector for $\lambda = 0$ is $[-2, 0, 1]^T$. For $\lambda = \sqrt{5}$: solve $(A_3 - \sqrt{5}I)x = 0$, giving $[1, \sqrt{5}-1, 2]^T$ (up to scaling). For $\lambda = -\sqrt{5}$: $[1, -\sqrt{5}-1, 2]^T$.

Problem 4: Explain why, for any invertible matrix $A$, the projection matrix onto $C(A)$ is the identity. Then give an example of a singular matrix $B$ where the projection matrix onto $C(B)$ is NOT the identity, and describe what $C(B)$ is.

Hint: If $A$ is invertible, what is its column space? If $B$ is singular, the column space is a proper subspace.

Solution

If $A$ is invertible, its columns are independent and span $\mathbb{R}^n$, so $C(A) = \mathbb{R}^n$. The projection matrix onto $\mathbb{R}^n$ is $I$, since projecting any vector onto the whole space leaves it unchanged. For a singular matrix $B$, $C(B)$ is a proper subspace. For example, $B = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$ has $C(B) = \text{span}\{[1, 2]^T\}$, a line in $\mathbb{R}^2$. The projection matrix onto this line is $P = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \neq I_2$.
