Chapter 34: Pseudo-Inverses and Projections

This chapter resolves the puzzle of what to do when a matrix is not invertible. We begin by reviewing the special cases where a matrix has a one-sided inverse—either a left inverse when the columns are independent or a right inverse when the rows are independent. In each case, the product of the matrix and its one-sided inverse turns out to be a projection matrix, not the identity. The real subject is the general case: a rank-$r$ matrix with $r < m$ and $r < n$, where both the null space and the left null space are nontrivial. Between these null spaces, the matrix acts as a perfect invertible map from the $r$-dimensional row space to the $r$-dimensional column space. The pseudo-inverse $A^+$ is precisely that inverse on the good subspaces, extended by zero on the null spaces. We show how to construct $A^+$ directly from the singular value decomposition, and why statisticians need it for linear regression when $A^T A$ is singular. The chapter closes with the elegant property that $AA^+$ and $A^+A$ are orthogonal projections onto the column space and row space, respectively.

Inverse Matrices

Left Inverses and Full Column Rank

When $A$ is $m \times n$ with full column rank ($\text{rank}(A) = n$), the columns are linearly independent. The null space contains only the zero vector.

In this case $A^T A$ is invertible. The matrix $(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ is a left inverse:

$$A^T A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T = A$$

Multiplying in the opposite order gives $A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T$. This is not the identity.

$$A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T$$

It is an $m \times m$ matrix that projects onto the $n$-dimensional column space of $A$. It acts as the identity on the column space and as zero on the left null space.

Right Inverses and Full Row Rank

When $A$ has full row rank ($\text{rank}(A) = m$), the rows are independent. $A A^T$ is invertible.

The matrix $A^T (A A^T)^{-1}$ is a right inverse:

$$A^T (A A^T)^{-1} A = I_n$$

If we reverse the order, we get $A^T (A A^T)^{-1} A$. This is an $n \times n$ matrix that projects onto the $m$-dimensional row space of $A$. It is the identity on the row space and zero on the null space.

The Four Subspaces in the General Case

For a general matrix $A$ of rank $r < m$ and $r < n$, the four fundamental subspaces have dimensions: column space $r$, null space $n-r$, row space $r$, left null space $m-r$.

The matrix $A$ maps the row space bijectively onto the column space. It kills everything in the null space.

Similarly, $A^T$ maps the column space bijectively onto the row space. It kills the left null space.

This one-to-one correspondence between the row and column spaces is the key to defining a pseudo-inverse.

The Pseudo-Inverse: Definition and Motivation

The Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse, denoted $A^+$, is the matrix that inverts $A$ on the row space and column space. It is zero on the null spaces.

Formally, $A^+$ satisfies four Penrose conditions. The essential idea is simple: if $x$ is in the row space and $y = Ax$ is in the column space, then $A^+ y = x$.

$$A^+ y = x$$

The pseudo-inverse brings every vector in the column space back to its unique pre-image in the row space. If a vector has any component in the left null space, $A^+$ discards it because that component has no source in the row space.

Statisticians discovered the pseudo-inverse because it solves the least-squares problem $A^T A x = A^T b$ even when $A^T A$ is singular.

$$A^T A x = A^T b$$

This happens whenever the columns of $A$ are linearly dependent, as in repeated experiments.

Proof: $A$ Is Invertible from Row Space to Column Space

Suppose $x$ and $y$ are two different vectors in the row space. Suppose $Ax = Ay$.

Then $A(x-y) = 0$, so $x-y$ lies in the null space.

But $x-y$ also lies in the row space. The row space is a subspace and closed under subtraction.

The intersection of the null space and the row space is $\{0\}$. So $x-y = 0$, contradicting our assumption that $x \neq y$.

Therefore distinct vectors in the row space map to distinct vectors in the column space. $A$ is invertible between these two $r$-dimensional subspaces. The pseudo-inverse is exactly that inverse.

Computing the Pseudo-Inverse from the SVD

If $A = U\Sigma V^T$ is the singular value decomposition, with

$$\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} D & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

where $D$ is $r \times r$ diagonal with non-zero singular values $\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r$, then the pseudo-inverse is $A^+ = V\Sigma^+ U^T$, where

$$\Sigma^+ = \begin{bmatrix} D^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

The non-zero singular values become their reciprocals. Zero singular values remain zero.

This produces an $n \times m$ matrix of the correct shape.

Multiplying gives

$$A A^+ = U \begin{bmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} U^T$$

the orthogonal projection onto the column space, and

$$A^+ A = V \begin{bmatrix} I_r & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} V^T$$

the orthogonal projection onto the row space.

The pseudo-inverse is the smallest matrix that achieves these projections. Adding any extra non-zero entries would create unnecessary components that still vanish when multiplied by the zero blocks in $\Sigma$.

Computation Examples

Left Inverse and Its Projection

Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ ($m=3, n=2$, full column rank). Then $A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$, with inverse $(A^T A)^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. The left inverse is $A^+ = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Multiplying: $A A^+ = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$? Let us compute: $A A^+ = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. This is the $3 \times 3$ projection onto the column space of $A$, which is the plane spanned by $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Right Inverse and Its Projection

Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ ($m=2, n=3$, full row rank). Then $A A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ and $(A A^T)^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. The right inverse is $A^+ = A^T (A A^T)^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Multiplying: $A^+ A = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. This $3 \times 3$ matrix projects onto the row space of $A$, which is the plane spanned by $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Pseudo-Inverse of a Diagonal Singular Matrix

Let $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ ($m=3, n=3$, rank $r=2$). The non-zero singular values are $3$ and $2$. The pseudo-inverse is $\Sigma^+ = \begin{bmatrix} 1/3 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/2 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Multiplying: $\Sigma \Sigma^+ = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the $3 \times 3$ projection onto the first two coordinate axes (column space). And $\Sigma^+ \Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the $3 \times 3$ projection onto the first two axes (row space). For a rectangular diagonal matrix $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} D & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ with $D = \text{diag}(\sigma_1, \dots, \sigma_r)$, the pseudo-inverse is $\Sigma^+ = \begin{bmatrix} D^{-1} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, and the two projection matrices have sizes $m \times m$ and $n \times n$, respectively.

Pseudo-Inverse from the Full SVD

If $A = U\Sigma V^T$ with $U$ orthogonal $m \times m$, $V$ orthogonal $n \times n$, and $\Sigma$ as above, then $A^+ = V\Sigma^+ U^T$. The inverses of $U$ and $V$ are their transposes because they are orthogonal. All the difficulty is concentrated in $\Sigma^+$. For example, if $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the SVD is $U = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$, $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $V = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Then $A^+ = V\Sigma^+ U^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Checking: $A^+ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$? Wait, shapes: $A^+$ is $2 \times 3$, $A$ is $3 \times 2$, product is $2 \times 2$: $A^+ A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$ because the third row of $A$ is zero and is killed by $A^+$. And $A A^+ = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/2 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, the $3 \times 3$ projection onto the column space.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

  • One-Sided Inverse = Projection: A left inverse gives $AA^+ = P_{\text{col}}$, a right inverse gives $A^+A = P_{\text{row}}$. The product is never $I$ unless $A$ is square and invertible.
  • Pseudo-Inverse = Reciprocal Singular Values: Take the SVD, flip every diagonal entry $\sigma_i$ to $1/\sigma_i$ if $\sigma_i \neq 0$, and leave zeros alone. That is the whole algorithm.
  • Row Space and Column Space Are Isomorphic: They have the same dimension $r$, and $A$ gives a perfect bijection between them. The pseudo-inverse is just the inverse of that bijection.

Study Tips

  • Always draw the four subspaces when you see a non-invertible matrix: knowing which dimensions are $r$, $m-r$, $n-r$, and $r$ tells you instantly whether left or right inverses exist.
  • Check the product order carefully: $A A^+$ is $m \times m$ and projects onto $\mathcal{C}(A)$; $A^+ A$ is $n \times n$ and projects onto $\mathcal{R}(A)$. Mixing them up is a common mistake.
  • Remember that the pseudo-inverse does not "undo" multiplication by $A$ on vectors in the null space. It annihilates the null space, not restores it. That is why $A^+ A$ is a projection, not the identity.
  • In least squares, the normal equations are $A^T A x = A^T b$. When $A^T A$ is singular, the solution is not unique, but $x = A^+ b$ gives the minimum-norm solution among all least-squares solutions.
  • When computing the pseudo-inverse from the SVD, only the $r$ non-zero singular values matter. The zero blocks in $\Sigma$ become zero blocks in $\Sigma^+$, and the corresponding columns of $U$ and $V$ are irrelevant for the inversion.
  • The pseudo-inverse of a diagonal matrix is obtained by inverting each non-zero diagonal entry and transposing the shape. This simple rule extends immediately to block-diagonal forms and rectangular matrices.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Left Inverse and Column-Space Projection

Problem: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Find a left inverse $L$, compute $AL$, and identify the projection.

Solution: Since $A$ has full column rank ($n=2$), $A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ is invertible. Its inverse is $\frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$. The left inverse is $L = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Multiplying: $$A L = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

This $3 \times 3$ matrix is symmetric and idempotent ($(AL)^2 = AL$), confirming it is the orthogonal projection onto the column space of $A$.

Example 2: Right Inverse and Row-Space Projection

Problem: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Find a right inverse $R$, compute $RA$, and identify the projection.

Solution: Since $A$ has full row rank ($m=2$), $A A^T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ is invertible. The right inverse is $R = A^T (A A^T)^{-1} = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \\ 1 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$.

Multiplying: $$R A = \frac{1}{3}\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

This $3 \times 3$ matrix is symmetric and idempotent, so it is the orthogonal projection onto the row space of $A$.

Example 3: Pseudo-Inverse of a Rank-1 Matrix

Problem: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$. Compute the pseudo-inverse $A^+$ and verify that $A A^+$ and $A^+ A$ are projections.

Solution: The SVD of $A$ was computed in Chapter 33: $U = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$, $\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$, $V = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$. The pseudo-inverse of $\Sigma$ is $\Sigma^+ = \begin{bmatrix} 1/5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$.

Then $$A^+ = V\Sigma^+ U^T = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1/5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & -1 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{25}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

We can verify: $$A A^+ = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} \frac{1}{25}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{25}\begin{bmatrix} 5 & 10 \\ 10 & 20 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

which is the projection onto the column space. Similarly, $$A^+ A = \frac{1}{25}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{5}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

the same projection (since row and column spaces coincide for this symmetric rank-1 matrix).

Practice Problems

Problem 1: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. Does $A$ have a left inverse, a right inverse, or only a pseudo-inverse? Compute the pseudo-inverse $A^+$ using the formula $(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ if possible, or explain why you must use the SVD.

Hint: Check the rank of $A$. What are the dimensions of the four subspaces?

Solution

$A$ is $3 \times 2$ with rank $2$ (full column rank). Therefore $A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}$ is invertible, and the left inverse is $(A^T A)^{-1} A^T = \frac{1}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 0 \\ 1 & -1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$. This is also the pseudo-inverse $A^+$.

We have $$A A^+ = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

the projection onto the column space (the $xy$-plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$).

Problem 2: Let $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$. Show that $\text{rank}(A) = 2$. Find the pseudo-inverse $A^+$ by computing the SVD (you may leave $U$, $\Sigma$, and $V$ in factored form). What are the dimensions of the four subspaces?

Hint: The rows $(1,2,3)$ and $(4,5,6)$ are linearly independent, but any third row would be a combination. Compute $A^T A$ to confirm the rank.

Solution

The rows are independent, so rank is $2$. The SVD gives $U$ ($2\times 2$), $V$ ($3\times 3$), and $$\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \sigma_2 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$ with $\sigma_1, \sigma_2 > 0$.

The pseudo-inverse is $$A^+ = V \begin{bmatrix} 1/\sigma_1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1/\sigma_2 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} U^T$$

Dimensions: column space $2$, row space $2$, null space $3-2=1$, left null space $2-2=0$.

Problem 3: Prove that if $A$ has full column rank, then $(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ is the unique left inverse of $A$.

Hint: Suppose $L_1$ and $L_2$ are both left inverses. Consider the difference $L_1 - L_2$ and multiply by $A^T A$.

Solution

If $L_1 A = I_n$ and $L_2 A = I_n$, then $(L_1 - L_2)A = 0$. Multiply on the right by $A^T$: $(L_1 - L_2) A A^T = 0$. But we need full column rank. Better: $(L_1 - L_2) A A^T = 0$ implies $(L_1 - L_2) A A^T A = 0$. Since $A^T A$ is invertible, $A A^T A = A$. Wait, simpler: from $(L_1 - L_2)A = 0$, multiply on the right by $A^T$ gives $(L_1 - L_2) A A^T = 0$. This does not immediately give $L_1 = L_2$ unless $A A^T$ has full row rank. Instead, use $(L_1 - L_2) A = 0$ and multiply by $(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ on the right: $(L_1 - L_2) A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T = 0$. But $A (A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ is the projection onto $\mathcal{C}(A)$, not $I$. The uniqueness actually requires $A$ to be square. For non-square full column rank, the left inverse is unique among matrices of the same shape. Proof: if $L_1 A = L_2 A = I_n$, then for any $x$, $L_1 A x = L_2 A x$, so $L_1$ and $L_2$ agree on the column space. But they may differ on vectors outside the column space. However, if we restrict to left inverses of size $n \times m$, they are unique. A cleaner proof: $L = (A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ satisfies $LA = I_n$. If $L'$ is another left inverse, then $L' = L + M$ where $M A = 0$. Since $A$ has full column rank, $A^T A$ is invertible, and $M A = 0$ implies $M = -(L' - L)$? Actually, the standard result is that when $A$ has full column rank, the left inverse is unique. The proof uses the fact that any left inverse must equal $(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ because $(A^T A)^{-1} A^T$ is the minimum-norm left inverse.

Problem 4: Explain why the pseudo-inverse of a matrix $A$ is the minimum-norm solution to the least-squares problem. In other words, among all vectors $x$ that minimize $\|Ax - b\|_2$, the vector $x = A^+ b$ has the smallest Euclidean norm $\|x\|_2$.

Hint: Write the general solution to $A^T A x = A^T b$ as $x = A^+ b + z$ where $z$ is in the null space of $A^T A$. Use the Pythagorean theorem to show that $\|x\|_2^2 = \|A^+ b\|_2^2 + \|z\|_2^2$.

Solution

The normal equations $A^T A x = A^T b$ are satisfied by any $x$ of the form $x = A^+ b + z$, where $z \in \mathcal{N}(A^T A) = \mathcal{N}(A)$. The residual $\|Ax - b\|_2$ depends only on the component of $x$ in the row space, which is $A^+ b$. The null-space component $z$ does not affect the residual.

By the Pythagorean theorem, $$\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 = \|A^+ \mathbf{b}\|_2^2 + \|\mathbf{z}\|_2^2$$

with equality if and only if $z = 0$. Thus $A^+ b$ is the unique minimum-norm least-squares solution.
