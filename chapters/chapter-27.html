Chapter 27: Complex Matrices; Fast Fourier Transform

This chapter makes the leap from real to complex vector spaces, introducing the Fourier matrix as the most important example of a complex matrix with orthonormal columns. The $n \times n$ Fourier matrix $F_n$ is constructed from the $n$-th roots of unity, and its orthogonal columns make the inverse immediate: $F_n^{-1} = \bar{F}_n^T / n$. The climax is the Fast Fourier Transform (FFT), the algorithm that reduces the cost of multiplying by an $n \times n$ matrix from $n^2$ operations to $\frac{1}{2}n \log_2 n$ operations by recursively factoring $F_n$ into smaller Fourier matricesâ€”a reduction by a factor of 200 for $n = 1024$.

Detailed Topic Breakdowns

Roots of Unity and the Primitive Root $W$

The $n$-th roots of unity are the complex numbers $1, W, W^2, \ldots, W^{n-1}$ where $W = e^{2\pi i/n}$. These lie on the unit circle in the complex plane at angles $0, 2\pi/n, 4\pi/n, \ldots, 2\pi(n-1)/n$. The number $W$ is called the primitive $n$-th root of unity because all other roots are powers of $W$. For $n=4$, $W = e^{2\pi i/4} = e^{i\pi/2} = i$, and the four roots are $1, i, -1, -i$. For $n=6$, $W = e^{2\pi i/6} = e^{i\pi/3} = \frac{1}{2} + i\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, and the six roots include $W^6 = 1$. Every power of $W$ has absolute value 1, so all roots lie on the unit circle.

The Fourier Matrix $F_n$

The $n \times n$ Fourier matrix $F_n$ has entries $(F_n)_{jk} = W^{jk}$ where $W = e^{2\pi i/n}$ and the indices $j, k$ start at 0. For $n=4$, the matrix is: $$F_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & i & -1 & -i \\ 1 & -1 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & -i & -1 & i \end{bmatrix}$$ The entry in row $j$, column $k$ is $W^{jk} = i^{jk}$. The pattern is clear: the exponents are the product of the row number and column number. The Fourier matrix is the matrix that computes the discrete Fourier transform: multiplying a vector $x$ by $F_n$ gives its Fourier coefficients.

Orthogonal Columns and the Inverse

The columns of $F_n$ are orthogonal but not orthonormal. The inner product of column $j$ with column $k$ is $\sum_{m=0}^{n-1} W^{mj} \bar{W}^{mk} = \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} W^{m(j-k)}$. When $j \neq k$, this is a geometric series that sums to 0. When $j = k$, each term is $W^0 = 1$, so the sum is $n$. Thus the columns are orthogonal and each has length squared $n$. Dividing by $\sqrt{n}$ gives orthonormal columns. The inverse of $F_n$ is therefore $F_n^{-1} = \frac{1}{n} \bar{F}_n^T$, where $\bar{F}_n$ is the complex conjugate of $F_n$. This is the complex analogue of $Q^{-1} = Q^T$ for real orthogonal matrices.

The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)

The FFT is an algorithm that multiplies by $F_n$ (or $F_n^{-1}$) in $\frac{1}{2}n \log_2 n$ operations instead of $n^2$. The key insight is that $F_{2n}$ can be expressed in terms of two copies of $F_n$ separated by a permutation matrix that selects even and odd indexed components. Specifically, for $n=2m$: $$F_{2n} = \begin{bmatrix} I & D \\ I & -D \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} F_n & 0 \\ 0 & F_n \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} P \\ 0 & I \end{bmatrix}$$ where $P$ is the permutation matrix that reorders $[x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_{2n-1}]$ into $[x_0, x_2, \ldots, x_{2n-2}, x_1, x_3, \ldots, x_{2n-1}]$ and $D$ is a diagonal matrix of powers of $W$. The recursive structure means we solve two problems of size $n$ instead of one problem of size $2n$. The recurrence for the operation count is $M(2n) = 2M(n) + O(n)$, which solves to $M(n) = O(n \log n)$. For $n=1024$, this is $512 \times 10 = 5120$ operations instead of $1,048,576$â€”a factor of 200 reduction.

Computation Examples

Derivation: The Fourier Matrix for $n=4$

For $n=4$, the primitive root is $W = e^{2\pi i/4} = e^{i\pi/2} = i$. The entries of $F_4$ are $W^{jk} = i^{jk}$ for $j, k = 0, 1, 2, 3$:

$$F_4 = \begin{bmatrix} i^{0\cdot0} & i^{0\cdot1} & i^{0\cdot2} & i^{0\cdot3} \\ i^{1\cdot0} & i^{1\cdot1} & i^{1\cdot2} & i^{1\cdot3} \\ i^{2\cdot0} & i^{2\cdot1} & i^{2\cdot2} & i^{2\cdot3} \\ i^{3\cdot0} & i^{3\cdot1} & i^{3\cdot2} & i^{3\cdot3} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & i & -1 & -i \\ 1 & -1 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & -i & -1 & i \end{bmatrix}$$

Each column has length squared $1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2 = 4$. The inner product of column 0 and column 2 is $1\cdot1 + 1\cdot(-1) + 1\cdot1 + 1\cdot(-1) = 0$, confirming orthogonality.

Derivation: Orthogonality of Fourier Matrix Columns

For $F_n$, the inner product of column $j$ and column $k$ is $\langle c_j, c_k \rangle = \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} W^{mj} \bar{W}^{mk} = \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} W^{m(j-k)}$. When $j = k$, this is $\sum_{m=0}^{n-1} 1 = n$. When $j \neq k$, this is a geometric series with ratio $W^{j-k} \neq 1$:

$$\sum_{m=0}^{n-1} (W^{j-k})^m = \frac{1 - (W^{j-k})^n}{1 - W^{j-k}} = \frac{1 - (W^n)^{j-k}}{1 - W^{j-k}} = \frac{1 - 1^{j-k}}{1 - W^{j-k}} = 0$$

Thus $\langle c_j, c_k \rangle = n\delta_{jk}$. The columns are orthogonal with length squared $n$. Dividing by $\sqrt{n}$ gives an orthonormal basis.

Derivation: The FFT Operation Count

Direct multiplication by $F_n$ requires $n^2$ complex multiplications. The FFT recursively factors $F_{2n}$ into two copies of $F_n$ plus $O(n)$ overhead for the permutation and diagonal matrices. Let $M(n)$ be the operation count for size $n$. Then $M(2n) = 2M(n) + O(n)$. Assuming $M(1) = 0$, unfolding the recurrence for $n = 2^k$:

$$M(2^k) = 2M(2^{k-1}) + c \cdot 2^{k-1}$$

Dividing by $2^k$: $M(2^k)/2^k = M(2^{k-1})/2^{k-1} + c/2$. Telescoping gives $M(2^k)/2^k = k \cdot c/2$, so $M(2^k) = \frac{c}{2} \cdot k \cdot 2^k = \frac{c}{2} n \log_2 n$. Thus the FFT computes the transform in $O(n \log n)$ operations.

Learning Strategies

Mnemonic Devices

Mnemonic: "W is the Primitive Root: W^n = 1, All Others Are Powers"

The Fourier matrix is built from $W = e^{2\pi i/n}$. All $n$-th roots of unity are $1, W, W^2, \ldots, W^{n-1}$. The entry in row $j$, column $k$ is $W^{jk}$. Every entry is a power of the same primitive root.

Mnemonic: "Columns Orthogonal, Length Squared Is n"

The columns of $F_n$ are orthogonal but not orthonormal. Each column has length squared $n$, so the inverse is $F_n^{-1} = \frac{1}{n}\bar{F}_n^T$. Divide by $\sqrt{n}$ to get an orthonormal matrix.

Mnemonic: "FFT: n Squared Becomes n log n"

The Fast Fourier Transform reduces matrix multiplication from $n^2$ to $n \log_2 n$ by recursively splitting the problem in half. For $n=1024$: $n^2 \approx 10^6$ becomes $n \log_2 n \approx 512 \times 10 = 5120$.

Study Tips

  • Memorize the pattern: $F_n$ has entries $W^{jk}$ where $W = e^{2\pi i/n}$ and indices start at 0
  • For $n=4$, know that $W = i$ and the matrix entries follow $i^{jk}$
  • To check orthogonality of columns, compute the inner product with conjugation: $\sum W^{mj} \bar{W}^{mk}$
  • The inverse formula $F_n^{-1} = \frac{1}{n}\bar{F}_n^T$ mirrors $Q^{-1} = Q^T$ for real orthogonal matrices
  • The FFT recursion is $F_{2n} \to$ two copies of $F_n$ plus permutation and diagonal fix-up
  • The operation count goes from $n^2$ to $\frac{1}{2}n \log_2 n$â€”memorize this for exam questions

Visual Aids

Figure: The 6th Roots of Unity

The six 6th roots of unity are $W^k$ for $k = 0, 1, \ldots, 5$ where $W = e^{2\pi i/6} = e^{i\pi/3}$:

$$W^0 = 1, \quad W^1 = \tfrac{1}{2} + i\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \quad W^2 = -\tfrac{1}{2} + i\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \quad W^3 = -1, \quad W^4 = -\tfrac{1}{2} - i\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \quad W^5 = \tfrac{1}{2} - i\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$$

All lie on the unit circle at angles $0, \pi/3, 2\pi/3, \pi, 4\pi/3, 5\pi/3$. The primitive root $W = e^{i\pi/3}$ generates all others by taking powers.

Figure: The 4Ã—4 Fourier Matrix

For $n=4$, $W = e^{2\pi i/4} = i$. The Fourier matrix is:

$$F_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & i & -1 & -i \\ 1 & -1 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & -i & -1 & i \end{bmatrix}$$

Each column has length squared $1^2+1^2+1^2+1^2=4$. The columns are orthogonal: $\bar{c}_0^T c_2 = 1-1+1-1 = 0$. The inverse is $F_4^{-1} = \frac{1}{4}\bar{F}_4^T = \frac{1}{4}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -i & -1 & i \\ 1 & -1 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & i & -1 & -i \end{bmatrix}$.

Figure: FFT Recursion Tree

The FFT recursively splits an $n$-point transform into two $n/2$-point transforms:

$$F_{64} \to \begin{bmatrix} F_{32} & 0 \\ 0 & F_{32} \end{bmatrix} + \text{permutation} + \text{diagonal fix-up}$$

$$F_{32} \to \begin{bmatrix} F_{16} & 0 \\ 0 & F_{16} \end{bmatrix} + \cdots$$

The recursion depth is $\log_2 n$. At each level, the cost is $O(n)$ for the fix-up diagonal matrices. Total cost: $O(n \log n)$ instead of $O(n^2)$.

Practice Problems

Problem Set 27

Problem 1: For $n=8$, find the primitive root $W = e^{2\pi i/8}$, list all eight 8th roots of unity, and verify that $W^8 = 1$.

Hint: $W = e^{i\pi/4} = \cos(\pi/4) + i\sin(\pi/4) = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$.

Solution

$W = e^{i\pi/4} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2} + i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. The 8th roots are $W^0=1, W^1, W^2=i, W^3, W^4=-1, W^5, W^6=-i, W^7$. Specifically: $W^0=1$, $W^1=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}+i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, $W^2=i$, $W^3=-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}+i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, $W^4=-1$, $W^5=-\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}-i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$, $W^6=-i$, $W^7=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}-i\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$. All have magnitude 1.

Problem 2: Write down the $4 \times 4$ Fourier matrix $F_4$ explicitly. Verify that $\bar{F}_4^T F_4 = 4I$.

Hint: The entry in row $j$, column $k$ is $i^{jk}$ for $j, k = 0, 1, 2, 3$. Compute the conjugate transpose and multiply.

Solution

$F_4 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & i & -1 & -i \\ 1 & -1 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & -i & -1 & i \end{bmatrix}$. Then $\bar{F}_4^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & -i & -1 & i \\ 1 & -1 & 1 & -1 \\ 1 & i & -1 & -i \end{bmatrix}$. Multiplying: each diagonal entry of $\bar{F}_4^T F_4$ is the inner product of a column with itself, which is 4. Each off-diagonal entry is the inner product of two different columns, which is 0 by orthogonality. Thus $\bar{F}_4^T F_4 = 4I$, so $F_4^{-1} = \frac{1}{4}\bar{F}_4^T$.

Problem 3: Explain why the columns of the Fourier matrix $F_n$ are orthogonal. What is the length of each column? Why does this make $F_n^{-1}$ easy to compute?

Hint: Compute the inner product of column $j$ and column $k$ using the geometric series formula for $\sum_{m=0}^{n-1} W^{m(j-k)}$.

Solution

The inner product of column $j$ and column $k$ is $\sum_{m=0}^{n-1} W^{mj} \bar{W}^{mk} = \sum_{m=0}^{n-1} W^{m(j-k)}$. When $j=k$, this is $n$. When $j \neq k$, this is a geometric series with ratio $W^{j-k} \neq 1$: $\sum_{m=0}^{n-1} (W^{j-k})^m = \frac{1-(W^{j-k})^n}{1-W^{j-k}} = \frac{1-1}{1-W^{j-k}} = 0$. Thus the columns are orthogonal. Each column has length $\sqrt{n}$. Since the columns are orthogonal, $F_n^H F_n = nI$, so $F_n^{-1} = \frac{1}{n}F_n^H = \frac{1}{n}\bar{F}_n^T$. This is exactly analogous to $Q^{-1} = Q^T$ for real orthogonal matrices.

Problem 4: For the FFT, explain why the operation count is $O(n \log n)$ instead of $O(n^2)$. Use the recurrence $M(2n) = 2M(n) + cn$ and solve it for $n = 2^k$.

Hint: Unfold the recurrence: $M(2^k) = 2M(2^{k-1}) + c \cdot 2^{k-1}$. Divide by $2^k$ and telescope.

Solution

$M(2^k) = 2M(2^{k-1}) + c \cdot 2^{k-1}$. Divide by $2^k$: $M(2^k)/2^k = M(2^{k-1})/2^{k-1} + c/2$. Telescoping: $M(2^k)/2^k = k \cdot c/2$. Thus $M(2^k) = \frac{c}{2} k \cdot 2^k = \frac{c}{2} n \log_2 n$. The $n^2$ term from direct multiplication is replaced by $n \log_2 n$. For $n=1024$: $n^2 = 1,048,576$, while $n \log_2 n \approx 10,240$. The FFT is about 100Ã— faster.
